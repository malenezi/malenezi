## Course Description

A one-day, five-hour beginner workshop that turns an AI assistant from something participants have *heard about* into something they *use every day*. The course assumes nothing: no programming, no prior AI exposure, no English fluency. In five hours, each participant holds their first structured conversation with an assistant, drafts real correspondence in Arabic and English, summarises a long document into a one-paragraph brief, plans a working week, extracts action items from meeting notes, uses the assistant to learn and research with verification habits built in from the first hour, and leaves with a personal, printed-or-digital **Murafiq (مرافق) playbook** — their own tested prompts for the ten tasks they actually do at work.

The pedagogical thesis is stated in the first ten minutes and returned to every hour: **an AI assistant is a capable, tireless, occasionally wrong junior colleague — you delegate to it, you never surrender to it.** Every activity therefore pairs a delegation skill (how to ask well) with a control skill (how to check, correct, and stay responsible for the result). Safe use is not a closing slide; PDPL-aware data rules — what may and may never be pasted into a public assistant — are introduced in Hour 1, practised in every activity, and assessed in the playbook.

The course is built around a single golden thread: **"a Tuesday in the life of Sara"**, a programme coordinator at a Saudi public entity. Her realistic working day — an overflowing inbox, a 14-page report she must brief her director on, a bilingual invitation letter, a meeting that needs minutes, and a task she has never done before — is replayed twice: once the manual way (as a timed baseline) and once with Murafiq, her assistant. Participants measure the difference themselves, on their own devices, which is what converts a workshop into a habit.

## Learning Outcomes

By the end of this course, participants will be able to:

1. **LO1** — Explain in plain language what a smart personal assistant is, what it does well, where it fails, and why its answers must be checked
2. **LO2** — Hold an effective conversation with an assistant using the four-part ask (role · task · context · format) and iterate on a draft instead of accepting the first answer
3. **LO3** — Produce everyday written work — emails, letters, summaries, translations between Arabic and English — faster and to a higher standard with the assistant
4. **LO4** — Use the assistant to organise personal work: plans, prioritised to-do lists, meeting minutes, and action-item tracking
5. **LO5** — Use the assistant to learn, research, and explain unfamiliar topics, applying a simple verification routine to every factual claim
6. **LO6** — Apply the personal-data and workplace-data safety rules (aligned with Saudi PDPL) that govern what may be shared with an assistant
7. **LO7** — Assemble and demonstrate a personal Murafiq playbook of tested prompts covering their own ten most frequent tasks

# Module 1 — What a Smart Personal Assistant Is — and Is Not
## ما هو المساعد الشخصي الذكي — وما ليس هو

## Module Overview

The opening module removes both fear and magic. Participants meet the assistant as a concrete thing: a system trained on enormous amounts of text that predicts good continuations of a conversation — which makes it superbly useful for drafting, summarising, translating, planning, and explaining, and structurally unreliable as a source of facts it was never given. The module installs the course's two governing ideas. First, the **junior-colleague contract**: the assistant is a capable, tireless, occasionally wrong junior colleague; you delegate, review, and remain responsible — you never sign what you have not read. Second, the **three data red lines** that no ask may cross. The hour ends with every participant holding their first structured conversation and writing the contract and red lines into page one of their Murafiq playbook.

## Learning Objectives (mapped)

- Describe, in one plain sentence, what an AI assistant does and why it sometimes produces confident errors (**LO1**)
- List five everyday task families where the assistant excels and three situations where it must not be trusted unchecked (**LO1**)
- State the junior-colleague contract and apply it to a worked example (**LO1**, **LO2**)
- Recite the three data red lines and classify eight sample asks as allowed or forbidden (**LO6**)
- Complete a first structured conversation: greet, ask, refine, and save a useful result (**LO2**)

## Technical Content

### 1. The assistant, demystified

One projector demo replaces an hour of theory: the instructor types "اكتب لي بريدًا قصيرًا أعتذر فيه عن حضور اجتماع الغد" and a polished apology appears in three seconds. Then the instructor asks "كم عدد موظفي الجهة التي أعمل بها؟" and the assistant either refuses or guesses — and the guess is wrong, because *it was never given that information*. Those two moments are the whole model: the assistant is brilliant at **shaping language** and blind to **facts outside its reach**. Everything else in the day builds on knowing which of the two kinds of request is being made.

### 2. What it does well — the five task families

The course names five families and returns to them all day: **drafting** (emails, letters, announcements), **condensing** (summaries, briefs, minutes), **transforming** (Arabic ↔ English, formal ↔ friendly, text ↔ table), **organising** (plans, priorities, checklists), and **explaining** (unfamiliar topics, at any chosen depth). Participants tick, on a printed card, which families dominate their own job — that card seeds their capstone playbook.

### 3. Where it fails — and why that is not a scandal

Assistants fabricate ("hallucinate") when asked for facts they do not hold: statistics, names, dates, regulations, citations. The failure is structural, not occasional, so the control must be structural too: **facts get verified; drafts get reviewed; decisions stay human.** The module previews the two-source verification routine that Hour 4 drills.

### 4. The junior-colleague contract

Delegate the way you would to a bright new hire on their first week: give context, state the task, describe the audience and format, review the work, request changes, and sign only what you have read. The contract is printed as the first page of the playbook and every subsequent activity begins by re-reading it aloud.

### 5. The three data red lines (introduced, not deferred)

Never paste into a public assistant: (1) **personal data** of citizens, customers, or colleagues — names tied to IDs, phone numbers, records; (2) **confidential or internal work documents** — anything not already public; (3) **credentials** — passwords, access links, financial details. Aligned with Saudi PDPL and typical entity policy; deepened in Module 5.

### 6. Design principles, best practices, common mistakes

**Best practices:** treat the first answer as a first draft; ask in whichever language you think in; keep one conversation per task so context accumulates. **Common mistakes:** asking one-line questions and blaming the tool for generic answers; pasting forbidden data "just this once"; copying output unread; asking the assistant to make the decision instead of preparing the decision.

### 7. Real-world example walkthrough

Sara's Tuesday, 8:05 a.m.: forty unread emails. The module closes by showing — not yet doing — what the rest of the day will build: the inbox triaged into three folders, the report condensed to one paragraph, the invitation letter drafted bilingually, the meeting notes turned into minutes, all before noon, every output reviewed by Sara before it left her desk.

## Prompt Examples

### The first structured conversation (Arabic)

```text
أنت مساعد إداري خبير. أعمل منسقة برامج في جهة حكومية.
اكتب لي بريدًا إلكترونيًا قصيرًا وباللغة العربية الفصحى المبسطة،
أطلب فيه من زملائي إرسال تقاريرهم الشهرية قبل يوم الخميس،
بنبرة ودية ولكن واضحة، وفي ثلاث جمل كحد أقصى.
```

### The same ask, English, showing the four-part structure

```text
[Role]    You are an experienced administrative assistant.
[Task]    Draft a short email asking my colleagues to submit their monthly reports by Thursday.
[Context] I am a programme coordinator at a government entity; the tone should be friendly but clear.
[Format]  Arabic, three sentences maximum.
```

### The refusal test (used live in class)

```text
ما هو رقم الجوال الخاص بمدير الإدارة في جهتي؟
→ Correct assistant behaviour: it does not know and should say so.
→ Teaching point: any confident answer here would be fabricated. Facts it was never given cannot be retrieved — only invented.
```

## Hands-on Activity 1 — Your First Structured Conversation

**Duration:** 20 minutes · **Mode:** individual, on own device · **Deliverable:** one saved, useful result + playbook page 1

1. Open the approved assistant and greet it in Arabic. Observe that it responds naturally in Arabic.
2. Run the four-part ask above verbatim (Sara's version), then **refine it twice**: "اجعل النبرة أكثر رسمية" then "أضف سطرًا يشكرهم على التزامهم الشهر الماضي".
3. Repeat the pattern once on a real ask from your own work (within the red lines).
4. Write into your playbook: the junior-colleague contract, the three red lines, and your first personal prompt.

**Checkpoint questions:** What changed between your first and third version? Which of the four parts (role/task/context/format) made the biggest difference when you added it?

## Mini Exercises

1. Classify eight sample asks (provided on a card, bilingual) as ✅ allowed or ❌ crosses a red line, and name the line crossed.
2. Rewrite the one-line ask "اكتب تقرير" into a four-part ask for a report you actually owe someone.
3. In one sentence each, explain to a colleague what the assistant is good at and what it must never be trusted with unchecked.

## Case Study — The Confident Wrong Number at "Bushra" (بشرى) Municipality Office

A new employee asked a public assistant for the official fee of a municipal service and pasted the answer into a citizen-facing reply. The figure was fabricated — plausible, precisely formatted, and wrong — and the correction cost far more time than the original lookup would have. The office's fix was not to ban the assistant but to install the rule this module teaches: **language from the assistant, facts from the source.** The assistant now drafts the reply with an explicit placeholder — "الرسوم: [تُؤخذ من الدليل الرسمي]" — and the employee fills the fact from the published tariff. Drafting time stayed short; the error class disappeared.

# Module 2 — Everyday Writing with an Assistant
## الكتابة اليومية بمساعدة الذكاء الاصطناعي

## Module Overview

Writing is where the assistant pays for the whole day. This module drills the four-part ask on the three written artefacts office workers produce most — emails and replies, formal letters, and summaries — and adds the transformation skills that matter most in a bilingual workplace: Arabic ↔ English translation with register control, and tone adjustment (more formal, warmer, shorter) as an *instruction*, not a rewrite. The golden thread advances: Sara clears her inbox, briefs her director on a 14-page report in one paragraph, and produces a bilingual invitation letter — with a measured manual baseline first, so the gain is her own number, not the instructor's claim.

## Learning Objectives (mapped)

- Draft an email, a formal letter, and a reply using the four-part ask, and iterate to final in ≤ 3 turns (**LO2**, **LO3**)
- Condense a multi-page document into a one-paragraph brief and a five-bullet summary, and check the summary against the source (**LO3**)
- Translate a short official text Arabic → English and back with correct register, and spot-check key terms (**LO3**)
- Adjust tone (formal/friendly/urgent) and length by instruction rather than manual rewriting (**LO2**, **LO3**)
- Apply the review pass: names, dates, numbers, claims — checked by the human before anything is sent (**LO1**, **LO6**)

## Technical Content

### 1. The four-part ask, now as a habit

Role, task, context, format — drilled in Module 1, now applied without the scaffold card. The module's refrain: **a vague ask produces a generic draft; a specific ask produces your draft.**

### 2. Drafting: emails, replies, and formal letters

Three patterns, each shown live: the fresh draft ("write me…"), the reply ("here is the email I received — draft a polite reply that says yes to A and defers B"), and the formal letter with required structure (بسملة, addressee block, subject line, body, closing). The letter pattern explicitly instructs the assistant on the entity's letter conventions rather than hoping it guesses them.

### 3. Condensing: the one-paragraph brief

Summarisation is the highest-leverage skill for anyone who reports upward. The pattern taught: state **who the summary is for and what decision it serves** ("لمديري، ليقرر الموافقة من عدمها، في فقرة واحدة") — audience-and-purpose is what separates a useful brief from a shorter copy. Then the control skill: scan the source for the three most important numbers and confirm they appear, unaltered, in the summary.

### 4. Transforming: Arabic ↔ English with register control

Translation asks specify register ("official correspondence English", "عربية فصحى مبسطة") and preserve names and figures exactly. The spot-check habit: key terms are checked in both directions; anything legal, financial, or citizen-facing gets a human bilingual review — the assistant accelerates translation, it does not certify it.

### 5. Tone and length as instructions

"اجعلها أكثر رسمية" · "اختصرها إلى النصف" · "أضف فقرة شكر" — participants learn that editing-by-instruction is usually faster than editing by hand, and that the conversation *remembers*, so refinements stack.

### 6. Design principles, best practices, common mistakes

**Best practices:** paste the email you are replying to (if it crosses no red line) — context transforms reply quality; keep your entity's letter template in your playbook and include it in the ask. **Common mistakes:** sending without the names-dates-numbers pass; translating confidential documents on a public assistant; accepting flowery filler — instruct "بدون مبالغة وبدون حشو".

### 7. Real-world example walkthrough

Sara's 14-page quarterly report becomes: (a) a one-paragraph decision brief for her director, (b) a five-bullet version for the team channel, and (c) an English courtesy summary for a visiting delegation — three audiences, one source, eleven minutes including her review of every number.

## Hands-on Activity 2 — Sara's Correspondence, Then Yours

**Duration:** 30 minutes · **Mode:** pairs (writer + reviewer, then swap) · **Deliverable:** finalised letter + brief; two prompts saved to the playbook

**Checkpoint questions:** What did your reviewer catch that you missed? Which figure would have been most damaging to get wrong?

2. Translate the workshop invitation's detail block into English, then back-translate and list any drift.
3. Turn the five-bullet summary into a two-sentence WhatsApp-style update without losing the numbers.

## Case Study — The Eleven-Minute Brief at "Manar" (منار) Programme Office

A coordinator who routinely spent Sunday mornings condensing weekly reports for three different audiences adopted the audience-and-purpose summary pattern. Output time fell from roughly two hours to under fifteen minutes — but the office's own telling of the story centres on the near-miss that shaped their rule-set: an early summary silently rounded a budget figure. The names-dates-numbers pass caught it before sending, and the pass has been mandatory since. The lesson taught to every new joiner: **the speed is real, and so is the review.**

# Module 3 — Planning, Meetings, and Personal Organisation
## التخطيط والاجتماعات والتنظيم الشخصي

## Module Overview

The assistant as a personal chief of staff. This module applies the same delegation-and-review loop to time and tasks: turning a jumbled brain-dump into a prioritised weekly plan, breaking an intimidating task into a starter checklist, and — the highest-value pattern for anyone who attends meetings — turning raw notes into clean minutes with owners, deadlines, and a follow-up message ready to send. Sara's thread advances through her 11:00 meeting: her scribbled notes become minutes, the minutes become three tracked actions, and the follow-up email writes itself from the same conversation.

## Learning Objectives (mapped)

- Convert an unordered task list into a prioritised day/week plan with time estimates, and adjust it by instruction (**LO4**)
- Decompose a large or unfamiliar task into a first-hour starter checklist (**LO4**)
- Transform raw meeting notes into structured minutes: decisions, action items, owners, deadlines (**LO4**, **LO3**)
- Generate the follow-up message from the minutes in the same conversation (**LO2**, **LO4**)
- Keep organisational asks inside the red lines: no personal data of colleagues beyond first names and roles (**LO6**)

## Technical Content

### 1. The brain-dump plan

Participants paste (or dictate) everything on their plate — unordered, in whatever language it comes — and instruct: prioritise by deadline and importance, estimate durations, fit into working hours, mark what can be delegated or dropped. The control skill: the plan is a *proposal*; the human moves what the assistant cannot know (energy, politics, prayer times, the school run).

### 2. Decomposing the intimidating task

"أحتاج أن أنظم ورشة عمل ولم أفعل ذلك من قبل — قسّمها لي إلى خطوات، وابدأ بما يمكن إنجازه في الساعة الأولى." Decomposition converts procrastination into a first step; the starter-hour framing is deliberate and is the pattern participants report using most after the course.

### 3. Notes → minutes → actions → follow-up: one conversation, four artefacts

The module's centrepiece. Raw notes go in once; then, in the same conversation: "أخرج محضرًا منظمًا" → "استخرج جدول مهام: المهمة، المسؤول، الموعد" → "اكتب رسالة متابعة قصيرة للمكلفين". Because the conversation holds context, each artefact takes one instruction. Review rule: owners and deadlines are facts — confirm each against what was actually agreed before the follow-up is sent.

### 4. Recurring structures: templates you keep

Weekly-plan prompt, minutes prompt, and status-update prompt are written once, personalised, and stored in the playbook — the module makes explicit that **the playbook is the product**: a prompt used weekly is worth an hour of this course every month.

### 5. Voice as an input channel

A brief, deliberate demo: dictating the brain-dump by phone voice input while walking out of a meeting. For many participants voice-in/text-out is the mode that makes the habit stick.

### 6. Design principles, best practices, common mistakes

**Best practices:** one conversation per meeting (context accumulates); first names and roles only for colleagues; date-stamp your minutes ask ("اجتماع يوم الثلاثاء 2026-07-28"). **Common mistakes:** letting the assistant invent owners for actions nobody claimed — instruct "إن لم يُذكر مسؤول، اكتب: يُحدد لاحقًا"; treating the generated plan as binding; pasting a colleague's full personnel details into a scheduling ask.

### 7. Real-world example walkthrough

Sara's 11:00 coordination meeting: nine lines of messy notes become a one-page minute, a three-row action table (two named owners, one "يُحدد لاحقًا" honestly flagged), and a four-sentence follow-up — 7 minutes total, of which 3 are Sara verifying that the deadlines match what was said in the room.

# Module 4 — Asking, Learning, and Verifying
## السؤال والتعلم والتحقق

## Module Overview

The assistant as a patient teacher and research aide — and the module where the course confronts fabrication head-on. Participants use the assistant to explain an unfamiliar topic at chosen depth ("اشرح لي كأني غير متخصص"), to prepare before a meeting on a subject they barely know, and to compare options they must choose between. Then the control skill takes centre stage: a live demonstration of a confident wrong answer, the taxonomy of what assistants fabricate (numbers, names, dates, regulations, citations), and the **two-source verification routine** that participants drill until it is reflex: *facts that will be repeated, sent, or acted on get checked against an authoritative source — the assistant can even suggest where to check, but it cannot be the check.*

## Learning Objectives (mapped)

- Use depth-controlled explanation asks ("as if to a non-specialist", "in three levels of detail") to learn an unfamiliar topic quickly (**LO5**)
- Prepare a briefing sheet — likely questions, key terms, sensible positions — before a meeting on an unfamiliar subject (**LO4**, **LO5**)
- Name the five fabrication-prone categories and explain why fabrication is structural, not a bug being fixed next month (**LO1**, **LO5**)
- Apply the two-source verification routine to every factual claim that will leave their desk (**LO5**, **LO6**)
- Distinguish asks the assistant answers well (concepts, structure, language) from asks that require a source (figures, rules, current events) (**LO1**, **LO5**)

## Technical Content

### 1. Explanation at chosen depth

The teaching superpower: the same topic explained "في ثلاث جمل"، ثم "بتفصيل متوسط مع مثال"، ثم "كأنك تشرح لمختص". Participants learn to ask for the depth they need rather than accepting the default — and to ask follow-up questions freely, because the assistant never tires and never judges.

### 2. The pre-meeting brief

Pattern: "عندي اجتماع غدًا حول [موضوع لا أعرفه جيدًا]. جهّز لي: أهم خمسة مصطلحات بتعريف سطر واحد، وأهم ثلاثة أسئلة يُتوقع أن تُطرح، وما الذي ينبغي ألا أقوله لأنه شائع وخاطئ." Ten minutes of preparation that used to require an hour of unfocused reading.

### 3. The anatomy of fabrication — live

The instructor asks the assistant for a specific statistic with a source. The assistant produces a plausible number and a plausible-looking citation. The class checks: the citation does not exist. This is the day's most important five minutes. The taxonomy on the board: **numbers, names, dates, regulations, citations** — the five categories where confidence and correctness fully disconnect.

### 4. The two-source verification routine

For any fact that will be repeated, sent, or acted on: (1) ask the assistant *where* such a fact is authoritatively published (official portal, published tariff, the entity's own documents); (2) check the fact **there**, not in the chat. Corollary taught explicitly: for facts that live inside your entity (your own fees, your own policies), the authoritative source is internal and the assistant simply cannot know — the placeholder pattern from Module 1's case study is the professional move.

### 5. Comparison and decision preparation

"قارن لي بين الخيارين في جدول: المزايا، والمخاطر، والتكلفة التقريبية، وما الأسئلة التي أطرحها قبل الاختيار" — the assistant structures the comparison; the human supplies judgement and verifies any figure that matters. Decisions stay human, but they arrive better prepared.

### 6. Design principles, best practices, common mistakes

**Best practices:** ask "ما الذي قد يكون خاطئًا في إجابتك؟" — the assistant critiques itself usefully; ask for the *kind* of source rather than trusting a generated citation. **Common mistakes:** citing the assistant as a source in official work; verifying only the facts that look suspicious (the dangerous ones look plausible); asking about very recent events without checking dates — the assistant's knowledge has a horizon.

### 7. Real-world example walkthrough

Sara, 1:00 p.m., is told she will join tomorrow's meeting on a data-classification initiative she knows nothing about. Fifteen minutes: three-level explanation, five key terms, three expected questions, one common misconception — and the two figures she plans to quote verified on the official portal, per the routine. She walks in prepared, and nothing she says came unverified from the chat.

### Depth-controlled learning

```text
1) في ثلاث جمل لغير المتخصص
2) بتفصيل متوسط مع مثال من بيئة عمل حكومية
3) ثم اذكر خطأ شائعًا يقع فيه غير المتخصصين عند الحديث عنه
```

```text

- خمسة مصطلحات أساسية بتعريف من سطر واحد لكل منها
- ثلاثة أسئلة يُرجح أن تُطرح في الاجتماع
```

## Hands-on Activity 4 — Learn It, Brief It, Verify It

**Duration:** 25 minutes · **Mode:** individual, then pair-check · **Deliverable:** a verified pre-meeting brief; the verification checklist saved to the playbook

## Case Study — The Citation That Did Not Exist at "Rakeen" (ركين) Studies Department

Ironically, the assistant remained in the workflow — it is excellent at *finding the vocabulary* with which to search — but the click-through to the real document became mandatory.

# Module 5 — Safe and Responsible Use · The Murafiq Capstone
## الاستخدام الآمن والمسؤول · مشروع «مرافق» الختامي

## Module Overview

The closing module does two jobs. First, it deepens the safety thread from red lines into working judgement: what Saudi PDPL means for an assistant user in practice, how to recognise personal data in its less obvious forms, what entity policy typically permits, and the anonymisation habit that keeps useful asks legal — describing situations by role and pattern instead of by name and number. Second, it converts the day into a durable artefact: each participant assembles their **Murafiq (مرافق) playbook** — the contract, the red lines, the verification checklist, and ten personalised, tested prompts for their own most frequent tasks — and demonstrates one prompt live in a two-minute showcase. The playbook is the assessed deliverable and, more importantly, the thing that makes Monday morning different from last Monday morning.

## Learning Objectives (mapped)

- Explain, at user level, what PDPL protects and why the red lines exist, with two workplace examples each (**LO6**)
- Recognise personal data in non-obvious forms (a case description that identifies by combination; a photo of a filled form) (**LO6**)
- Apply the anonymisation habit: rewrite a forbidden ask into a safe, role-and-pattern ask that gets the same help (**LO6**, **LO2**)
- State their entity's expected posture: approved tools, what needs manager sign-off, where to ask when unsure (**LO6**)
- Assemble, personalise, and demonstrate the Murafiq playbook (**LO7**)

## Technical Content

### 1. PDPL for assistant users, in plain language

Not a legal lecture — three operational sentences: personal data belongs to the person, not to the office that holds it; sending it to an external service is a *transfer* that needs a lawful basis the employee almost never personally holds; therefore the red lines are not etiquette, they are compliance. Two minutes on why "the assistant company probably doesn't store it" is not a defence an employee gets to make.

### 2. Personal data in disguise

The forms that catch people: a "hypothetical" that copies a real case identifiably; small-population combinations (role + department + circumstance can identify a person as surely as a name); photographs and screenshots of documents with names, IDs, or signatures in a corner. The classification drill from Hour 1 returns with these harder cards.

### 3. The anonymisation habit

The professional move that keeps the assistant useful: strip identity, keep structure. "موظف لديه رصيد إجازات كذا واعتُرض على طلبه لسبب كذا — ما الخيارات النظامية المعتادة في مثل هذه الحالة؟" gets the same quality of help as the forbidden version with the name attached — with a follow-up habit: verify anything that sounds like a rule against the actual regulation, per Module 4.

### 4. Workplace posture and living policy

Approved-tool lists, what requires sign-off, and where to ask when unsure differ by entity and change over time; the module teaches the *questions to ask* rather than a fixed answer sheet, and sets the norm that checking is a strength, not an admission.

### 5. The Murafiq playbook: assembly and ownership

Structure (one or two pages, paper or notes app): the junior-colleague contract · the three red lines with the disguise examples · the verification checklist · **ten prompts** — at least one from each module, personalised to the participant's real work, each already tested once today. The build criterion: *would you actually reach for this on Sunday morning?* Anything decorative gets cut.

### 6. Design principles, best practices, common mistakes

**Best practices:** put the playbook where your work happens (notes app, desk drawer, taped inside a notebook); calendar a 15-minute review in one month — prompts you have not used get replaced, not kept. **Common mistakes:** writing prompts for the job you wish you had; keeping Sara's versions instead of personalising; treating the showcase as a performance instead of a rehearsal for Monday.

### 7. Real-world example walkthrough

Sara's Tuesday, 4:00 p.m., replayed as numbers on one slide: the inbox, the brief, the letter, the minutes, the pre-meeting prep — the manual timings against the assisted-and-reviewed timings the room measured itself today, and the one thing that never changed: every fact verified, every draft read, every decision hers.

## Case Study — The Policy That People Actually Follow at "Etmam" (إتمام) Shared Services

The closing lesson of the course: safety that people can follow beats prohibition that they route around.
