    Course Description¶

    A shared on-ramp that builds a common language across AI, machine learning, generative AI, and the data value chain. Participants learn how modern AI systems create value, where they fail, and how data-driven decisions are made in organisations. The module anchors all subsequent pathways and requires no technical background.

    The course is built around a single evolving artefact: the AI Use-Case Evaluation Dossier — a structured case file that every participant builds for one real (or scenario-pack) AI opportunity in their own organisation. Each module contributes one section: Day 1 frames the use case in precise language, sketches how a model would learn it, and maps the data value chain behind it; Day 2 tests it against generative-AI capabilities, scores it on a feasibility–value–risk framework, and aligns it with the National Strategy for Data & AI. By the final hour each participant owns a complete, evidence-backed evaluation of one AI use case — including screenshots of their own hands-on tool exploration and annotated model outputs — and defends it in a three-minute pitch. This is the same evaluation discipline they will apply as sponsors, product owners, and reviewers of AI initiatives throughout the SDAIA pathway.

    Learning Outcomes¶

    By the end of this course, participants will be able to:

    1. LO1 — Explain core AI, ML, and generative-AI concepts and their business applications in plain language
    2. LO2 — Differentiate between rule-based systems, machine learning, deep learning, and generative AI
    3. LO3 — Analyze the data value chain from collection to decision within an organisational context
    4. LO4 — Evaluate AI use cases for feasibility, value, and risk using a structured framework
    5. LO5 — Identify common failure modes, biases, and limitations of AI systems
    6. LO6 — Interpret basic data visualisations and model outputs to support decisions

    Instructor Guidance Notes (Delivery Plan Level)¶

    • Golden thread: every module writes one section of the same AI Use-Case Evaluation Dossier. Never run an activity on a throwaway example when it can run on the participant's own use case — ownership is what converts literacy into behaviour. Participants who cannot bring a workplace use case pick from the five-scenario pack (retail demand, hospital no-shows, citizen-service chatbot, HR CV screening, predictive maintenance) in the first 20 minutes and keep it for both days.
    • Pace control: Labs 2 and 5 are the most overrun-prone. For Lab 2, pre-load the no-code tool with the fallback dataset so nobody burns 15 minutes on uploads; for Lab 5, enforce the 25-minute scoring timebox with a visible timer — debate belongs in the red-team round, not the scoring round.
    • Pairing: rotate pairs each day. Pair a manager with a fresh graduate and a domain expert with a generalist; the translation practice between them is the learning outcome (LO1).
    • Environment strategy: primary = browser-based tools only (no-code AI tool, cloud BI dashboard sample, enterprise AI assistant) — zero installation. Fallback = instructor-projected walkthrough plus printed worksheet versions of every canvas; the course must survive a full network outage because canvases, card sorts, and scorecards are paper-executable.
    • Language: deliver in Arabic, English, or mixed; all dossier templates are bilingual. Encourage participants to write the dossier in the language they would actually use with their own leadership — the pitch must persuade a real audience, not the instructor.
    • Prayer and break scheduling: each "hour" is 50 minutes of instruction + 10 minutes buffer; schedule the long break around Dhuhr. Day 2 afternoon is deliberately activity-heavy to hold energy through the capstone.
    • Assessment logistics: Quiz 1 closes Day 2 Hour 1 (covers M1–M3); Quiz 2 runs inside Day 2 Hour 5 (covers M4–M6). Collect dossiers (photo or file upload) before pitches begin so scoring is against the frozen artefact; the pitch order is drawn at random to keep Hour 5 moving.

    Module 1 — The AI, ML, and Generative-AI Landscape and Terminology¶

    Module Overview¶

    Purpose. Most organisations do not have an AI problem; they have a vocabulary problem. "AI" is used in the same meeting to mean a regex script, a forecasting model, and a chatbot — and decisions made on blurred words are blurred decisions. This module gives participants one precise, plain-language map of the field: what artificial intelligence actually covers, how rule-based systems, machine learning, deep learning, and generative AI differ, and which business jobs each one does well. It is the terminology foundation every later module — and every later meeting — stands on.

    Business relevance. Saudi organisations are under a visible mandate to adopt AI (Vision 2030, NSDAI targets, sector regulators asking about AI plans). The gap between that mandate and delivery is usually a literacy gap in the leadership layer: budgets approved for "AI" that buy rule engines, ML projects rejected because they were judged like software projects, and vendor claims that nobody in the room can interrogate. A shared, precise vocabulary measurably shortens procurement cycles, sharpens vendor evaluations, and prevents the most expensive failure mode of all — solving the wrong problem with the wrong class of system.

    Industry use cases. - A ministry's procurement committee must compare three "AI-powered" document-processing bids — one is template OCR, one is ML-based extraction, one is an LLM pipeline — and price the difference in capability and risk. - A retail bank's innovation team triages 40 staff-submitted "AI ideas"; half are automation requests that need no model at all and can ship in weeks through RPA instead. - A hospital director is offered a "deep learning triage system" and needs to ask the five questions that reveal whether it was trained on a population resembling her patients.

    Expected competencies. After this module a participant can define AI, ML, deep learning, and generative AI in one plain sentence each; classify any product or initiative into rule-based / ML / deep learning / generative AI with justification; translate the twelve highest-frequency AI terms for a non-technical audience; and name the seven business application patterns of AI with one Saudi-context example each.

    Technical Content¶

    1. AI, automation, and analytics: drawing the lines¶

    Three words are used almost interchangeably in business conversation, and separating them is the single highest-value clarification of the course:

    • Automation executes rules a human wrote. A workflow that routes invoices above SAR 50,000 to a second approver is automation. It is deterministic, auditable line-by-line, and never surprises you — because it never learned anything. Robotic process automation (RPA), scripted chatbots with fixed decision trees, and Excel macros all live here.
    • Analytics describes what happened (and sometimes why). Dashboards, monthly reports, and KPI reviews are analytics: humans look at summarised history and make the decision themselves. Analytics informs judgment; it does not replace it.
    • AI produces learned behaviour: the system's responses were not written by a programmer but induced from data. The defining question participants should carry out of this hour: "Did a human write the rule, or did the system learn the pattern?" If a human wrote it, it is automation, however impressive. If the system learned it, you have inherited a new set of powers — and a new set of failure modes (Module 5).

    The boundary matters commercially. Automation projects are estimated like software: scope, build, test, done. AI projects are estimated like experiments: data first, performance uncertain until tried, behaviour that can degrade after launch. Funding an AI project on a software-project mental model is the root cause of a large share of enterprise AI disappointment.

    Instructor note: open the hour with a cold poll — show six one-line product descriptions and ask the room to vote "AI or not" before any teaching. Revisit the same six at the end of Hour 1; the vote shift is the participants' own evidence that the distinction is learnable in fifty minutes.

    2. The AI family tree: rule-based → ML → deep learning → generative AI¶

    The field is best taught as four generations, each subsuming — not replacing — the last:

    Generation How it gets its behaviour Strengths Weaknesses Everyday example
    Rule-based systems Experts write explicit if–then rules Transparent, predictable, cheap to audit Brittle; cannot cover cases nobody anticipated; rules rot as reality changes VAT calculation; visa-eligibility checkers; scripted IVR menus
    Machine learning Algorithm finds patterns in historical examples Handles complexity no rulebook can; improves with data Needs labelled history; inherits data's biases; confidence ≠ correctness Credit scoring; churn prediction; demand forecasting
    Deep learning Many-layered neural networks learn from raw signals (pixels, audio, text) Superhuman at perception tasks; no manual feature design Data- and compute-hungry; hard to explain; fails oddly on unfamiliar inputs Face recognition at eGates; Arabic speech-to-text; medical image triage
    Generative AI Very large deep-learning models trained to produce content Drafts, summarises, translates, converses across open-ended tasks Can state falsehoods fluently ("hallucination"); no native notion of truth AI assistants drafting emails; meeting summarisation; code copilots

    Two framing points prevent common confusion. First, the tree is nested: deep learning is machine learning; generative AI is deep learning. When a vendor says "we use AI," the useful follow-up is "which generation, trained on what?" Second, newer is not better — newer is different. A VAT rule must never be "learned"; it must be written, because it must be exactly right and legally auditable. Choosing the generation that matches the task is the literacy this module teaches; Module 5 turns it into a scored framework.

    3. Twelve terms that carry every AI meeting¶

    Participants receive the bilingual glossary card; the twelve below are drilled until each participant can say them plainly without notes:

    Term Meeting-ready plain sentence
    Model The learned pattern, packaged so it can be applied to new cases — the "graduate" produced by training
    Training Showing the algorithm historical examples until it finds the pattern — the "education phase"
    Inference / prediction Applying the trained model to one new case to get an answer — the "working phase"
    Dataset The collection of historical examples used for training or evaluation
    Label The known correct answer attached to a historical example ("this transaction was fraud")
    Feature One input signal the model considers (amount, time of day, customer tenure)
    Accuracy The share of predictions that were correct — useful headline, dangerously incomplete on rare events (Module 2)
    Confidence score The model's own estimate of how sure it is — an input to a decision, never the decision itself
    Hallucination A generative model producing fluent, confident, false content
    Prompt The instruction you give a generative model — quality in, quality out (Module 4)
    Bias Systematic unfairness the model absorbed from its training data or design (Module 5)
    Drift The world changing after training so the model's picture of it slowly goes stale

    The pedagogical rule: never let a term pass in class without its plain sentence. Participants police the instructor too — the "jargon jar" (a point deducted from the instructor's whiteboard score each time an undefined term slips through) keeps this playful and rigorous.

    4. The seven jobs AI does for a business¶

    Application patterns matter more than technologies: executives fund jobs-to-be-done, not architectures. Every AI use case a participant will ever review is one (or a combination) of:

    1. Predict a number — demand next Ramadan, load on the grid at 14:00, days until a pump fails. (Forecasting)
    2. Predict a category — fraud/not-fraud, churn/stay, malignant/benign. (Classification)
    3. Rank options — which 500 of 50,000 customers to call first; which CVs to read today. (Prioritisation / scoring)
    4. Recommend — next product, next article, next best action for this customer. (Personalisation)
    5. Perceive — read documents (OCR + extraction), recognise defects on a production line, transcribe Arabic calls. (Perception)
    6. Generate — draft correspondence, summarise a 40-page report, translate, brainstorm. (Generative)
    7. Optimise — schedule crews, route deliveries, balance inventory under constraints. (Decision optimisation — often paired with predictions from job 1)

    For each job the instructor elicits one local example from the room before offering one: Saudi grocery chains forecasting Ramadan demand (1), SAMA-supervised banks scoring transactions (2), HR teams ranking applications (3 — with a deliberate foreshadow of the bias case in Module 5), e-commerce recommendation (4), Absher-style face verification at eGates (5), ministry correspondence drafting (6), and airline crew scheduling (7). The dossier's first exercise asks each participant to tag their own use case with its job number — a ten-second act that instantly clarifies fuzzy ideas.

    5. Common misconceptions and how professionals get burned¶

    Close the module by naming the traps directly; each reappears later in the course with its antidote:

    1. "AI is objective because it's mathematics." AI is a mirror of its training data, biases included. A model trained on yesterday's decisions automates yesterday's prejudices at scale (Module 5).
    2. "More AI is more progress." A rules engine that ships in three weeks often beats an ML project that ships in a year — and half of proposed "AI" use cases are automation in disguise. Right tool, not fanciest tool.
    3. "The demo is the product." Vendor demos run on curated data. The literacy question is: "show me performance on data like ours, measured how, by whom?"
    4. "It said so with high confidence, so it's right." Confidence scores are calibrated to training data, not to truth; generative systems in particular are fluent regardless of correctness.
    5. "We'll do AI when our data is perfect." No organisation's data is perfect; the realistic question is whether data is good enough for this use case — which is exactly what the Module 5 scorecard asks.
    6. "AI replaces people." In nearly all near-term enterprise use, AI replaces tasks within jobs and shifts humans toward exception-handling and judgment; workforce design is a Module 6 topic and an NSDAI theme.

    Production consideration for a non-technical audience: the professional's job is rarely to build AI — it is to commission, question, and consume it responsibly. Every hour of this course sharpens one of those three verbs.

    Worked Example 1 — The "AI or Not?" classification worksheet (filled)¶

    The classification test applied to six real product descriptions. Participants complete six more in Lab 1.

    # Product description Human-written rules or learned pattern? Classification One-line justification

    Worked Example 2 — The jargon translation table in action¶

    A real paragraph of vendor prose, translated line by line. Left column: as written. Right column: what a literate professional hears.

    Worked Example 4 — The five vendor questions (wallet-card checklist)¶

    The module's terminology, weaponised for the most common professional encounter with AI — the vendor meeting. Participants receive it as a wallet card and rehearse it in the translation drill:

    # Question What a good answer contains What a weak answer sounds like
    1 "Which generation is this — rules, ML, deep learning, or generative — and what was it trained on?" A direct placement + a description of the training data's origin and scale "It's proprietary AI" (a placement refusal is itself an answer)
    2 "Show me performance on data like ours — measured how, on what base rate?" Held-out evaluation, metric named, base rate stated A single accuracy number, demo-only evidence
    3 "What happens when it's wrong — and how would we know?" Error types priced, monitoring described, alert ownership "It's very accurate" (answering a different question)
    4 "What data does it need from us, and is that permitted?" (PDPL reflex) A field list + processing location + lawful-basis awareness "Just connect us to your systems"
    5 "Who has deployed this in our sector, and may we speak to them?" A referenceable deployment at comparable scale Logos without contactable references

    The card's footer states the meta-rule: a vendor who answers all five plainly is a partner; a vendor who cannot answer question 1 has told you everything. The card returns in Module 5, where questions 2–4 become evidence lines on the FVR scorecard.

    Hands-on Lab 1 — AI Landscape Safari¶
    Objective Classify 12 real products by AI generation, run a first structured session with an AI assistant, and complete Dossier §1 for the participant's own use case
    Duration 50 minutes
    Setup Card deck (12 product cards, bilingual), AI assistant open in browser, Dossier workbook (printed + digital), scenario pack for participants without a workplace use case

    Instructions & tasks

    1. (10 min) Card sort. In pairs, sort the 12 product cards onto the four-generation floor mat (rule-based / ML / deep learning / generative AI + the "analytics — not AI" side zone). Cards include: ATM cash-dispensing rules, Ramadan demand forecast, Arabic call-centre transcription, résumé-ranking tool, spam filter, fixed-menu IVR, meeting summariser, credit-limit rulebook, defect-detection camera, product recommender, monthly KPI dashboard, correspondence-drafting assistant.
    2. (5 min) Defend one card. Each pair must defend its most contested placement to a neighbouring pair using the "who wrote the rule?" test.
    3. (15 min) First assistant session. Individually, open the AI assistant and run the three-prompt sequence from the worksheet: (a) "Explain the difference between machine learning and rule-based systems using an example from [participant's industry]"; (b) "Now explain it to a 10-year-old"; (c) "What did you oversimplify in the second explanation?" Capture screenshots — this is the first tool-exploration evidence for the dossier (capstone requirement).
    4. (15 min) Dossier §1. Complete the Use-Case Framing Canvas for your own use case (or select from the scenario pack). The technology-word ban in box 1 is enforced by your partner, who initials the canvas.
    5. (5 min) Gallery walk. Canvases go on the wall; participants place one sticky-note question on someone else's canvas — the question must use a glossary term correctly.

    Expected output 

    Card sort: ≥ 10/12 cards correctly placed after the defence round
Assistant session: 3 screenshots captured; one oversimplification identified in the model's own answer
Dossier §1: canvas complete, box 1 technology-free (partner-initialled), job number + generation tagged
Wall: one glossary-correct peer question per participant

    Troubleshooting guidance

    Symptom Cause Fix
    Pair deadlocks on the résumé-ranking card It is genuinely ML and a Module 5 bias trap Accept ML placement; park the fairness question visibly for Module 5
    Participant's "use case" is a technology ("we want GenAI") Solution-first thinking Ask "what would you use it for?" three times until a problem appears; that is box 1
    Assistant answer is too technical to judge Prompt (a) missing the industry anchor Re-run with the industry named; teach that specificity is free performance (Module 4 preview)
    Participant has no workplace use case and freezes Fresh graduate / rotating staff Scenario pack — assign the closest sector; conviction matters more than authenticity

    Instructor notes. The safari's job is confidence: every participant leaves Hour 2 having used an AI system deliberately and classified twelve real ones. Walk the room during task 3 and collect two or three assistant answers worth projecting — one good, one oversimplified — for the debrief. Fast finishers: classify their own organisation's three most recent "digital" initiatives on the same mat.

    Mini Exercises¶

    Quiz (5 questions) 1. A system routes citizen complaints using keywords chosen by a policy team. AI or automation? → Automation — humans wrote the rules. 2. Complete: deep learning is a kind of __, which is a kind of ____. → machine learning; artificial intelligence. 3. Give the one-sentence plain definition of "model." → The learned pattern, packaged so it can be applied to new cases. 4.     Module 2 — How Machine Learning Works: Data, Models, Predictions¶

    Module Overview¶

    Purpose. Professionals do not need to build models, but they must understand the machine they are commissioning: what training actually does, what a model actually is, what a prediction actually means, and — above all — how to read a model's outputs without being misled by them. This module opens the black box exactly wide enough for sound judgment: the data → training → model → prediction loop, the difference between learning types, and the plain-language mathematics of "how good is good?"

    Business relevance. The most expensive misreadings of AI in organisations are output misreadings: a 95%-accurate model deployed on a 2%-frequency event (useless), a precision-heavy model used where recall mattered (fraud slips through), a confident score treated as a fact (loan wrongly denied, patient wrongly deprioritised). Every one of these failures happens after the technical work is done, in the decision layer — which is staffed by this course's audience. Reading model outputs correctly is a decision-quality skill, not an engineering skill, and regulators of Saudi banking and health increasingly expect decision-makers to evidence it.

    Industry use cases. - A telecom retention manager receives a weekly churn-risk list and must decide how far down the ranking to spend the retention budget — a precision/recall trade-off she makes whether she knows the words or not. - A hospital operations head pilots a no-show predictor and must set the threshold that balances wasted reminder calls against empty clinic slots. - A procurement committee must interrogate a vendor's "99.2% accurate" defect-detection claim for a Jubail plant where defects occur in 1 of 400 units.

    Expected competencies. After this module a participant can narrate the ML loop end-to-end in plain language; distinguish supervised, unsupervised, and reinforcement learning by the kind of data each consumes; read a confusion matrix and explain precision vs recall with a business example; explain why accuracy misleads on rare events; and recognise overfitting and drift as stories, not equations.

    Technical Content¶

    1. The loop: data → training → model → prediction¶

    Machine learning is one loop, and every ML system a participant will ever meet is an instance of it:

    1. Data. Historical examples, each with the inputs that were known at the time (features) and — for supervised learning — the outcome that eventually happened (label). For the hospital no-show case: appointment records (specialty, lead time, patient age band, prior no-shows, weekday) each labelled showed / did not show.
    2. Training. An algorithm searches for the pattern that best connects features to labels across thousands of examples. Nothing mystical happens: it is systematic trial-and-error at machine speed, scored against the known answers.
    3. Model. The found pattern, frozen and packaged. It is a thing — a file — that can be copied, versioned, tested, and (importantly for governance) audited and retired.
    4. Prediction. The model applied to one new case produces an output — typically a score (0.87) — plus nothing else. It does not know whether it is right. Someone, or some rule, must convert that score into an action.

    Two plain-language consequences carry the whole course. First, a model can only learn patterns present in its data — no examples of Ramadan demand means no Ramadan pattern; historical hiring biases in, historical hiring biases out. Second, the model's world stops at training day: reality keeps moving (new fraud tactics, new customer behaviour, a pandemic), so a model is milk, not honey — it expires. That expiry is drift, and mature organisations monitor for it (a forward pointer participants will meet again in later modules and their engineering colleagues live with daily).

    2. Three ways machines learn¶

    Learning type Data it needs Question it answers Business examples
    Supervised Historical examples with known outcomes (labels) "Given what we knew, what will happen?" Churn, fraud, no-shows, demand forecasts, CV screening
    Unsupervised Examples without labels "What natural groups or oddities exist here?" Customer segmentation, anomaly detection in transactions, grouping similar complaints
    Reinforcement An environment with feedback (reward/penalty) "What sequence of actions works best?" Dynamic pricing, energy-grid balancing, game-playing systems

    Supervised learning dominates enterprise AI (roughly the first two of every three use cases participants will evaluate), and its appetite for labels is the hidden cost centre: someone must have recorded the outcome, consistently, for a long time. "Do we have labels?" is question one of the Module 5 feasibility check — and the most common honest answer, "sort of, in three systems, inconsistently," is where AI budgets actually go.

    3. How good is good? Reading model quality like a professional¶

    The single most valuable table of the course. A hospital no-show model evaluated on 1,000 appointments it never saw during training:

    Predicted: no-show Predicted: will show
    Actually no-show (180) 135 caught (true positives) 45 missed (false negatives)
    Actually showed (820) 90 falsely flagged (false positives) 730 correctly cleared (true negatives)

    From this one grid, every headline number a vendor will ever quote:

    • Accuracy = (135 + 730) / 1000 = 86.5% — "how often right overall."
    • Precision = 135 / (135 + 90) = 60% — "when it flags a no-show, how often is it right?" Low precision = wasted reminder calls and annoyed patients.
    • Recall = 135 / 180 = 75% — "of the real no-shows, how many did it catch?" Low recall = empty clinic slots continue.

    The accuracy trap, taught as a story: a model that predicts "will show" for everyone achieves 82% accuracy on this data (820/1000) while catching zero no-shows. On rare events — fraud at 0.5%, serious defects at 0.25% — "do nothing" models score in the high 90s. Whenever the event is rare, accuracy is marketing; precision and recall are management. Participants practise the reflex question: "What is the base rate, and what are precision and recall at the threshold you propose?"

    Thresholds are business decisions. The model outputs a score; someone chooses the cut-off that turns scores into actions. Lowering the threshold catches more no-shows (recall ↑) at the cost of more false alarms (precision ↓). That trade-off belongs to whoever owns the costs — reminder-call budget vs empty-slot cost — which is precisely why this audience, not the data team alone, must understand it. This is the module's bridge to LO4: an "AI decision" is usually a human policy wrapped around a model score.

    4. Why models fail: three stories every professional should recognise¶

    • Overfitting — the student who memorised the textbook. The model learns the training examples too specifically (including their noise) and fails on new cases. Symptom: dazzling performance in the pilot report, disappointment in production. Literate question: "what was performance on data the model never saw?" (the holdout — if the vendor cannot answer, the meeting is over).
    • Data leakage — the exam with the answers printed on it. A feature accidentally contains the outcome (e.g., "reminder call made" as a feature for predicting no-shows, when calls were only made to likely no-shows). Symptom: performance too good to be true. Literate question: "was every feature genuinely knowable before the outcome?"
    • Drift — the world moved on. Customer behaviour, fraud tactics, or referral patterns change; the model's frozen picture decays quietly. Symptom: performance that erodes month by month with no code change. Literate question: "how is performance monitored after launch, and who is alerted when it slips?"

    Each story maps to a governance behaviour, not an engineering one: demand holdout evidence, interrogate feature timing, require post-launch monitoring in every AI proposal (all three become scored items on the Module 5 risk side).

    5. What no-code tools reveal — and what they hide¶

    Lab 2 uses a browser-based no-code tool (Teachable-Machine-class for perception, or a no-code AutoML table trainer) deliberately: watching a model train on your own examples in four minutes demystifies the loop better than any slide. But the debrief must also name what the toy hides: real projects spend most of their effort before training (collecting, cleaning, labelling — Module 3's value chain) and after it (integration, monitoring, retraining). The no-code demo is the visible 20% of an iceberg, and executives who have only seen demos systematically underestimate the other 80%. The module closes by connecting the toy to the profession: the same loop, the same confusion matrix, the same threshold decision — at organisational scale and consequence.

    6. Common mistakes and decision-layer considerations¶

    1. Treating the score as a verdict. 0.87 is an input to a policy, not a decision. The policy (threshold + exception path + human review) is where accountability lives.
    2. Quoting accuracy on rare events. The signature of an unexamined claim; retrain the reflex to ask for the base rate.
    3. Judging the model without a baseline. 75% recall means little until compared with the current process ("coordinators guess no-shows at ~40% recall today"). Always ask: better than what we do now, by how much, at what cost?
    4. Confusing the pilot's data with production's data. A model piloted on Riyadh clinics may not transfer to Jazan clinics; population match is a literate question, not a technical nicety.
    5. One-time evaluation. Model quality is a time series, not a certificate. Approval processes that end at launch institutionalise drift.
    6. Skipping the "so what" arithmetic. 90 false positives per 1,000 appointments = 90 unnecessary calls/day at scale; whether that is trivial or ruinous is a business calculation only this audience can do.

    Worked Example 2 — Confusion-matrix reading protocol (the four questions)¶

    A laminated card participants keep. For any classification model claim:

    1. What is the base rate? How common is the event actually? (180/1,000 = 18% here; fraud might be 0.5%.)
    2. Show me the grid. Insist on all four cells, not one headline number.
    3. Price the two mistakes. False positive costs what? False negative costs what? In SAR, minutes, or trust.
    4. Who chose the threshold, and on what trade-off? If nobody chose it, the tool's default is silently making policy.

    Applied live to the vendor claim from Module 1 ("99.2% accurate defect detection" at 1-in-400 defect rate): a do-nothing model scores 99.75% — the vendor's number is worse than doing nothing until precision/recall are on the table. The room audibly enjoys this arithmetic; let them do it themselves.

1. The score is 0.78 — a rank on a scale, NOT "78% certain."
2. The band [HIGH] is a threshold someone set at 0.65 — who, and

    Case Study — The Churn Model That Wasted the Retention Budget at Tawasul Telecom¶

    Scenario. Tawasul Telecom (regional operator, 6M subscribers) deploys a churn-prediction model. The data-science team reports "89% accuracy"; marketing receives a weekly list of the 20,000 highest-risk subscribers and spends the full retention budget (discount offers averaging SAR 90 each) on them.

    Business context. After two quarters, churn is unchanged and the CFO wants the programme cancelled. A post-review finds two compounding misreadings — neither of them a modelling error.

    Technical challenge. First, the list was ranked by raw score but the threshold was never costed: at the operating point marketing used, precision was 31% — 7 of 10 discounts went to subscribers who were never leaving (SAR ~1.2M/month in giveaways to loyal customers, some of whom learned to expect discounts). Second, recall at that threshold was 22% — the model caught barely a fifth of true churners, but the "89% accuracy" headline (on a 6% churn base rate) had convinced leadership the problem was solved, so no complementary programme ran.

    Constraints. The model itself was adequate; retraining is not the fix. Marketing owns the budget but not the model; data science owns the model but not the offer economics; nobody owned the threshold. The CFO's cancellation review is in three weeks.

    Solution approach (facilitate, don't lecture). Participants, in the marketing director's chair, apply the four-question protocol: base rate (6%), full grid (demanded, finally produced), both mistakes priced (wasted discount SAR 90 vs lost subscriber lifetime value SAR 2,400), threshold re-derived from those costs (a much smaller, higher-precision list plus a cheaper bulk touch — an SMS — for the mid-band). The saved programme is presented to the CFO not as "the model works" but as "the decision policy now matches the economics"; a monthly grid review with named threshold ownership is instituted.

    Discussion questions. 1. Whose failure was this — data science's, marketing's, or a missing role between them? What is that role called in your organisation? (Bridge to Module 6.) 2. Reconstruct the CFO memo in four sentences using only plain-language terms from this module. 3. The mid-band SMS idea changes the action, not the model. What does that suggest about where AI value actually gets decided? 4. What would you monitor monthly to detect drift in this system, and who should see it?

    Module 3 — The Data Value Chain and Data-Driven Decision-Making¶

    Module Overview¶

    Purpose. Models are the visible tip of a long chain: data is collected somewhere, by someone, for some original purpose; it is stored, moved, cleaned, combined, summarised — and only then does it reach a dashboard or a model, and only after that does a human decide anything. Every link can silently corrupt the decision at the end. This module teaches participants to see, map, and interrogate that chain in their own organisation, and to practise the last mile — reading dashboards and turning evidence into decisions — as a discipline rather than a glance.

    Business relevance. "Data-driven" fails in two characteristic ways in organisations: garbage travels up the chain and gets laundered into confident-looking charts, or good data arrives and decision-makers misread, cherry-pick, or ignore it. Both failure modes are governance-and-literacy problems in exactly this audience's hands. In the Saudi context the stakes are codified: the Personal Data Protection Law (PDPL) regulates what may be collected and why, and NDMO data-management standards make data quality and stewardship formal organisational obligations — the chain this module maps is the chain regulators now audit.

    Industry use cases. - A hospital's bed-occupancy dashboard shows 84% while ward staff swear they are full — a discharge-entry lag three links upstream is making an executive number fictional. - A retailer's "top-selling product" ranking silently excludes cash sales from two legacy stores, steering purchasing decisions with a biased denominator. - A ministry KPI review discovers two departments define "case resolved" differently, so the flagship metric compares apples to dates.

    Expected competencies. After this module a participant can map the six links of the data value chain for a real process; identify the weakest link and its decision impact; apply the five-question dashboard interrogation protocol; distinguish correlation from causation in a business argument; write a one-paragraph decision memo that cites evidence honestly (including its limits); and name where PDPL/NDMO obligations attach to their chain.

    Technical Content¶

    1. The six links, walked slowly¶

    The chain is taught on one running example (hospital bed management) and immediately re-applied to each participant's dossier case:

    1. Collection. Data is born: a nurse discharges a patient in the ward system, a POS terminal logs a sale, a sensor reads a temperature. The literacy question at this link: who records it, when, with what incentive to be accurate? (A discharge entered "when the shift calms down" is data born late; a sales rep's forecast entered to hit a quota is data born biased.)
    2. Storage. Data lands in systems — often several, often disagreeing. The question: which system is the source of truth, and does everyone agree? Duplicate customer records and mismatched IDs live here.
    3. Processing. Data is cleaned, joined, transformed, aggregated. Invisible decisions accumulate: excluded rows, filled-in blanks, currency conversions, timezone handling. The question: what was removed, filled, or assumed on the way — and is it documented?
    4. Analysis. Dashboards, reports, models (Module 2 lives at this link). The question: does the metric definition match what the decision needs? ("Resolved cases" counted at first reply vs at confirmed fix are different businesses.)
    5. Decision. A human (or a policy) chooses. The question: did the decision actually use the evidence, or decorate a decision already made?
    6. Action & feedback. The decision changes the world — and, crucially, changes future data. Reminder calls change no-show rates; the next model trains on the changed world. Mature organisations close the loop deliberately; immature ones are confused by it.

    The central teaching image: decisions inherit the weakest link. An impeccable dashboard on top of a lagging collection process is a beautiful picture of a fiction. Participants learn to state chain quality the way engineers state system quality — by its weakest component, not its prettiest.

    2. Data quality in six words: complete, correct, current, consistent, relevant, permitted¶

    Six tests, one per finger and thumb-plus:

    Test Question Bed-management failure example
    Complete Are all the cases there? Two wards paper-based; their beds invisible
    Correct Are values true? Transfers double-counted as discharges
    Current Fresh enough for this decision? Discharges entered 4 hours late — fatal for hourly bed decisions, harmless for monthly stats
    Consistent Same definitions everywhere? "Occupied" includes reserved beds in one ward system, excludes them in another
    Relevant Does it measure what the decision needs? Occupancy % doesn't show bottleneck specialty
    Permitted May we collect/use it for this purpose? Patient-level dashboards viewed by non-clinical staff — a PDPL question, not a technical one

    The sixth test carries the compliance content lightly but firmly: under PDPL, personal data has a lawful purpose at collection, and re-purposing it (say, marketing on clinical data) is a legal event, not a lunchtime decision; NDMO standards oblige entities to know their data (cataloguing), own it (stewardship), and quality-manage it. Participants do not need the statute text — they need the reflex: "Are we permitted, and who is the data steward I should ask?" (SDA-FND-105 takes governance further; this module plants the question.)

    3. Reading dashboards like an examiner: the five-question protocol¶

    Dashboards are rhetorical objects: someone chose these metrics, these colours, these scales. The protocol turns passive viewing into examination:

    1. What question is this dashboard answering — and is that my question? (A revenue dashboard cannot answer a margin question, however beautiful.)
    2. Where does each number come from? (Which links of the chain, how fresh, which exclusions? If nobody can say, the number is decoration.)
    3. What is the comparison? A number without a baseline, target, or trend is a fact without meaning. 84% occupancy — versus what plan, what season, what peer?
    4. What would change my mind? Decide before looking harder what evidence would reverse your current instinct — the discipline that defeats cherry-picking.
    5. What is not shown? Absent metrics, truncated axes, cherry-picked date ranges, averages hiding distributions (the average wait of 20 minutes that hides a 3-hour tail).

    Add the two classic distortions every professional must spot on sight: truncated y-axes that turn a 2% wiggle into a cliff, and correlation dressed as causation ("stores with more staff have higher sales — hire more staff!" — or do busy stores get more staff?). The causation test in plain language: could a third factor drive both? did the cause precede the effect? what happens when we intervene? — and the honest answer that only a controlled change (pilot, A/B test) settles it.

    4. The last mile: from evidence to decision memo¶

    Data-driven decision-making fails most often at the handover from analysis to authority. The module installs a lightweight artefact — the decision memo — as the professional's standard closing move:

    • One paragraph, four sentences: (1) the decision taken; (2) the key evidence, with source and freshness; (3) the main uncertainty or data limit, stated plainly; (4) the review point ("revisit when X or by date Y").
    • Writing the limit sentence is the cultural innovation: it makes honest uncertainty normal paperwork instead of weakness, and it creates the audit trail regulators and post-mortems wish existed.
    • The memo also disciplines the meeting before it: if the dashboard cannot fill sentence 2, the decision is being made on instinct — which is sometimes fine, but should be said.

    5. Common breakdowns and organisational considerations¶

    1. The laundering effect. Bad data acquires false authority as it moves up the chain — by the time it is a boardroom chart, nobody present can see link 1. Antidote: chain maps attached to key dashboards ("this number is born where?").
    2. Metric drift. Definitions change (a system migration redefines "active customer") and trend lines silently break. Antidote: definition change-log owned by a named steward.
    3. Dashboard sprawl. 40 dashboards, no decisions. Every dashboard should name its decision and its owner or be retired.
    4. The HiPPO override. Highest-paid person's opinion beats the data with no record that it did. The decision memo makes overrides visible and legitimate — leaders may override evidence, but on the record.
    5. Feedback blindness. Actions change the data (link 6); teams read the change as organic. Classic: reminder calls cut no-shows, and the next quarter's "no-show model performance drop" is actually the programme working.
    6. Permission debt. Data collected for purpose A quietly powering purpose B until an audit or a complaint surfaces it — the PDPL failure mode that turns a data project into a legal event.

    Worked Example 4 — Correlation on trial (worked mini-case)¶

    The causation test applied end-to-end to a claim participants will recognise:

    The claim (as presented to a leadership meeting): "Branches that completed the new customer-service training have 14% higher satisfaction scores. Roll the training out everywhere — the data proves it works."

    The three-question cross-examination: 1. Could a third factor drive both? Yes, plausibly: training was piloted in volunteer branches — branch managers who volunteer for training are likely the same managers who drive satisfaction by other means. Enthusiasm is the confound. 2. Did the cause precede the effect? Partially checkable: pull the satisfaction trend before the training. In the worked data (provided), the volunteer branches were already 9 points ahead pre-training — most of the gap predates the "cause." 3. What happens when we intervene? The only decisive evidence: assign the next training wave to a random half of the remaining branches and compare after one quarter. In the worked continuation, the randomised effect is +4% — real, worth rolling out, and one-third of the claimed size.

    The literate conclusion, written as the meeting's decision memo: "Decision: proceed with a randomised second wave, not a full rollout. Evidence: observed +14% is confounded by volunteer selection; pre-trend explains ~9 points. Limit: true effect unknown until the randomised comparison reads out. Review: end of next quarter." The lesson participants quote back for the rest of the course: the data was accurate; the claim was not — and the fix cost one sentence of humility plus one quarter of patience.

    Technical Content¶

    1. What an LLM actually does — and what follows from it¶

    A large language model is a deep-learning system trained on enormous quantities of text to do one thing extraordinarily well: predict what text plausibly comes next. Every capability participants see — answering, summarising, translating, drafting — is that one skill, steered. Three plain-language consequences follow directly, and together they explain nearly every GenAI behaviour a professional will meet:

    • Fluency is guaranteed; truth is not. The model produces what plausible text looks like. Most of the time, plausible and true coincide — that is what makes the failure mode dangerous. When they diverge, the model states falsehoods with the same polished confidence, because confidence is a property of its prose, not of its knowledge. This is hallucination, and it is not a bug being patched away next release: it is the shadow of the design. (Modern systems reduce it with retrieval and citations — participants should prefer tools that show sources — but reduction is not elimination.)
    • It has a training cut-off and no native access to your facts. Yesterday's org chart, this morning's policy change, your customer's file — absent unless provided in the prompt or connected by the organisation's own systems. The literate reflex: "does the model actually have what it needs to answer this, or will it improvise?"
    • It does not remember or 'know' you — but your prompt goes somewhere. What participants type may be processed outside the organisation depending on the tool and contract. The governance question ("which tool tier is this data allowed in?") is settled before typing, not after.

    The single most useful mental model for this audience: an eloquent, endlessly willing junior colleague with encyclopaedic reading, no accountability, and no shame. You would happily let that colleague draft; you would never let them sign.

    2. Generative vs predictive: two different contracts¶

    Predictive ML (Modules 2–3) Generative AI
    Output A score or category about a defined question Open-ended content (text, images, code)
    Evaluated by Confusion matrix, precision/recall, against labels Human judgment; usefulness; factual verification
    Wrong looks like A measurable error rate you can monitor A fluent falsehood you must catch

    The table earns its place in the dossier: participants whose use case is really a language task (summarise complaints, draft responses) discover in this hour that they may need GenAI, not a trained predictive model — or vice versa. Both routes flow into the same Module 5 scorecard, but the risk profiles differ and the scorecard's risk column knows it.

    3. Prompting as professional communication¶

    Prompt quality is not magic; it is the same skill as briefing a junior colleague well. The course pattern — Role, Task, Context, Format (RTCF) — with an upgrade shown live:

    • Weak: "Write about our customer service."
    • Strong: "You are an operations analyst at a Saudi utilities company (role). Draft a one-page summary of the attached 30 complaint excerpts for the head of customer care (task). Context: the audience has 5 minutes and cares about systemic causes, not individual cases; Ramadan operating hours changed on the 10th and may explain some patterns. Format: three themes with counts, one 'exceptions and anomalies' section listing anything that fits no theme, and three recommended next checks — not recommendations, checks."

    Four teaching points ride on the example: specificity is free performance; the exceptions section defends against the model's tendency to smooth anomalies into tidy themes; asking for "next checks, not recommendations" keeps the human in the decision seat; and iteration is normal — the second prompt ("now challenge your own summary: what would a sceptic say is missing?") is often worth more than the first. Participants also meet the critique pattern (paste your own draft, ask for weaknesses), which sidesteps hallucination risk almost entirely because the facts are supplied, not generated.

    4. The task-fit matrix: delegate, draft, verify, never¶

    The module's decision tool. Two questions position any task: How costly is an undetected error? and How easily can I verify the output?

    Easy to verify Hard to verify
    Low error cost DELEGATE — meeting-note first pass, reformatting, tone adjustment, internal brainstorms DRAFT — idea generation, outline drafts; treat output as raw material
    High error cost VERIFY — client/citizen-facing text, policy summaries, translations for signature: use freely, verify line-by-line before release NEVER (unassisted) — legal positions, regulatory citations, numbers you cannot check, personal-data judgments; use only with expert review and source-grounded tools

    Plus the overriding data gate applied before the matrix: no personal data, no confidential business data, no client identifiers into tools not approved for them — under PDPL, pasting citizen data into a public assistant is a data transfer, with everything that implies. The organisational tiering (public tools / enterprise tools / no tools) is presented as the pattern participants should expect and demand from their employers; SDA-FND-105 covers the governance side in depth.

    5. Verification as a workflow, not a virtue¶

    "Always verify" fails as advice because it is unbounded. The course installs a bounded, teachable pass — the three-check verification:

    1. Facts: every checkable claim — names, numbers, dates, citations, quotes — gets checked against a source outside the model. In source-grounded tools, follow the citation; a citation that does not support the sentence is treated as a miss.
    2. Omissions: what did it leave out? (Re-prompt: "list anything in the source you did not include and why." The complaint-summary anomaly problem is caught here.)
    3. Fit: would I sign this? Tone, audience, implications — the uniquely human check.

    Time-boxed honestly: the pass costs 20–30% of the drafting time saved — the productivity gain survives, and the hallucination risk largely does not. The cultural rule that makes it stick, stated as policy language participants can take home: "AI drafts, humans sign — and the signer verifies." Accountability never transfers to the tool.

    6. Common mistakes and workplace considerations¶

    1. The copy-paste career risk. Sending unverified AI text under your own name; the signature rule exists because the first hallucinated citation that reaches a regulator or citizen is remembered for years.
    2. Confidential data in public tools. The most common real-world violation; the data gate comes before every other consideration, and "it was faster" is not a defence under PDPL.
    3. Prompt laziness. One vague prompt, disappointing output, "the tool is useless." The RTCF pattern plus one iteration resolves the majority of quality complaints.
    4. Anchoring. The first draft frames all subsequent thinking — including wrong framings. Antidote for consequential work: sketch your own position first, then invoke the assistant.
    5. Uniform trust or uniform distrust. Both are literacy failures; the matrix exists to make trust task-shaped.
    6. Skill atrophy and the training pipeline. If juniors delegate all first drafts, where do seniors come from? Teams need deliberate practice policies — an organisational design question this audience owns (Module 6 picks up the workforce thread).
    7. Believing the model's self-reports. Asking "are you sure?" produces confident text about confidence — verification happens outside the model, full stop.

    Case Study — The Citation That Never Existed: GenAI at the Diwan Correspondence Unit¶

    Scenario. A ministry's correspondence unit (14 officers, ~500 citizen letters/week) pilots an AI assistant for first drafts. Productivity jumps: average drafting time falls from 25 to 6 minutes, backlog clears, officer satisfaction rises. In week seven, a citizen's lawyer replies to a ministry letter pointing out that the regulation article it cites does not exist.

    Business context. The letter carried an officer's signature and the ministry's letterhead. The incident escalates to the deputy minister; the easy institutional reflex — ban the tool — would also surrender a 4× productivity gain the unit now depends on and push usage underground (two officers admit to using personal accounts "before the pilot anyway").

    Technical challenge. Reconstruct how the fabricated citation survived to signature, and design the workflow that keeps the productivity while making the failure class structurally unlikely.

    Constraints. Officers are not lawyers; legal review of all 500 letters/week is impossible; citizen personal data is involved (PDPL applies to what may enter prompts — the pilot tool's tier must be verified); the deputy minister wants an answer in ten days, not a study.

    Solution approach (facilitate, don't lecture). The post-incident review maps cleanly onto the module: the officer trusted fluency (no fact-check on the citation — check 1 skipped); the unit had no matrix (citizen-facing legal-adjacent text was implicitly DELEGATE when it is VERIFY-at-minimum); and the tool was not source-grounded (citations generated, not retrieved). The redesign participants should converge on: (1) tier the letters — routine acknowledgements stay VERIFY-light, anything citing regulation requires the officer to open the cited article (a 90-second check) with a checkbox in the workflow; (2) procure/configure a source-grounded tool that links citations to the actual regulation database, converting check 1 from research into a click; (3) the signature rule enters the unit's standing procedure verbatim — "AI drafts, humans sign, the signer verifies"; (4) the two shadow-users are folded into the sanctioned pilot (amnesty + training beats prohibition); (5) a one-page incident memo (Module 3 format) goes to the deputy minister: decision, evidence, limit, review point. Net result in the scenario: drafting time settles at 9 minutes — the verification pass costs 3 — and the failure class has not recurred at scale because it now requires two independent misses.

    Discussion questions. 1. Why was "ban the tool" the wrong fix even from a pure-risk perspective? (Shadow usage, and the counterfactual of manual-drafting error rates nobody ever measured.) 2. The officer signed it. The tool generated it. The unit head approved the pilot. Distribute the accountability — and defend your distribution. 3. What changes when the tool becomes source-grounded? Which of the three checks does it absorb, and which does it never absorb? 4. Draft the two-sentence note to all officers announcing the new rule without shaming the officer involved. 5. PDPL angle: what had to be true about the pilot tool for citizen names to lawfully appear in prompts at all?

    Module 5 — AI Opportunity and Risk Assessment Frameworks¶

    Module Overview¶

    Purpose. Everything so far — vocabulary, the ML loop, the data chain, GenAI task-fit — converges here into the course's central professional act: evaluating an AI use case before money and reputation are committed. This module delivers a structured, repeatable framework (the Feasibility–Value–Risk scorecard), trains participants to apply it honestly against evidence rather than enthusiasm, and equips them with a working knowledge of how AI systems fail — bias, drift, brittleness, misuse — so that risk scoring is informed, not ritual.

    Technical Content¶

    1. Why structured evaluation beats enthusiasm¶

    Unstructured AI decisions follow predictable pathologies: the demo effect (a polished vendor demo substitutes for evidence about your data), the CEO's-favourite effect (seniority substitutes for feasibility), the pilot trap (a pilot succeeds on curated data and scale-up dies on the real chain), and risk asymmetry (upside is presented, downside is discovered). A structured scorecard defeats these not because the numbers are precise — they are judgments — but because it forces every use case to answer the same nine questions in front of the same audience. Comparability is the product; the score is just the receipt. Two rules make it honest: every score cites its evidence (a Module 3 chain map, a labelled-data check, a baseline measurement — not adjectives), and the scorer is not the sponsor for any use case above a spending threshold.

    2. The Feasibility–Value–Risk (FVR) scorecard¶

    Nine items, three per column, each scored 1–5 against written anchors. The course's canonical instrument, used in Lab 5 and the capstone:

    Feasibility — can we actually build and run this? - F1 Data readiness. Do the data and labels exist, at quality, accessibly, permitted for this use? (Scored from the Dossier §3 chain map — 1 = data doesn't exist or isn't permitted; 3 = exists but needs significant collection/cleaning work; 5 = exists, quality-tested, permitted, owned.) - F2 Task clarity. Is the prediction/generation task crisply defined with a measurable outcome? (1 = "make us smarter"; 5 = Dossier §1 box 5 quality: named metric, named baseline.) - F3 Integration & operability. Can the output reach the decision point, and can someone own the system after launch? (1 = no path from score to action; 5 = decision workflow identified, owner named, monitoring plausible.)

    Value — is it worth it? - V1 Financial impact. Quantified benefit vs baseline (cost saved, revenue gained, loss avoided), in SAR/year, with the arithmetic shown. (1 = unquantified adjectives; 5 = costed against the measured baseline from Dossier §2 box 6.) - V2 Strategic fit. Alignment with organisational strategy and — where relevant — NSDAI priorities (Module 6). (1 = orphan idea; 5 = advances a named strategic objective with an executive sponsor.) - V3 Scale & reuse. Does the value repeat and grow (many decisions, many units), or is it one-off? (1 = single team, single event; 5 = organisation-wide decision made thousands of times.)

    Risk — what can go wrong, to whom? (higher score = LOWER risk) - R1 Error consequence. What does a wrong output do to a person, the business, or the public — and is there a human checkpoint before harm? (1 = automated harm to individuals with no review; 5 = errors cheap and self-correcting.) - R2 Bias & fairness exposure. Could performance differ across groups (nationality, gender, region, age)? Do the training data encode past unfairness? (1 = high-stakes decisions about people with known-skewed history; 5 = no people-affecting decisions.) - R3 Compliance & reputation. Personal data (PDPL), sector regulation, explainability requirements, public sensitivity. (1 = personal data + regulated decision + no explainability plan; 5 = no personal data, no regulated surface.)

    Reading the totals: column sums (max 15 each), never a single grand total — a use case scoring F14/V13/R4 is not "31/45, decent"; it is a valuable, buildable thing that should not be built as designed. The decision grammar taught: strong F and V with weak R → reshape (add human review, narrow scope, stage the rollout) and re-score; strong V with weak F → fix-first (usually data work — often the honest first project); weak V → decline politely, whatever the demo looked like. Scores of 1 or 2 on R1 or R3 are gates, not weights: no value total overrides them.

    3. The eight failure modes (the risk column's field guide)¶

    1. Data bias → decision bias. The Lab 2 hard-hat model at organisational scale: a hiring screener trained on a decade of skewed hiring replicates the skew with mechanical consistency. Bias enters through data composition, label meaning ("good employee" = whoever thrived under old conditions), and deployment mismatch (trained on one population, applied to another).
    2. Drift. The Module 2 expiry story: behaviour, fraud tactics, and demand patterns move; frozen models decay silently. Risk question: who monitors, how often, alerted how?
    3. Brittleness at the edges. Models excel on typical cases and fail strangely on rare ones — which in citizen-facing services are exactly the cases that matter (unusual names, rare circumstances, edge eligibility).
    4. Feedback loops. The system's own actions reshape its future data (Module 3 link 6): a policing-style allocation model over-samples where it over-predicted; a recommender narrows what it learns from.
    5. Hallucination & automation of plausibility. Module 4's failure mode entering documents, decisions, and citizen communications at scale.
    6. Goodhart's law. When the model's metric becomes the target, the metric detaches from the goal: optimising "calls closed per hour" produces fast, bad calls.
    7. Misuse & scope creep. A model built for one purpose quietly applied to another (attendance data → performance scoring), voiding both its validity and its PDPL basis.
    8. Accountability vacuum. "The algorithm decided" — the failure mode that contains all others: no named owner for the threshold, the monitoring, the appeal path. Antidote: every deployed model has a human owner whose name is written down; every automated decision affecting a person has an explanation and an appeal route.

    Participants practise attachment, not memorisation: given a use case, name the two or three failure modes with the strongest claim on it, and let those drive the R-column scoring and the mitigation design.

    4. Mitigations that change scores (reshaping, not decorating)¶

    The framework is generative, not just evaluative: a poor risk score plus the right mitigation is often a fundable redesign.

    • Human-in-the-loop: the model recommends, a human decides — mandatory where R1 ≤ 2 and often converting it to 4. Cost: throughput; the design question is which cases route to humans (low-confidence bands, high-stakes categories — the Module 2 threshold logic reappearing as governance).
    • Scope narrowing: launch on the sub-population or decision slice where data is strongest and errors are cheapest; expand with evidence.
    • Staged rollout with a shadow phase: run the model alongside the current process, compare before it touches anyone — the organisational version of the holdout principle.
    • Explainability & appeal by design: reasons attached to outputs; an appeal path with a human on the end. Not decoration — in citizen-facing use, the licence to operate.
    • Monitoring with named ownership: the drift antidote written into the proposal (what metric, what cadence, who is paged).

    The rule participants internalise: a mitigation that does not change who does what, on which cases, with what evidence — is a paragraph, not a mitigation. Re-score only for the former.

    5. Common mistakes and portfolio considerations¶

    1. Scoring the demo, not the chain. F1 comes from the Dossier §3 map, not the vendor's slide. If the chain map has three "?" marks, F1 cannot be a 4.
    2. Value by adjective. "Significant efficiency gains" scores V1 = 1 until arithmetic appears. The baseline requirement (Module 2, box 6) exists precisely for this row.
    3. Risk theatre. Listing generic risks ("data privacy is important to us") without attaching failure modes to this use case's specifics.
    4. Averaging away a gate. The single most dangerous spreadsheet habit: a 14/13/4 use case hidden inside "31/45". Columns are reported separately, gates are absolute.
    5. One-time scoring. Scores are revisited at each stage gate — after the data fix, after the shadow phase — or the framework becomes an approval ritual.
    6. Portfolio blindness. Individual scoring is necessary but not sufficient: a healthy portfolio mixes quick automation wins (Module 1's reclassified items), one or two high-F ML builds, and a GenAI productivity layer — not five moonshots. The Al-Manar case (Module 1) supplies the running portfolio for this discussion.

    Worked Example 4 — The verdict letters (fix-first and decline, model texts)¶

    Because the scorecard's verdicts must eventually be delivered to people, the course supplies two model letters. Both are four sentences; both cite the scorecard; neither apologises for the framework.

    The fix-first letter (to the CEO's war-room sponsor, Bunyan case):

    "The churn war-room scored strongly on ambition and weakly on foundations: we have never systematically recorded why customers leave (F1 = 2), and the current retention process has no measured baseline to beat (V1 = 2 — the arithmetic cannot be done yet). On that evidence, the war-room re-enters scoring next cycle with F and V it can actually earn. This is not a 'no' — it is the version of 'yes' that survives contact with our data."

    The decline letter (to a submitter whose idea scored V1 = 1, F1 = 2):

    "Thank you for submitting the meeting-transcription analytics idea — the scoring sheet is attached so you can see exactly how the committee read it. It declined on value: no decision we could identify changes based on the output (V1 = 1), and the audio data is neither centrally stored nor cleared for this use (F1 = 2, permission unresolved). If a concrete decision-owner emerges who would act on this weekly, resubmit with that name and the permission answer — those two lines alone would re-open the case. The committee funds evidence, and this letter tells you precisely which evidence."

    The teaching point rides in the last sentence of each: a framework-literate organisation tells people how to succeed next time — which is why the Bunyan campaign gets better submissions every year, and why declined submitters keep submitting.

    Hands-on Lab 5 — Score Your Use Case, Survive the Red Team¶
    Objective Score the participant's own use case on the FVR scorecard with evidence from Dossier §§1–4, attach failure modes and owned mitigations, and defend the recommendation in a structured peer red-team round
    Duration 50 minutes (Day 2 Hour 4, followed by the M6 express session)
    Setup FVR scorecard sheets with printed scoring anchors; failure-mode field guide card; risk-register strip; red-team question cards; participants' dossiers §§1–4 open on the table

    Instructions & tasks

    1. (20 min) Score, with receipts. Complete the nine ratings using the anchors. Rule enforced by the sheet layout: the evidence line must be filled before the score box — and the evidence must point at a dossier page (§1 box 5 for F2, §3 chain map for F1, §2 box 6 for V1…). Unquantified V1 is capped at 2; chain maps with ≥ 3 "?" cap F1 at 3.
    2. (5 min) Attach failure modes. Pick the 2–3 with the strongest claim on your case from the field guide; write one line each on how it shows up here.
    3. (5 min) Design one real mitigation. Who does what, on which cases, with what evidence — then re-score the one rating it changes. Decoration doesn't re-score.
    4. (15 min) Red-team rounds (2 × 7 min). Pairs swap dossiers. The red-teamer draws three question cards ("What is the baseline number, exactly?", "Which chain link breaks this?", "Who is harmed by a false positive, and what is their appeal path?", "What would make you decline your own idea?"…) and attacks; the owner defends or amends scores live. Amended scores are marked in a second colour — visible honesty is the deliverable.
    5. (5 min) Commit. Write the recommendation line and the two-sentence sponsor justification. This freezes Dossier §5 for the capstone.

    Expected output 

    Scorecard: 9/9 rated with evidence lines pointing at dossier pages; column totals + gate check done
Failure modes: 2–3 attached with case-specific one-liners; ≥ 1 mitigation with a named owner and a re-scored rating
Red team: ≥ 1 score amended in second colour per participant (cohorts average 2.4 amendments — this is the honesty metric)
Recommendation: committed, with two-sentence sponsor justification

    Symptom Cause Fix

    Participant gates their own case on R and deflates Reading gates as verdicts Teach the reshape move explicitly: what human checkpoint converts R1 from 2 to 4? Re-score and watch the case revive

    Red team turns hostile or personal Question cards abandoned Cards are the protocol: attacks address the card question, owners answer the question, timekeeper enforces
    "My use case is GenAI — does FVR even apply?" Perceived predictive bias in the card Yes: F1 becomes source-data/tool-tier readiness, R risks lean on modes 5 and 7; the §4 matrix placement is the evidence

    Instructor notes. The amendment count is your live measure of whether the module worked — a room with zero amended scores is a room that performed the scorecard rather than used it; say this out loud at the start of the red-team round. Keep the Al-Manar 6-item ML portfolio on the wall: fast finishers score a second use case from it and experience how much faster the second scoring goes (the framework becoming reflex).     Mini Exercises¶

    Quiz (5 questions) 1. Why are column totals never merged into one grand total? → A gate-level risk hides inside a healthy-looking sum; columns answer different questions. 2. A use case scores F13 V12 R5. What is the move? → Reshape: design mitigations (human-in-the-loop, scope, staging) that change specific R ratings, then re-score — not average, not decline by reflex. 3. Name the failure mode: "the call-centre model's 'resolution rate' target produced fast, bad calls." → Goodhart's law. 4. What does a mitigation need before it can change a score? → Changed behaviour: who does what, on which cases, with what evidence — plus an owner. 5. Which dossier page is the evidence for F1, and why? → §3 chain map — data readiness is a property of the chain, not of enthusiasm.

    Gate hunt. Five one-line use cases read aloud; participants raise a red card the moment they hear a probable R1/R3 gate and name it (citizen eligibility automation, minor-targeted marketing, cross-purpose attendance data…).

    Failure-mode bingo. Each participant gets a card with 8 modes; instructor reads six news-style AI incident vignettes; first correct full attachment wins. (Vignettes provided in the media pack, anonymised.)

    The decline note. Write the three-sentence decline of a colleague's beloved low-V use case — honest, specific, and relationship-preserving. Peers score for all three properties.

    Discussion questions. - Who in your organisation could run this scorecard credibly today — and who would need to accept its verdicts for it to matter?     Case Study — Forty Ideas, One Committee: the Bunyan Bank Portfolio Triage¶

    Scenario. Bunyan Bank (Jeddah, retail and SME banking) runs an internal "AI ideas" campaign, netting 40 submissions — from "predict SME loan default earlier" to "GenAI assistant for relationship managers" to "AI to write our board reports." An innovation committee (head of digital, head of risk, two business heads, one data-science lead — three of five non-technical) has one month and budget for four initiatives.

    Business context. The CEO's favourite — a customer-churn "AI war room" with a vendor demo everyone loved — is submission #7. SAMA-supervised model governance applies to anything touching credit decisions. Last year's single AI project (a chatbot) quietly died after launch for lack of an owner; the committee knows it and the room knows they know it.

    Technical challenge. Triage 40 ideas fairly, fast, and defensibly — including telling the CEO that #7 scores badly.

    Constraints. One month; three of five committee members non-technical (the point of the exercise: the scorecard is their instrument); data-science lead available 20%; any credit-adjacent model triggers governance review (R3 anchor); the campaign promised every submitter a written response.

    Solution approach (facilitate, don't lecture). Round one: all 40 scored on F and V only (30 minutes/idea is too slow — the committee uses a rapid two-anchor screen, F1 and V1, killing 19 ideas at "no data / no number" with respectful fix-first letters, many of them routed to automation/BI teams per the Module 1 classification). Round two: full FVR on the surviving 21. Outcomes participants should reproduce from the provided pack: the SME early-default idea scores F12 V13 R6 → reshape (human credit-officer checkpoint, shadow phase, explainability plan for SAMA-style review) → funded first; the RM GenAI assistant scores high with a VERIFY-workflow condition (Module 4's matrix written into the funding letter); the CEO's war room scores F7 (no labelled churn history — the bank never systematically recorded why customers left) V8 (baseline never measured) → fix-first, presented to the CEO as "fund the data foundation now, the war room in two quarters on real rails"; the board-report writer gates on R3 (confidential financials into an unapproved tool tier) pending enterprise tooling. Four funded, sixteen fix-first or rerouted, twenty declined with reasons — every submitter answered in scorecard language.

    Discussion questions. 1. Rehearse the CEO conversation about #7: two minutes, scorecard in hand, career intact. What makes "fix-first" land differently from "no"? 2. The rapid two-anchor screen risks false negatives (good ideas killed at round one). What cheap safeguard fixes that? (Appeal path; resubmission with evidence.) 3. Why did the committee's non-technical majority turn out to be a strength rather than a weakness — and what did the scorecard have to provide for that to be true? 4. The chatbot that died for lack of an owner: which scorecard item now blocks that history from repeating, and is an item enough? 5. Draft the one-paragraph written response to a declined submitter (V1 = 1, F1 = 2) that keeps them submitting next year.

    2. The institutional map: who does what nationally¶

    Kept deliberately compact — four institutions, one sentence each, and the reflex of knowing whom a question belongs to:

    • SDAIA (Saudi Data & AI Authority): the national steward of the data-and-AI agenda — strategy, national platforms, regulation of data and AI, and the analytics and AI capabilities serving government.
    • NDMO (National Data Management Office): the standard-setter for how public-sector data is managed, classified, quality-controlled, and shared — the institutional home of Module 3's discipline.
    • PDPL (Personal Data Protection Law): the Kingdom's personal-data statute — the legal floor under every "permitted?" question this course has raised, from chain mapping to prompt hygiene.
    • The National Centre for AI and sector regulators: applied AI capability and sector-specific supervision (e.g., SAMA's model-governance expectations in banking) — the reason Module 5's R3 anchor asks about sector regulation, not just PDPL.

    The module does not turn professionals into lawyers; it installs the routing table. A use case touching citizen data routes its "may we?" to the data steward and PDPL basis; a credit model routes its explainability question to the sector regulator's expectations; a data-sharing idea routes to NDMO-aligned classification. Knowing the address is the literacy; SDA-FND-105 (unlocked by this module's badge) teaches the law and governance in depth.

    5. Common mistakes and organisational considerations¶

    1. Strategy tourism. Quoting NSDAI in slide decks without the implication column — alignment claimed, never operationalised. Antidote: V2 scoring discipline; name the pillar and the changed behaviour.
    2. Hiring the famous role first. Five data scientists, no data steward, no translator: expensive people cleaning data they don't own for problems nobody framed. Diagnose gaps with the six-role map before the first requisition.
    3. Literacy as a one-off event. A single course (even this one) decays without practice structures — communities of practice, dossier reviews at idea stage, the scorecard in the funding workflow. The organisational ask participants take home: institutionalise the artefacts, not the memory.
    4. Treating national alignment as government-only. Private-sector participants sometimes bench this module; the correction: PDPL binds them, NDMO patterns reach them through partners and procurement, sector programmes fund them, and the talent market is shaped by the workforce pillar either way.
    5. The unworn hat. Small teams merging roles is fine; the audit question is whether every question in the role table has a name that answers it — for the participant's dossier use case, literally, in writing (Dossier §6 asks).
    6. Pathway drift. Choosing the next module by fashion ("everyone says Python") rather than role fit; the pathway plan's two-year role line exists to anchor the choice.

    Case Study — Six Hires, No Team: Building AI Capability at Kahraba Utilities¶

    Scenario. Kahraba Utilities (regional power distribution, 4,500 staff) commits to an AI programme after a successful predictive-maintenance vendor pilot on substation transformers. Leadership approves six technical hires. Twelve months later: five data scientists and one ML engineer are on payroll, three proofs-of-concept are "95% done," nothing is in production, the maintenance department has quietly stopped attending project meetings, and the board asks where the value went.

    Business context. The Kingdom's utility sector sits squarely in NSDAI's priority-adoption lane; the board's original enthusiasm was strategy-aligned and sincere. The new hires are genuinely skilled. The failure is structural, and its diagnosis is this module's role map.

    Technical challenge. Diagnose the capability gap without blaming the people hired, and re-sequence the programme: which roles, which order, which development-versus-hire decisions.

    Constraints. No additional headcount this year — gaps must close by assignment and development; the maintenance department's trust must be rebuilt (they experienced the PoCs as "data people asking us to label failures for free"); transformer telemetry includes no personal data, but workforce-scheduling data (a proposed second use case) does — a PDPL boundary the programme crossed casually in a workshop, which legal flagged; the board wants a credible plan in 30 days.

    Solution approach (facilitate, don't lecture). The role-map diagnosis participants should produce: business owner — nominally the CTO, actually nobody who owned maintenance outcomes (the maintenance director was never made sponsor); translator — GAP, the central finding: nobody converted "transformer health" into a framed problem with a baseline, a threshold owner, and an integration path into the existing work-order system, so five data scientists optimised models against a question no one had costed; data steward — GAP: telemetry ownership split across OT and IT, quality unknown, the §3 chain never mapped; governance reviewer — assigned late, reflex-blocked the scheduling use case instead of reshaping it (the PDPL flag becomes a routing lesson, not a stop sign). The 30-day plan the case builds toward: make the maintenance director the sponsor with written risk acceptance; develop two senior maintenance engineers as translators (this exact course as step one — the strategy's workforce pillar applied to itself); formalise telemetry stewardship; freeze two of three PoCs (FVR fix-first verdicts on the evidence in the pack) and drive the strongest one through a shadow phase into the work-order system; re-score the scheduling use case with a human checkpoint and a proper PDPL basis before any retry. The closing observation the room should reach unprompted: the SAR-expensive part of the programme was healthy — the missing parts were the two roles that cannot be hired, only grown.

    Discussion questions. 1. Rank the six roles by how much their absence explains the twelve lost months. Defend the top two. 2. Why did the maintenance department disengage, and which artefacts from this course (canvas, chain map, threshold sheet) would have kept them at the table? 3. The governance reviewer's reflex-block on the scheduling use case: right instinct, wrong move? Redesign it as a reshape using Module 5's levers. 4. Map Kahraba's recovery plan to NSDAI pillars with the evidence rule enforced — which rows can it honestly fill today? 5. Draft the board's one-paragraph summary in decision-memo form: decision, evidence, limit, review point.
