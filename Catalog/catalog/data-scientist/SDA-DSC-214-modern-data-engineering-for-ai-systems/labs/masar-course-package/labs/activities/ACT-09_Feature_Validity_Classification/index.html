    ACT-09 — Feature Validity Classification¶

    Could this feature have been known at prediction time?¶

    Field Value
    Activity ID ACT-09
    Module M8 — Serving Gold to AI and BI
    Day / Hour Day 5 · Hour 4 (immediately after CS-08, before the capstone review)
    Duration 25 minutes
    Format Card-sort tribunal — every column of gold.eta_features classified against one question
    Group size 3–4 per team
    Deliverable All 13 columns of gold.eta_features placed in one of four categories, each with a written rationale; plus a reasoned position on the two genuinely arguable features
    Status Client-requested and mandatory. Do not cut this activity for time

    1. Objective¶

    Participants acquire one question and learn to apply it without exception to every feature they will ever build:

    "Could this feature have been known at prediction time?"¶

    That single question resolves most feature-correctness disputes immediately. The activity teaches four things in order of difficulty:

    1. The question itself, and that it is about a timestamp, not about a table.
    2. That "invalid" is not one thing — a feature can fail in three distinct ways, each with a different fix.
    3. That a column's verdict can depend on its provenance, not on its name — the same column name can be valid or leaking depending on where the value came from.
    4. That two of the thirteen are genuinely arguable, and that the professional response to ambiguity is not to win the argument but to remove the ambiguity by construction.

    2. The four categories¶

    Post these large on the wall. Participants must be able to distinguish them by minute 5.

    Category Definition Fix
    VALID Knowable at feature_ts, computed from data strictly at or before it, and available identically offline and online none
    STALE Knowable at prediction time in principle, but the served value is older than the model was trained to expect. The value is not from the future — it is from too far in the past Increase publish frequency; add a freshness SLA and monitor publish lag
    LEAKAGE Computed using information that did not exist at feature_ts. Inflates offline metrics and cannot be reproduced live Re-bound the window to close strictly before the prediction instant
    UNAVAILABLE ONLINE Correct and non-leaking offline, but cannot be produced at inference within the latency budget, or is computed by a different code path online Materialise it: publish the offline value to the online store; never re-implement

    The distinction that matters most and is most often missed: LEAKAGE and UNAVAILABLE ONLINE both produce "great offline, mediocre live" and they have completely different remedies. A team that diagnoses one when the other is present will ship a fix that changes nothing.

    3. Materials (printable)¶

    • Card deck (§8): 13 column cards, one per gold.eta_features column, printed A5 and cut. One deck per team.
    • Category mats: four A4 sheets per team, headed VALID / STALE / LEAKAGE / UNAVAILABLE ONLINE.
    • The scenario card (§8) — the prediction moment. One per team, read aloud first.
    • Verdict sheet (§7), one per team.

    4. Setup (3 minutes)¶

    Post the four category definitions. Lay four mats and a shuffled deck on each table. Put the scenario card face-up at the head of each table — it must be visible throughout, because every ruling refers back to it.

    5. Run of show¶

    Min What happens Facilitator
    0–3 Frame. Read the scenario card aloud, slowly. Write 08:12:00 on the board and circle it. "That is the moment. Every card is judged against that moment and nothing else." Post the one question next to it. Do not add any other criterion.
    3–5 Categories. Walk the four definitions. Stress that LEAKAGE and UNAVAILABLE ONLINE have the same symptom and different cures. 90 seconds. Do not over-explain; the sort teaches it.
    5–15 Sort. Teams place all 13 cards and write a rationale on each. Circulate with exactly one question, repeated: "Could that have been known at 08:12?" Add nothing. Where a team is stuck on provenance, say: "Read the card again. It tells you where the value comes from."
    15–19 Gallery walk. Each team moves one table right and marks, in a second colour, any card it would move. Silent. The cards that move: rolling_zone_demand_15m, historical_route_duration, distance_km, weather_condition.
    19–23 Tribunal on the contested cards, in this order: distance_km (provenance), weather_condition (skew), then the two genuinely arguable ones. Resolve distance_km and weather_condition crisply. Do not resolve the two arguable ones by fiat — run §9's structured argument.
    23–25 Debrief. §6. Close on the one question. Land the "remove the ambiguity by construction" point.

    6. Debrief questions¶

    1. How many cards did you place in under ten seconds? (Usually 8 or 9. The question is decisive far more often than people expect, which is why it is worth memorising verbatim.)
    2. Which card changed verdict when you read its provenance line? (distance_km. Metered distance is known only at dropoff — LEAKAGE. Routing-engine estimated distance is known at request — VALID. Same column name, opposite verdict. Lineage is not documentation; it is a correctness control.)
    3. What is the difference between STALE and LEAKAGE? (Direction in time. Leakage reads the future; staleness reads too far into the past. Leakage inflates your offline metric and cannot be reproduced live; staleness does not inflate anything — it silently degrades live performance while offline looks fine.)
    4. Why do LEAKAGE and UNAVAILABLE ONLINE feel identical? (Both present as "great offline, mediocre live." Fixing a leak when the real problem is an online re-implementation moves the live metric by almost nothing — which is exactly what CS-08's Wojhah discovered after a quarter of work.)
    5. What single architectural change removes the whole UNAVAILABLE ONLINE category? (Materialise the same table to both consumers: compute once in gold.eta_features, publish a copy to masar:features:zone:{zone_id}. Serving reads a value; it never derives one. The failure mode becomes staleness, which is bounded and monitorable, instead of skew, which is neither.)
    6. The two arguable features — did your team agree? (§9. The professional answer is not a verdict; it is a construction that makes the question unnecessary.)
    7. Which of these features did CS-11 corrupt, and does that change its verdict? (rolling_avg_speed_10m. No — it remains VALID. It was never leaking, never stale, never unavailable. It was semantically wrong. Point-in-time correctness and semantic correctness are independent properties, and you need both. This is the most important sentence in the activity.)

    9. Answer key — full rationale per column¶

    # Column Verdict Rationale
    1 trip_id VALID (not a model input) Created at 08:12:00 as part of the request. It is row identity and a join key, never a feature — a model that learns from a monotonically increasing id is learning the calendar. Flag any team that feeds it to the model
    2 pickup_zone_id VALID Resolved from device geolocation at the request instant. The rider is physically there
    3 dropoff_zone_id VALID Entered by the rider before the request was submitted. The subtlety worth naming: it is the requested destination, not the actual one. If a rider changes destination mid-trip, the actual dropoff is future information; the requested one is not
    4 hour_of_day VALID Deterministic function of feature_ts. Zero risk
    5 day_of_week VALID Same. (Note for Masar: Friday's demand curve differs materially from Sunday–Thursday's, so this is a genuinely useful feature, not filler)
    6 rolling_zone_demand_15m ARGUABLE → VALID if the frame is bounded See §10. The frame reaches CURRENT ROW, which includes the prediction instant and any row sharing that feature_ts — including, arguably, this request itself
    7 rolling_avg_speed_10m VALID Window is strictly [feature_ts − 10 min, feature_ts). Nothing from the future. Available online from the same published table. And it is the column CS-11 corrupts — which does not change this verdict. Point-in-time correct and semantically wrong are independent failures
    8 driver_recent_trip_count LEAKAGE The calendar hour 08:00–09:00 contains the prediction instant and extends past it. The count includes trips starting at 08:31 and 08:52. Worse, the driver was not even assigned until 08:12:44 — 44 seconds after the prediction, so at prediction time there is no driver to count trips for. Two independent leaks in one column
    9 weather_condition UNAVAILABLE ONLINE (skew-prone) Not leaking — weather at 08:12 is observable at 08:12. But offline it is joined from weather_hourly (clear, dust, …) and online it comes from a third-party API with a different vocabulary ("Clear", "Dust storm"). The model receives categories it never saw in training. The fix is materialisation, not string normalisation — normalising two vocabularies keeps two implementations alive
    10 distance_km LEAKAGE — as provenanced The card says it is the metered distance, written at dropoff (08:41). That is future information. Change the provenance to the routing-engine estimate available at 08:12 and the same column name becomes VALID. The verdict is a property of the lineage, not of the name — this is the card that teaches the most
    11 historical_route_duration ARGUABLE → LEAKAGE as implemented; UNAVAILABLE ONLINE as served See §10. Offline, the 28-day window is anchored to the training job's run date, so a row from 3 May receives a median computed from trips through 1 June — a point-in-time violation. Online, it is a live 340 ms percentile query, blowing a 50 ms budget, over a 30-day window. Two different defects in one column
    12 feature_ts VALID (the anchor, not a feature) It is the reference point every other verdict is measured against. Its own correctness is the precondition for the entire exercise: if feature_ts is ever set at trip completion instead of at request, every window in the table silently admits the future and no lint will catch it
    13 label_actual_duration_min LEAKAGE if used as a feature; correct as the label Known only at 08:41:33. It is the target. The card is in the deck to test whether teams distinguish feature from label — a surprising number place it in VALID because "it is in the feature table". A label in a feature table is normal and correct; a label fed to the model as an input is the most complete leak possible

    Summary: VALID 7 (cards 1, 2, 3, 4, 5, 7, 12) · LEAKAGE 3 (8, 10, 13-as-feature) · UNAVAILABLE ONLINE 1 (9) · ARGUABLE 2 (6, 11) · STALE 0 as written — and the STALE category being empty is itself a finding: no column here is stale yet, but every column served from Redis at a 60-second publish cadence becomes stale the moment the publish job falls behind, which is why publish lag must be monitored as a first-class SLA.

    10. The two genuinely arguable features¶

    Run these as a structured argument. Take the "valid" case, then the "leaking" case, then ask for the construction that removes the question. Do not award a verdict.

    Arguable #1 — rolling_zone_demand_15m¶

    The case for VALID. At 08:12:00 the number of trip requests in Olaya over 07:57–08:12 is a fact about the past. It is observable, it is causally prior, and it is exactly the kind of signal a dispatch system exists to react to. Nothing from the future is included.

    The case for LEAKAGE. Three specific concerns, in increasing seriousness: 1. RANGE BETWEEN INTERVAL 15 MINUTES PRECEDING AND CURRENT ROW includes rows sharing an identical feature_ts. At 174 requests/second platform-wide, ties are common. 2. It includes this request. The feature is therefore partly a function of the event being predicted — a self-referential term that is unavailable online, where this request has not yet been counted. 3. The fatal one: the frame's correctness depends entirely on feature_ts meaning "request time" for every row in the table. If any upstream path ever sets feature_ts at trip completion, the window silently admits fifteen minutes of the future and no syntactic lint will detect it, because the SQL is unchanged.

    The construction that removes the argument — and this is the deliverable:

    -- 1. Close the frame strictly before the prediction instant
COUNT(*) OVER (PARTITION BY pickup_zone_id ORDER BY feature_ts
               RANGE BETWEEN INTERVAL 15 MINUTES PRECEDING
                         AND INTERVAL  1 SECOND  PRECEDING)  AS rolling_zone_demand_15m

-- 2. Assert the anchor, as a build-failing test
--    (this is the assertion that protects concern 3, which the lint cannot)
SELECT COUNT(*) FROM gold.eta_features WHERE feature_ts <> pickup_ts;   -- must be 0

    The lesson: the professional response to an arguable feature is not to win the argument. It is to remove the ambiguity, cheaply, so that nobody has the argument again — including the version of you that reviews this table in eight months.

    Arguable #2 — historical_route_duration¶

    The case for VALID. It is a median over historical completed trips on the same route at the same hour. It is strictly historical by construction, it is the single most predictive feature in the model, and removing it would materially degrade a model that is genuinely useful.

    The case for LEAKAGE. The window is anchored to the training job's run date, not to each row's feature_ts. So a training row from 3 May receives a median computed from trips through 1 June — a month of future information, averaged in. This is the most common point-in-time violation in industry, it is invisible in the SQL text (a lint sees only INTERVAL 28 DAY), and it inflates offline metrics quietly rather than dramatically.

    And separately: UNAVAILABLE ONLINE. The serving path issues a live percentile_cont against Postgres at p95 340 ms against a 50 ms budget, over a 30-day window where training used 28. Even with the anchoring fixed, training and serving compute different numbers.

    The construction that removes both problems:

    -- 1. Anchor the window PER ROW, not per job run
LEFT JOIN route_stats r
  ON  r.pickup_zone_id  = f.pickup_zone_id
  AND r.dropoff_zone_id = f.dropoff_zone_id
  AND r.hour_of_day     = f.hour_of_day
  AND r.as_of_date      = date(f.feature_ts)   -- <<< per-row anchor, precomputed daily
-- route_stats holds, for each as_of_date, the median over the 28 days ENDING that date.

-- 2. Materialise, do not re-derive online
--    publish route_stats into the online store keyed by
--    (pickup_zone, dropoff_zone, hour_of_day); serving does a GET, ~2 ms.

    The lesson: a feature definition that does not state its temporal anchor is not a definition. Two engineers can implement "28-day median" correctly and differently, and only one of them is point-in-time correct.

    The meta-lesson¶

    Both arguable features are arguable for the same underlying reason: their definitions are under-specified about time. Neither states, unambiguously, relative to what instant the window is measured. Every genuinely contested feature you will meet in your career will have this shape.

    The rule to write down:

    Every feature definition must state its anchor, its window boundaries (open or closed), and its source table. A definition missing any of the three is a conversation, not a specification.

    11. Extension for fast teams¶

    Hand these three cards to any team that finishes early. They are the traps that appear in real feature tables.

    ┌──────────────────────────────────────────────────────────────────────┐
│ X1  driver_rating                                           double   │
│     The driver's current star rating, from silver.drivers.           │
│     PROVENANCE: drivers.csv is OVERWRITTEN nightly with the          │
│     CURRENT rating. The training set joins today's ratings onto      │
│     trips from 90 days ago.                                          │
└──────────────────────────────────────────────────────────────────────┘
   Verdict: LEAKAGE. A slowly-changing dimension joined without an
   as-of date. The 90-day-old trip receives a rating partly EARNED BY
   THAT VERY TRIP. Fix: SCD-2 on drivers, joined as-of feature_ts.
   This is the single most common leak in production feature tables and
   it never appears in a window function, so no lint finds it.

┌──────────────────────────────────────────────────────────────────────┐
│ X2  zone_avg_fare_today                                     double   │
│     Mean fare in pickup_zone_id, for the CURRENT CALENDAR DAY.       │
└──────────────────────────────────────────────────────────────────────┘
   Verdict: LEAKAGE. A calendar-day bucket containing feature_ts
   extends to 23:59. An 08:12 prediction reads the whole day's mean.
   Same shape as driver_recent_trip_count. Calendar buckets that
   CONTAIN the prediction instant are always a leak.

┌──────────────────────────────────────────────────────────────────────┐
│ X3  surge_multiplier                                        double   │
│     The multiplier applied to this trip.                             │
│     PROVENANCE: silver.trips.surge_multiplier, written at the        │
│     moment the fare is quoted — 08:12:00.                            │
└──────────────────────────────────────────────────────────────────────┘
   Verdict: VALID — but interrogate it. It is known at 08:12:00, so it
   passes the question. However it is DOWNSTREAM OF THE MODEL'S OWN
   OUTPUT in the live system (surge responds to demand, which responds
   to dispatch, which responds to ETA). Training on it creates a
   feedback loop. Point-in-time correct, architecturally hazardous.
   The question resolves timing; it does not resolve causality.

┌──────────────────────────────────────────────────────────────────────┐
│ X4  vehicle_capacity                                           int   │
│     Seats in the assigned vehicle, from vehicles.csv.                │
└──────────────────────────────────────────────────────────────────────┘
   Verdict: LEAKAGE (by assignment timing). The vehicle is assigned at
   08:12:44 — 44 seconds AFTER the prediction. Any attribute of the
   assigned driver or vehicle is future information at quote time.
   Same root cause as driver_recent_trip_count, different column.

    12. Cohort variants¶

    Small cohort (< 8 participants). Run as an individual sort with mandatory defence. Each participant takes 2–3 cards, places them, and defends each aloud in 30 seconds; any other participant may challenge once, and a challenge must cite the timestamp that makes the feature invalid. Then run §10's two arguable features as a full-group structured argument with the facilitator writing both cases on the board. With few people, the depth on the two arguable features is worth more than breadth, so allocate 10 of the 25 minutes to §10 alone. Include the §11 extension cards in the main deck rather than holding them back.

    Large cohort (> 20 participants). Run role-split tribunals. Assign half the teams the role of Model Owner (incentive: keep as many features as possible — every removed feature costs accuracy) and half the role of Platform Reviewer (incentive: block anything that cannot be reproduced live). Both sort the same 13 cards independently (10 min), then pair one of each and reconcile card by card (7 min). They will disagree on exactly cards 6, 10 and 11 — which is precisely the disagreement that happens in real feature reviews, with real incentives behind it. Close with a 5-minute plenary in which each pair reports the construction they agreed on for their contested cards, not the verdict. The role split makes the ambiguity felt rather than described, and it is the strongest version of this activity. Duration 30 minutes.
