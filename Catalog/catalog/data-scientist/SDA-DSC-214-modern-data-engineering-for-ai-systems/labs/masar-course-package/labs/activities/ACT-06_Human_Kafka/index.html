    ACT-06 — Human Kafka¶

    Field Value
    Activity ID ACT-06
    Module M5 — Streaming Data and Event-Driven Architectures
    Day / Hour Day 4 · Hour 1 (before LAB-05; pairs naturally with CS-05)
    Duration 20 minutes
    Format Embodied simulation — partitions, offsets, consumer groups, rebalance and replay, acted out with keyed cards
    Group size Whole room; needs at least 9 people (1 producer, 4 partitions, 4 consumers). Scales to 30
    Deliverable Each consumer's offset ledger, reconciled at the end; plus the room's answer to "what did the replay cost us?"

    1. Objective¶

    Participants physically experience the four properties that make a log different from a queue:

    1. Ordering is per partition, not per topic — and the partition key decides what is ordered.
    2. An offset is a reader's position, not a property of the message — messages are not consumed and destroyed.
    3. Consumer groups are independent — two groups read the same events at different speeds without interfering.
    4. Replay is a normal operation — resetting an offset is a routine act, not a recovery project.

    2. Materials (printable)¶

    • Event cards (§7): 40 cards, each a GPS ping with vehicle_id, ts, speed_kmh. Printed and shuffled.
    • Partition mats (§7): 4 A3 sheets labelled PARTITION 0–3, each with an offset ruler printed down the side (0, 1, 2, …).
    • Offset ledgers (§7): one per consumer.
    • Role badges: PRODUCER, PARTITION 0–3, CONSUMER-GROUP-AI (2 people), CONSUMER-GROUP-OPS (2 people), OBSERVER (everyone else).
    • A hash rule posted visibly: partition = (last digit of vehicle_id) mod 4.

    3. Setup (5 minutes)¶

    Lay the four partition mats in a row on a long table or the floor. The PRODUCER stands at one end with the shuffled deck. The two consumer groups stand on the far side, each with its own ledger. Post the hash rule and the layout:

                  PRODUCER  (keys by vehicle_id)
                  │
      ┌───────────┼───────────┬───────────┐
      ▼           ▼           ▼           ▼
  ┌───────┐   ┌───────┐   ┌───────┐   ┌───────┐
  │ PART 0│   │ PART 1│   │ PART 2│   │ PART 3│    cards laid LEFT TO RIGHT
  │ 0 1 2 │   │ 0 1 2 │   │ 0 1 2 │   │ 0 1 2 │    in arrival order = OFFSETS
  └───────┘   └───────┘   └───────┘   └───────┘
      ▲           ▲           ▲           ▲
      └───────────┴─────┬─────┴───────────┘
                        │
         GROUP: ai-features        GROUP: ops-alerts
         (2 consumers)             (2 consumers)
         own offsets               own offsets

    4. Run of show¶

    Min Phase What happens Facilitator
    0–2 Frame. "This is not a queue. Nothing is consumed and destroyed. Each of you is a position in a log that everyone can read." Post the hash rule.
    2–5 Produce. PRODUCER deals all 40 cards, one at a time, applying the hash rule aloud. Each partition lays its cards left to right — that ordering is the offsets. Enforce "aloud". The room must hear the key deciding the partition.
    5–8 Consume, both groups. Each group's two consumers split the partitions (2 each) and read forward, recording partition:offset in their ledger. AI group reads slowly (every card). OPS group reads fast (skims). Both groups read the same cards. Ask an observer: "Did the AI group's reading remove anything from the OPS group's view?"
    8–10 Ordering check. Ask: "Vehicle VEH-00884 appears 5 times. Are its pings in order?" Then: "Are ping 3 of VEH-00884 and ping 3 of VEH-00459 in order relative to each other?" Answers: yes and no, and it does not matter. This is the ordering guarantee, discovered rather than told.
    10–13 Rebalance. Tap one OPS consumer and say "you have crashed." They step away. The remaining OPS consumer must take over all 4 partitions, starting from the committed offsets in the ledger, not from the beginning. Watch for a consumer restarting from offset 0 — that is the reprocessing bug from CS-05's INC-2025-0412, produced live.
    13–16 Replay. "The OPS alerting rule was wrong all morning. Reset the ops-alerts group to offset 0 on every partition and re-read." Then the key question: "What happened to the AI group's offsets?" (Nothing. That independence is the whole architecture.)
    16–18 The poisoned card. Slip in one prepared card with ts 11 hours in the future (§7). Ask both groups: "Your watermark is now driven by the highest timestamp you have seen. What happens to every card after this one?" They are all classified as late. One card silenced the pipeline. This is CS-10's Round 3, felt rather than read.
    18–20 Debrief. §5. Land the four properties.

    5. Debrief questions¶

    1. Did reading remove anything? (No. A log is read at a position; messages persist for the retention period. This is the single largest difference from a traditional queue and it is what makes everything else possible.)
    2. Whose ordering is guaranteed? (One vehicle's pings, because vehicle_id was the key. Two different vehicles have no ordering guarantee relative to each other — and no consumer needs one. You choose the key by asking what must not be reordered.)
    3. What would have happened if the key had been city? (Three keys, four partitions: one partition idle, one holding a third of all events. Total lag would look flat while one partition grew without bound. Ask the room to physically re-deal 10 cards by city and look at the mats.)
    4. When the OPS consumer crashed, why did the survivor not start from zero? (Because offsets are committed by the group, not held by the individual. Restarting from zero would reprocess everything — surge inflated on phantom demand, exactly CS-05's incident.)
    5. The replay reset OPS to zero. What did the AI group have to do? (Nothing. Two groups, two offset ledgers, zero coordination. This is why a new consumer costs nothing and needs no producer change.)
    6. The future-dated card. (One bad clock advanced the watermark past every subsequent legitimate event. Lag reads zero. Throughput reads normal. Output stops. In streaming, the dangerous failures are the quiet ones.)
    7. What did the replay cost? (Compute to re-process, and duplicate side effects if the consumer's sink is not idempotent — OPS would re-fire every alert. This is why replay strategy and sink idempotency are one decision, not two.)

    6. Answer key¶

    Partition assignment (hash rule: last digit of vehicle_id mod 4)¶

    vehicle_id last digit partition
    VEH-00884 4 0
    VEH-00459 9 1
    VEH-02210 0 0
    VEH-03427 7 3
    VEH-01922 2 2
    VEH-00787 7 3
    VEH-00698 8 0
    VEH-00001 1 1

    Every ping from one vehicle lands on one partition, always. That is the ordering guarantee, and it is a consequence of the key, not of the broker.

    Expected end state¶

    ai-features group ops-alerts group
    After the initial read p0:10, p1:9, p2:11, p3:10 p0:10, p1:9, p2:11, p3:10
    After the OPS crash + rebalance unchanged one consumer now owns all 4, resuming from committed offsets
    After the OPS replay unchanged reset to p0:0, p1:0, p2:0, p3:0, then re-read to the end
    Side effects of the replay none every alert re-fired, unless the sink is idempotent

    The four properties (write on the board at the end)¶

    1. ORDER IS PER PARTITION.   The key decides what is ordered. Choose it by
                             asking: what must not be reordered?
2. AN OFFSET IS A POSITION.  Not a property of the message. Reading destroys
                             nothing. Two readers, two positions, no conflict.
3. GROUPS ARE INDEPENDENT.   A new consumer is a new group and a starting
                             offset. No producer change. No coordination.
4. REPLAY IS ROUTINE.        Reset an offset and re-read. The cost is compute
                             plus any non-idempotent side effect.

    8. Cohort variants¶

    Small cohort (< 8 participants). Reduce to 2 partitions and 2 consumer groups of one person each; the facilitator is the PRODUCER and also plays the second member of a group when the rebalance is needed. Deal 20 cards rather than 40. Keep every phase — the rebalance and the replay are the two that must survive a cut, and the poisoned card is worth more than the volume. Add an extension: give one participant a second, different hash rule (partition = hash(trip_id) mod 2) and re-deal 8 cards, then ask what broke (a vehicle's pings now scatter across partitions; per-vehicle ordering is gone).

    Large cohort (> 20 participants). Use 6 partitions, 3 consumer groups (ai-features, ops-alerts, and a new fraud-investigation group introduced at minute 13 starting from offset 0). The third group is the payoff: it joins mid-session, reads the entire history from the beginning, and requires no change from the producer and no coordination with the other two groups. Stop the room and say so explicitly — that moment is LinkedIn's insight, physically demonstrated, and it is the bridge into CS-10. Everyone not holding a role is an OBSERVER with a specific assignment: half track total events processed across all groups (it exceeds 40, which surprises people), half track whether any group ever waited for another (none did). Duration 26 minutes.
