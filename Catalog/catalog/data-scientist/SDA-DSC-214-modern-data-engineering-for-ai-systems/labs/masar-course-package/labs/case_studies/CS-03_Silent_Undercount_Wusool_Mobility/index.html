    CS-03 — The Silent Undercount at "Wusool" Mobility¶

    Field Value
    Case ID CS-03
    Module M3 — ETL vs ELT and the Transformation Layer
    Day / Hour Day 2 · Hour 4 (immediately after LAB-03's incremental MERGE task)
    Duration 45 minutes
    Format Investigation → design exercise
    Group size 3–4 per team
    Deliverable (a) The exact SQL/dbt config that fixes the daily model; (b) a backfill runbook for six months that is safe to run twice; (c) the two tests that would have caught this in week one, written as dbt test YAML
    Materials Evidence pack §5 per team (5 pages); the Masar late_trips fixture open on the projector (data/fixtures/late_trips/trips_late_2026-06-02.csv, 126 rows / 120 new trip_id)
    Facilitator prep 10 min. Have data/fixtures/late_trips/README.md and the LAB-03 solution's incremental model open in tabs — you will demo the fix live in the debrief

    1. Scenario¶

    "Wusool" (وصول) is a Saudi mobility operator. Every morning at 06:00 a pipeline computes the previous day's completed trips per driver, and that figure pays the driver incentive.

    For four months, drivers have complained the count is low. Each complaint is investigated individually; each time, finance re-runs the query and gets the same number, and the complaint is closed as unfounded. A driver representative has now escalated to a labour-relations review, with a sample of 40 drivers who each have app screenshots showing more completed trips than they were paid for.

    Finance is confident. The count is reproducible. The query is correct.

    The count is also wrong.

    2. Business context¶

    Item Figure
    Incentive pool paid from the daily completed-trip count SAR 2,000,000 / month
    Period under review 6 months
    Estimated systematic undercount (to be derived by the teams) ~1.9% of completed trips
    Estimated underpayment over 6 months ≈ SAR 228,000
    Drivers in the escalated sample 40
    Drivers affected at all (any month) ~2,900 of 3,450
    Second-order exposure The same daily table is the label source for the zone demand-forecasting model

    The financial exposure is recoverable — Wusool can pay the difference. The harder exposures are the two that money does not fix: a labour-relations finding of systematic underpayment, and a demand model trained for six months on labels that are biased, not merely noisy.

    3. Technical challenge¶

    The daily model reads:

    WHERE trip_date = current_date() - INTERVAL 1 DAY

    and materialises with INSERT INTO ... SELECT. It runs once per day, at 06:00 +03, and never revisits a day it has already written.

    Trips whose payment settles after midnight — airport runs, long inter-city trips, trips where the driver's device was offline, and every trip flowing through the delayed settlement path — land in bronze.trips after the 06:00 job for the day they belong to has already run. They are then never counted, because tomorrow's run filters on tomorrow's date.

    Nothing errors. The row exists in bronze. It simply never enters the aggregate. The query is a correct implementation of a wrong assumption: that a day's data is complete at the moment you choose to look at it.

    4. Constraints¶

    • Source cannot be re-ingested. The upstream vendor retains 14 days; bronze is the only copy of anything older. (Frame this as a good property: immutable bronze is the reason the six-month correction is possible at all.)
    • Six months of history must be corrected and every corrected figure must be traceable back to raw for the labour review.
    • The fix must be auditable: a reviewer with no Spark knowledge must be able to follow the chain from a driver's payment to a trip_id in bronze.trips.
    • The backfill will be run by an on-call engineer, possibly twice, possibly interrupted. It must be safe under both.
    • The correction must not disturb the current daily run.

    5. Evidence pack¶

    P1 — Driver complaint, verbatim (translated)¶

    "On Thursday 4 June I completed 19 trips. The app shows 19. The incentive statement shows 17. On Sunday I completed 22, the statement shows 22. It is not every day. It is the busy days."

    "It is not every day. It is the busy days." — this single observation is the case. Late settlement correlates with load; the undercount is therefore not uniform, which is what makes it both hard to spot in aggregate and legally serious.

    P2 — The model as written (models/marts/driver_daily_trips.sql)¶

    {{ config(materialized='incremental') }}

SELECT
    trip_date,
    driver_id,
    COUNT(*) AS completed_trips,
    SUM(fare_sar) AS gross_fare_sar
FROM {{ ref('silver_trips') }}
WHERE status = 'completed'
  AND trip_date = current_date() - INTERVAL 1 DAY   -- <<<< the bug
GROUP BY trip_date, driver_id

    There is no unique_key. There is no incremental_strategy. is_incremental() is never called. The incremental materialisation is therefore an append, and re-running it duplicates rows — which is why nobody re-runs it, which is why nobody ever noticed the late trips.

    P3 — Ingestion lag distribution for bronze.trips (30-day sample, from _ingested_at minus dropoff_ts)¶

    Lag from trip completion to landing in bronze.trips Share of completed trips
    < 1 hour 91.4%
    1–6 hours 6.7%
    6–24 hours (still lands before the 06:00 job for its own day) 1.2%
    24–48 hours (lands AFTER its day's job has run) 1.6%
    48–72 hours 0.3%
    > 72 hours 0.02%

    1.9% of completed trips land after their day's aggregate was written and are never counted. Cross-check the arithmetic with the class: SAR 2,000,000 × 1.9% × 6 months ≈ SAR 228,000.

    P4 — Reconciliation, one day, run today for 4 June 2026¶

    -- what the incentive was paid from
SELECT SUM(completed_trips) FROM gold.driver_daily WHERE trip_date = '2026-06-04';
-- 6,681

-- what bronze actually holds for that day, as of now
SELECT COUNT(*) FROM bronze.trips
WHERE status='completed' AND DATE(dropoff_ts) = '2026-06-04';
-- 6,809

-- difference: 128 completed trips, 1.88%

    And the smoking gun — the same query grouped by landing time:

    SELECT DATE(_ingested_at) AS landed_on, COUNT(*)
FROM bronze.trips
WHERE status='completed' AND DATE(dropoff_ts)='2026-06-04'
GROUP BY 1 ORDER BY 1;

landed_on   | count
2026-06-04  |  6,203
2026-06-05  |    478      <- 6,681 counted by the 06:00 job on 06-05
2026-06-06  |    112      <- never counted
2026-06-07  |     16      <- never counted

    P5 — The Masar fixture, for the live drill¶

    data/fixtures/late_trips/trips_late_2026-06-02.csv — 126 rows, 120 of them new trip_id values for 2026-06-02, plus 6 rows that duplicate trip_ids already present in data/raw/trips_2026-06-02.csv.

    That fixture is Wusool's bug in miniature, and it is the fixture LAB-03 already made participants handle. The 120 new rows are the undercount. The 6 duplicates are the reason the fix must be a MERGE and not a re-insert — a lookback window that re-reads three days and appends would double-count every trip in those three days. State this explicitly: the naive fix for the undercount creates an overcount.

    6. Facilitation script (minute-by-minute)¶

    Min Activity Facilitator does Watch for
    0–3 Frame. Read P1 aloud, including the last sentence. Ask: "Finance re-ran the query and got the same number. Does that make the number right?" Write "reproducible ≠ correct" on the board. Leave it up. The room will split. Good.
    3–5 Distribute the pack; state the three deliverables. — —
    5–14 Round 1 — Find it. Teams locate the mechanism from P2–P4. Circulate silently for the first 4 minutes. Then to any stuck team: "P4 has two queries. Why did I give you the second one?" Most teams find it in 6–8 minutes. If a team finds it in 2, give them the extension in §7 Q5 immediately.
    14–17 Board the mechanism. One team explains; the rest confirm or correct. Draw the timeline: trip completes 23:40 Thu → lands 02:10 Sat → Friday's 06:00 job already ran → nothing ever looks again. Teams that describe it as "a timezone bug." It is not; it is a completeness bug. Correct this firmly — the distinction matters for the fix.
    17–32 Round 2 — Design the fix. All three deliverables. Emphasise deliverable (b): safe to run twice. Hand out P5 physically at minute 20, mid-round: "here is your test fixture — 126 rows, 120 new, 6 duplicates." Watch teams that had proposed a lookback + append visibly rewrite. The 6 duplicates are the pedagogical payload. Do not reveal them before minute 20.
    32–40 Present & stress-test. Each team hands its runbook to another team, which tries to break it: "I ran it, it failed at hour 3, I ran it again from the start." Score survival, not elegance. A runbook that survives a double-run wins. Teams that add "check whether it already ran" as a manual step — that is a human idempotency guarantee, i.e. none.
    40–45 Debrief + live demo. Run the LAB-03 solution's incremental model twice with the fixture landed in between; show MERGE counts 120 inserted / 6 updated, then 0 / 0. Close on §11. The second run showing zeros is the whole module in one screen. —

    7. Discussion questions with model answers¶

    Q1. Why did the original filter feel correct yet undercount?

    Model answer. Because it encodes an assumption that is invisible until stated: "a day's data is complete by the time I process it." In batch systems fed by well-behaved sources, that assumption holds often enough to survive review. It fails whenever the arrival time of a fact is decoupled from the occurrence time of the fact — which is always true in mobility, payments, IoT and anything involving a device that can be offline.

    The bug is the confusion of two different timestamps that both look like "when": - event time (dropoff_ts) — when the trip finished; a property of the world; - processing/arrival time (_ingested_at) — when we learned about it; a property of our infrastructure.

    The query filtered on event time but the job schedule implicitly filtered on arrival time, and the intersection of the two silently dropped everything that arrived late. Once a day is written, the pipeline has no mechanism to ever revisit it — there is no reopening of a closed period.

    Say the general form: a filter on event time combined with a single-pass schedule is a silent completeness bug in every batch pipeline that has ever been written.

    Q2. How does idempotency make the six-month backfill safe to run (even twice)?

    Model answer. Idempotency means f(f(x)) = f(x): applying the operation twice leaves the same state as applying it once. Concretely, replace INSERT INTO with a MERGE keyed on the natural grain:

    {{ config(
     materialized='incremental',
     incremental_strategy='merge',
     unique_key=['trip_date','driver_id']
) }}

    Now a re-run overwrites the affected (trip_date, driver_id) rows rather than adding to them. The consequences are all operational, and they are what actually matter at 03:00:

    • The on-call engineer does not need to know whether the previous attempt succeeded, partially succeeded, or died between the write and the log line. They just run it again.
    • The backfill can be chunked by month and each chunk retried independently, with no coordination and no state file.
    • A failure halfway through leaves a partially correct table rather than a corrupted one, and completing the run repairs it.
    • The same code path serves the daily run and the six-month backfill, so the backfill is executing exactly the logic that will run tomorrow — which is what makes the corrected figures defensible to the labour review.

    Contrast this with a non-idempotent backfill: it requires a runbook step that says "check whether this month already ran," i.e. it requires a human to hold correctness in their head at 03:00 during an incident. That is not a guarantee; that is a hope.

    Q3. What downstream AI harm did the label skew cause, and how would you detect it?

    Model answer. This is the question that connects M3 to the whole course, and it is worth over-running on.

    The daily completed-trip table feeds the zone demand-forecasting model's labels. The undercount is not random — P1's driver said it: "it is the busy days." Late settlement correlates with load, with airport and inter-city trips, with peak hours, and with poor connectivity. So the model has been trained for six months on labels that are systematically low exactly where demand is highest.

    The consequence is not "the model is slightly noisier." It is that the model has learned a flattened demand curve: it under-predicts peaks. Downstream, that under-allocates drivers precisely at the peaks, which increases rider wait times and cancellations at peak — which then reduces completed trips at peak in the next period, which confirms the model's under-prediction. The bias is self-reinforcing through the dispatch feedback loop. Retraining on more of the same data makes it worse, not better.

    Detection, in the order they would fire: 1. Reconciliation between gold.driver_daily and a fresh recount from bronze.trips for a closed period — the P4 query, run nightly for T−7. This is the cheapest and it catches it in one day. 2. Residual analysis segmented by hour and zone: if the model's errors were random, residuals would be centred at zero across every segment. Here they are negative at peak hours and near zero off-peak. A residual plot by hour-of-day would have shown a clear shape from week one. 3. A freshness/completeness monitor on the source: bronze.trips continues to receive rows for trip_date = T on T+1 and T+2. That is a signal, sitting in plain view in the ingestion log, that the period is not closed.

    The general lesson to land: BI shows you a number; a model learns the shape of the error. A 1.9% undercount is invisible on a dashboard tile and structural in a model's weights. This is the same asymmetry that CS-11 will make the centrepiece of the whole course.

    Q4. What test, added earlier, would have caught this in week one?

    Model answer. Two, and teams should write both as dbt YAML:

    # 1. Reconciliation: the mart must equal a fresh recount of a CLOSED period.
#    This is the test that actually catches the bug.
- name: driver_daily_reconciles_to_bronze
  description: >
    For a period 7 days old (assumed closed), the sum of completed_trips in the mart
    must equal the count of completed trips in bronze for the same trip_date.
    Fails if late-arriving rows were never absorbed.
  test: assert_equal_counts
  config:
    where: "trip_date = current_date() - INTERVAL 7 DAY"

# 2. Source freshness / late-arrival surveillance: rows must stop arriving for a
#    closed day. If they don't, the lookback window is too short.
- name: no_arrivals_for_closed_days
  description: >
    Fails if bronze.trips receives any row whose dropoff_ts is older than the
    lookback window (3 days). Directly measures whether the chosen window is adequate.
  test: assert_no_rows
  config:
    where: "_ingested_at::date - dropoff_ts::date > 3"

    Plus the two hygiene tests that should have existed anyway and would have made the bug loud rather than silent:

    models:
  - name: driver_daily
    tests:
      - dbt_utils.unique_combination_of_columns:
          combination_of_columns: [trip_date, driver_id]   # would fail loudly on any re-run
    columns:
      - name: completed_trips
        tests: [not_null, dbt_utils.accepted_range: {min_value: 0}]

    Make the point explicitly: unique_combination_of_columns does not detect the undercount, but it removes the fear of re-running, and the fear of re-running is what let the undercount survive four months. Tests that make an operation safe are worth as much as tests that detect a fault.

    The second test is the subtle one and the one to spend time on: it does not check the mart at all. It measures the assumption the mart depends on. Most data tests check outputs; the tests that prevent silent bugs check assumptions.

    Q5 (extension for fast teams). What lookback window should Wusool choose, and how do you defend the number?

    Model answer. From P3: a 3-day lookback captures 99.98% of arrivals; a 2-day window captures 99.68%; a 1-day window captures 98.1% (the status quo plus one day). The residual beyond 3 days is 0.02%, roughly 1 trip in 5,000.

    Defend it as a cost/completeness trade-off with a stated residual, never as "3 days feels right": each extra lookback day re-MERGEs ~48,200 rows, costing seconds; 3 days is cheap. But the number must be re-derived from the arrival distribution quarterly, because a vendor change or a settlement-process change moves the tail — and the no_arrivals_for_closed_days test above is precisely the automatic alarm for that.

    This is the same conversation as the streaming watermark in M5 (ACT-07): how long do you keep a period open, given that keeping it open costs state and closing it early costs correctness? Point forward to it explicitly — the batch lookback and the streaming watermark are the same decision at different timescales.

    8. Solution approach¶

    8.1 The fixed model¶

    {{ config(
     materialized       = 'incremental',
     incremental_strategy = 'merge',
     unique_key         = ['trip_date','driver_id'],
     on_schema_change   = 'fail'
) }}

WITH scoped AS (
    SELECT *
    FROM {{ ref('silver_trips') }}
    WHERE status = 'completed'
    {% if is_incremental() %}
      -- reprocess a 3-day window so late arrivals land in the day they belong to
      AND trip_date >= current_date() - INTERVAL {{ var('lookback_days', 3) }} DAY
    {% endif %}
)
SELECT
    trip_date,
    driver_id,
    COUNT(*)        AS completed_trips,
    SUM(fare_sar)   AS gross_fare_sar,
    MAX(_ingested_at) AS _last_recomputed_at   -- audit column for the labour review
FROM scoped
GROUP BY trip_date, driver_id

    Three changes carry the whole fix: merge (safe re-run), unique_key at the true grain (defines what "the same row" means), and a lookback window instead of a single day (reopens recently-closed periods).

    8.2 The backfill runbook (deliverable b)¶

    # Backfill 2026-01-01 .. 2026-06-30, one month at a time.
# Safe to run twice, safe to resume, requires no memory of prior attempts.
for m in 2026-01 2026-02 2026-03 2026-04 2026-05 2026-06; do
  dbt run --select driver_daily \
          --vars "{'backfill_month': '${m}', 'lookback_days': 400}" \
          --target prod
  dbt test --select driver_daily --vars "{'backfill_month': '${m}'}"
done

    Runbook properties to make participants state explicitly: - Resumable — a failure at month 4 is fixed by re-running the whole loop. - Idempotent — a completed month re-run produces 0 rows updated. - Verifiable — dbt test after each month runs the reconciliation test on that month. - Auditable — every corrected row carries _last_recomputed_at, and dbt docs regenerates lineage from the corrected figure back to bronze.trips and to _source_file, which is exactly the chain the labour review needs. - Non-disruptive — it writes the same table by the same code path as the daily run, so there is no "backfill version" of the logic that could differ from production.

    8.3 The correction communication¶

    The labour review will not read SQL. Produce for it: the per-driver delta table (old count, new count, difference, SAR owed), the arrival-lag distribution P3 as the explanation of why it happened, and the lineage graph as the proof that the new figure derives from raw. Wusool should pay the difference before being asked to. State to the class that the engineering fix is 20 lines and the institutional repair is everything else.

    9. What good looks like vs common wrong turns¶

    What good looks like Common wrong turn
    Distinguishes event time (dropoff_ts) from arrival time (_ingested_at) explicitly Calls it a timezone bug and adds a CONVERT_TZ, which changes the number without fixing completeness
    Fixes with MERGE on (trip_date, driver_id) Adds a 3-day lookback and keeps INSERT INTO — turning an undercount into a 3× overcount, which the 6 duplicate rows in the fixture will demonstrate
    Derives the lookback window from P3's arrival distribution and states the residual Picks 7 days because "7 is safe", with no cost or residual analysis
    Writes a reconciliation test against a closed period Writes only not_null and unique tests, neither of which detects an undercount
    Writes a test on the assumption (do rows still arrive for closed days?) Tests only the output table
    Traces the label skew into the demand model and names the feedback loop Treats it purely as a payroll error
    Backfill runbook survives being run twice by a stranger at 03:00 Runbook contains "first check whether this month has already been processed"
    Notes that unique_key removes the fear of re-running, which is what let the bug survive Treats the uniqueness test as unrelated to the incident
    Pays the drivers before the review demands it Focuses entirely on the technical remediation

    10. Debrief (5 minutes)¶

    1. Point at "reproducible ≠ correct" on the board. Finance re-ran the query dozens of times and got the same answer every time. Reproducibility is a property of the computation; correctness is a property of the relationship between the computation and the world. Wusool had perfect reproducibility of a wrong number for four months.
    2. The driver was the best monitoring system Wusool had. "It is not every day, it is the busy days" is a precise, correct, actionable bug report from someone with no access to the pipeline. It was closed as unfounded four times because the system of record agreed with itself. When a human with ground truth disagrees with your platform, the platform is on trial, not the human.
    3. Run the live demo. Land data/fixtures/late_trips/trips_late_2026-06-02.csv between two dbt run invocations of the fixed model. First run: 120 inserted, 6 updated. Second run, unchanged input: 0 inserted, 0 updated. That pair of lines is M3 in its entirety — processes only what changed, corrects late data, never duplicates.
    4. Name the general class. Every batch pipeline in the world contains a filter on event time and a schedule that decides when to look. Where those two meet without a lookback and a merge, there is a silent undercount waiting. It will not raise an error, it will not fail a test, and it will bias a model before it embarrasses a dashboard.
    5. The course question. "What happens to Masar's AI system if late-arriving trips are silently dropped?" The demand model learns a flattened peak, under-allocates drivers at exactly the busiest hours, and the resulting cancellations confirm its own under-prediction. The data engineering defect became a self-fulfilling operational failure — and no dashboard anywhere showed a red number.

    11. The one sentence participants should leave with¶

    A filter on event time plus a schedule that only ever looks once is a silent undercount, not a bug report — fix it with a lookback window and a MERGE on the true grain, and remember that a model does not merely inherit the error, it learns its shape.
