    CS-10 — LinkedIn and Kafka: Designing a Central Messaging Backbone¶

    Field Value
    Case ID CS-10
    Module M5 — Streaming Data and Event-Driven Architectures
    Day / Hour Day 4 · Hour 3 (after CS-05 and LAB-05; this is the design exercise that generalises both)
    Duration 75 minutes (three escalating rounds of 15 min + reviews)
    Format Design exercise, scored
    Group size 4 per team; 3–6 teams
    Deliverable A completed design sheet (§8.5) with a value and a justification for all twelve decisions, plus the team's answer to "what breaks first at 15M events/day and how do you know before it does"
    Materials Design sheet §8.5 printed per team; the escalation cards §5 P4–P6 in three sealed envelopes per team, opened on the facilitator's signal; the scoring sheet §8.6 for cross-team review; A1 paper for the architecture drawing
    Facilitator prep 25 min. This is the longest and most demanding case in the course. Rehearse the round transitions — the escalations must arrive after teams have committed to a design, never before

    This is a design exercise, not a Kafka tutorial. No team should spend a minute on broker installation, CLI syntax or configuration files. Every deliverable is a decision with a justification. If a team asks "which flag sets that", the answer is: "name the behaviour you want and why; the flag is a footnote."

    1. Scenario¶

    1.1 Where the pattern comes from¶

    Kafka originated at LinkedIn around 2010, to solve an organisational problem rather than a technical one. LinkedIn had many systems that each needed data from many other systems: profile updates, connection graphs, activity feeds, search indexing, monitoring, offline analytics, and a growing set of recommendation models. The result was a dense mesh of point-to-point integrations — each producer knowing each consumer, each new consumer requiring a change to every producer, each pipeline built and operated independently.

    The reframing was to stop treating data movement as integration and start treating it as publication. Producers publish facts to a durable, ordered, replayable log. Consumers subscribe independently, at their own pace, and read from their own position. Adding a consumer becomes a decision the consumer makes alone; the producer neither knows nor cares.

    Two properties made this more than a message queue and they are the two properties that matter for this case:

    • The log is durable and replayable. A message is not consumed and destroyed; it is read at an offset. A new consumer can start from the beginning. A broken consumer can rewind and reprocess. Reprocessing becomes a routine operation instead of a data-recovery project.
    • Consumers are decoupled in time and in failure. A slow, crashed or newly-deployed consumer does not slow the producer and does not affect other consumers, because each group holds its own offsets.

    LinkedIn ran this at a scale measured in trillions of messages per day. Masar's 15 million events per day is four to five orders of magnitude smaller — which is exactly why the architecture, not the scale, is what transfers.

    1.2 What Masar must build¶

    Masar's 14,000 vehicles emit, every 5 seconds:

    vehicle_id, latitude, longitude, speed, heading, timestamp, trip_id

    Two consumers exist today and both are business-critical, with genuinely different requirements:

    Consumer Needs Tolerance
    ETA features (AI) Complete, correct, unit-normalised positions feeding gold.eta_features Minutes of latency acceptable; incompleteness is not
    Operations alerts (Ops) Speeding, harsh braking, vehicle-stopped-in-traffic, geofence breach Seconds of latency required; a missed late event is acceptable

    More consumers are coming: a fraud investigation team, a regulator reporting feed, and a driver-safety scoring model. The design must make adding the fourth consumer a decision the fourth consumer makes alone.

    2. Business context¶

    Item Figure

    3. Technical challenge¶

    Design the pipeline from vehicle to both consumers, then defend it against three escalating realities that arrive after the design is committed.

    4. Constraints¶

    • Device firmware cannot change this year: the 5-second interval, the field names and the transport are fixed.
    • The platform is redeployed twice a week; correctness must survive restarts without human intervention.
    • Bronze must be append-only and immutable (CS-09's ADR is binding).
    • PDPL: precise lat/lon is sensitive-personal; retention on raw GPS is 30 days.
    • SAR 65,000/month ceiling.
    • No design may require a producer change to add a consumer.

    P2 — The twelve decisions (the design sheet; §8.5 is the printable version)¶

    1. Topic partitioning key · 2. Partition count · 3. Topic retention · 4. Replication factor · 5. Consumer-group topology · 6. Delivery semantics and the mechanism that achieves them · 7. Watermark length, per consumer · 8. Deduplication key and window, and the layer it happens in · 9. Checkpoint placement · 10. Replay strategy · 11. Backpressure response · 12. The health metric and its alert condition.

    P3 — What one event looks like on the wire, and what Bronze must record¶

    // produced
{"vehicle_id":"VEH-00884","latitude":26.41803,"longitude":50.10770,"speed":15.3,
 "heading":211,"timestamp":"2026-06-01T19:38:56Z","trip_id":"TRP-20260601-0000000"}

// landed in bronze.gps_events (canonical schema, CONVENTIONS.md)
{"event_id":"ev_00009f025f480001","vehicle_id":"VEH-00884","driver_id":"DRV-02492",
 "trip_id":"TRP-20260601-0000000","ts":"2026-06-01T19:38:56Z",
 "payload":{"lat":26.41803,"lon":50.10770,"speed_kmh":15.3,"heading_deg":211,"accuracy_m":17.3},
 "producer_version":"2.4.0",
 "_ingested_at":"2026-06-01T19:39:02Z","_source_file":"masar.gps.pings:7:441829",
 "_batch_id":"b_20260601_193900"}

    Note _source_file for a stream is topic:partition:offset. That triple is the streaming equivalent of a filename, and it is what makes an individual event traceable back to its exact position in the log — the single most useful thing you can record about a streamed row.

    P4 — ESCALATION ROUND 1 (sealed envelope; open at minute 25)¶

    Late-arriving events.

    Riyadh has tunnels, multi-storey car parks and underground malls. A device that loses connectivity buffers locally and flushes when it reconnects. Measured over 7 days:

    Lag (event time → arrival at broker) Share Cumulative
    ≤ 5 s 96.20% 96.20%
    5–30 s 2.10% 98.30%
    30 s – 2 min 0.90% 99.20%
    2–5 min 0.55% 99.75%
    5–10 min 0.18% 99.93%
    10–30 min 0.06% 99.99%
    > 30 min (device offline for a shift) 0.01% 100.00%

    A single device, after 40 minutes underground, flushes 480 buffered pings in under two seconds.

    Answer: (a) What watermark do you choose for the Ops path and for the AI path, and what residual do you accept for each? (b) What happens to the 480-ping burst in your design — is it one micro-batch or 480? (c) Does the burst reorder anything within its partition? (d) Is any of this data lost?

    P5 — ESCALATION ROUND 2 (sealed envelope; open at minute 40)¶

    Duplicates.

    Three independent sources of duplicates, and your design must handle all three:

    1. Producer-side. The device SDK retries on an unacknowledged send. Measured duplicate event_id rate at the broker: 0.4% (~60,000/day), and it is not uniform — it spikes to 3% for devices with poor connectivity, i.e. exactly the devices that also produce the late bursts in Round 1.
    2. Broker-side. A producer retry after a partial failure can write the same event to the same partition twice at different offsets.
    3. Consumer-side. A consumer restarted after processing a micro-batch but before its offsets were committed will reprocess that batch. Your platform is redeployed twice a week.

    On 4 June a deploy caused source (3): 7,300 pings reprocessed. gold.zone_hourly_demand read 1.41× actual for zone Z-RUH-01. Surge rose to 2.3× on demand that did not exist.

    Answer: (a) Which of the three does your delivery semantic solve, and which does it not? (b) What is your dedup key, in which layer does dedup happen, and over what window? (c) Bronze retains duplicates — how do you then guarantee silver.vehicle_positions has exactly one row per event? (d) Your dedup window is finite. What happens to a duplicate that arrives after it closes, and how would you know?

    P6 — ESCALATION ROUND 3 (sealed envelope; open at minute 55)¶

    Out-of-order timestamps, and one clock that lies.

    ~1% of events arrive with ts out of order relative to the preceding event from the same vehicle. Three distinct causes, and they are not the same problem:

    1. Network reordering — two pings sent 5 s apart arrive in the reverse order. Small offsets, seconds.
    2. Buffered flush (Round 1) — a device emits 480 events whose ts values are all in the past, interleaved with live events from other vehicles.
    3. A wrong device clock. 61 vehicles are running a firmware build whose NTP sync failed. Their ts is ahead by between 4 and 11 hours. These events arrive with timestamps in the future.

    Cause 3 is the dangerous one. In an event-time pipeline, a single event with a timestamp 11 hours in the future advances the watermark by 11 hours, and every subsequent legitimate event is then classified as late and dropped. One misconfigured device can silence the pipeline for the entire fleet.

    Answer: (a) Does your design survive cause 3? Trace it through. (b) Where do you defend — producer, broker, stream, or Silver? (c) What do you do with a future-dated event: drop it, clamp it, quarantine it, or let it through? (d) What alerts, and on what signal? (e) Now that you have seen all three rounds: what breaks first at 15M events/day, and how do you know before it does?

    7. Discussion questions with model answers¶

    Q1. What must not be reordered? (decision 1 — the partition key)

    Model answer. Kafka guarantees ordering within a partition, not across partitions. So the partition key is chosen by asking: for which entity would out-of-order delivery produce a wrong answer?

    For Masar the answer is vehicle_id. A vehicle's position sequence must remain ordered: distance travelled, heading change, harsh-braking detection and speed differencing all read consecutive pings from one vehicle. Two pings from different vehicles arriving in either order changes nothing.

    Candidate key Verdict
    vehicle_id Correct. 14,000 distinct keys spread evenly; ordering preserved exactly where it is required
    trip_id Orders per trip, but a vehicle's idle-time pings (no trip_id) scatter across partitions, and per-vehicle continuity across trips is lost
    city 3 distinct values on 24 partitions: 21 idle partitions and a hot one carrying a third of 15M events. Total lag looks fine while one partition grows unbounded
    none (round-robin) Maximum throughput, no ordering anywhere. Requires every consumer to reorder in state
    event_id Perfect distribution, zero useful ordering — the pathological case

    Partition count: at 620 events/s peak, a single partition is comfortable; the count is driven by consumer parallelism, not by throughput. 24 gives room to scale a consumer group to 24 instances, divides evenly by 2, 3, 4, 6, 8 and 12, and keeps ~583 vehicles per partition. Note the asymmetry teams must internalise: partitions can be increased later but never decreased, and increasing them re-hashes keys, breaking per-key ordering across the change. Over-provision modestly; do not under-provision.

    Q2. How long do you keep the log? (decision 3 — retention)

    Model answer. 7 days, and the reasoning is what matters.

    Topic retention is not a storage decision — it is the answer to "how far back can I replay?" Replay is needed when: a consumer had a bug and its output must be rebuilt; a new consumer starts and needs recent history; a deploy corrupted a downstream table; or a schema/logic change requires reprocessing.

    7 days at 0.9 GB/day compressed with replication factor 3 is ~19 GB — trivially cheap. It covers a bug discovered on Friday and fixed on Monday, which is the realistic operational window.

    The important framing: the topic is not the system of record — bronze.gps_events is. Anything older than 7 days is reprocessed from Bronze, in batch, far more cheaply than a stream replay. Retention therefore only needs to cover the window in which streaming replay is the right tool. A team proposing 90-day retention has confused the log with the lake.

    Replication factor 3, min.insync.replicas=2. Standard durability; tolerates one broker loss without data loss and one more without unavailability.

    Q3. How many consumer groups, and why not one? (decision 5)

    Model answer. Two groups now, one per consumer, and this is the property that makes the whole architecture worth building.

    masar.gps.pings ──┬── group: gps-bronze-land   (AI path: bronze → silver → gold.eta_features)
                  └── group: ops-alerts        (Ops path: low-latency alerting)

    A consumer group holds its own offsets. Therefore: - The Ops path can run a 2-second trigger; the AI path a 30-second trigger. Neither constrains the other. - If the Ops alerting sink is down for an hour, ingestion into Bronze is entirely unaffected. With a shared group, a stuck sink stalls both. - Replaying the Ops consumer from an earlier offset — because the alerting rules changed — does not replay ingestion, which would duplicate Bronze rows. - The fourth consumer (fraud, regulator, safety scoring) is added by creating a group and choosing a starting offset. No producer change, no coordination, no ticket. This is the LinkedIn insight, cashed out.

    Teams that propose one consumer writing to Bronze and then fanning out from Bronze to Ops are not wrong architecturally — but they must then explain how they meet a 5-second Ops SLA through a Delta table write, a commit and a downstream read. They cannot. The fan-out belongs at the log, because the log is the only place where two consumers can read the same data at two different speeds.

    Q4. What watermark, and what exactly does it cost? (decision 7)

    Model answer. Different watermarks per consumer, derived from P4's distribution, each with a stated residual:

    Path Watermark Admits Rationale
    Ops alerts 30 seconds 98.3% An alert about a harsh-braking event 4 minutes ago is worthless. Latency dominates completeness. Missing 1.7% of late events is explicitly acceptable — it was stated as a tolerance in §1.2
    AI / silver → gold 10 minutes 99.93% Feature correctness dominates; 5 minutes of latency is within the < 5 min freshness budget at a 30 s trigger. Drops 0.07%
    Training / historical none — batch rebuild from bronze.gps_events 100% Bronze is append-only and complete

    The insight most teams miss, and the one to make explicit: a watermark decides what the streaming aggregate sees; it never decides what you keep. Every event, however late, is in Bronze. The 0.07% dropped from the AI streaming path is absorbed by the next batch rebuild of gold.eta_features. This is why the medallion architecture and streaming compose so well — you can be aggressive about latency in a derived view precisely because the raw layer forgives you.

    The cost of getting it wrong in the other direction is concrete: state ≈ (events/s) × (watermark) × (per-key state). Going from 10 minutes to 60 costs 6× the state, and at 620 ev/s peak that is the path to the 41 GB OOM in CS-05's INC-2025-0517.

    Q5. Dedup key, window, and layer? (decision 8, from P5)

    Model answer. Key event_id. Window: the watermark's duration. Layer: Silver, never Bronze.

    Which of P5's three duplicate sources does each mechanism address?

    Source Addressed by Notes
    (1) Producer SDK retry Dedup on event_id in Silver Exactly-once delivery does not help — the producer genuinely sent two distinct messages
    (2) Broker-side producer retry Idempotent producer + dedup on event_id Belt and braces
    (3) Consumer reprocess on restart Exactly-once effect: checkpointed offsets committed atomically with the Delta write This is the only one delivery semantics solves

    Teams that answer "exactly-once handles duplicates" have answered one third of the question. Exactly-once transport does not give you exactly-once data — the 0.4% producer duplicates arrive as legitimately distinct messages.

    Bronze retains duplicates (CS-09's ADR): the duplicate rate is a producer health metric, spiking to 3% on poorly-connected devices, and destroying it saves nothing. Silver deduplicates:

    (spark.readStream.table("bronze.gps_events")
   .withWatermark("ts", "10 minutes")
   .dropDuplicates(["event_id", "ts"])       # bounded by the watermark
   .writeStream.option("checkpointLocation", SILVER_CKPT)
   .toTable("silver.vehicle_positions"))

    P5(d) is the question that separates a good answer from an excellent one: a duplicate arriving after the dedup window closes will pass through. With a 10-minute window and P4's distribution, that is ~0.07% of duplicates — roughly 40 events/day. Detect it, do not pretend it cannot happen:

    -- daily audit: duplicates that escaped the streaming dedup window
SELECT event_id, COUNT(*) AS n FROM silver.vehicle_positions
WHERE DATE(ts) = current_date() - 1 GROUP BY event_id HAVING COUNT(*) > 1;

    and repair with a batch MERGE. Every streaming dedup is bounded; the mature design pairs it with an unbounded batch audit.

    Q6. The lying clock. Does your design survive it? (P6)

    Model answer. Almost no Round-0 design does, and this is the most valuable failure in the case.

    Trace it: 61 vehicles emit ts 4–11 hours in the future. Spark's watermark is max(event_time_seen) − watermark_delay. One event at T+11h drives the watermark to T+11h−10min. Every subsequent legitimate event, at real time T, is now more than 10 minutes behind the watermark and is dropped as late. The pipeline goes silent for the entire fleet. Nothing errors. Throughput is normal. Consumer lag is zero — the consumer is happily reading and discarding everything.

    Where to defend, in order of preference:

    1. At the stream, before the watermark is computed — the essential defence. Route future-dated events out before they can influence event time: 
      .withColumn("_clock_skew_s",
            unix_timestamp("_ingested_at") - unix_timestamp("ts"))
# a negative skew means the device claims the future
.withColumn("_ts_trusted", col("_clock_skew_s") > -60)   # 60 s of tolerable skew
# only trusted rows drive the watermark; untrusted rows are branched to quarantine
      This is a plausibility check on event time itself, and it is the streaming analogue of CS-06's reconciliation tier: it compares the device's claim against an independent clock (the broker's / ingest's).
    2. In Silver, as quarantine. Future-dated events land in Bronze (they are what the device said — CS-09 forbids dropping them) and are routed to quarantine in Silver with the measured skew recorded.
    3. At the producer. Correct, but the firmware cannot change this year — and, more generally, you cannot rely on a defence that lives in the system that is broken.

    Do not clamp. Rewriting ts to now() produces a plausible, wrong, unmarked value — a silent repair, which CS-09 classifies as NEVER-before-Bronze and CS-11 will show you the cost of.

    Alert on the skew distribution per producer_version: 

    SELECT producer_version, COUNT(*) AS n,
       AVG(unix_timestamp(_ingested_at) - unix_timestamp(ts)) AS mean_skew_s
FROM   bronze.gps_events WHERE DATE(_ingested_at) = current_date()
GROUP  BY producer_version;
    The 61 bad devices appear immediately as a distinct producer_version cohort with a negative mean skew. Segment by producer version — the same instruction as CS-06, and the exact query CS-11 will require tomorrow.

    Q7. What breaks first at 15M events/day, and how do you know before it does? (P6(e))

    Model answer. Ranked by how soon it bites, with the leading indicator for each:

    Rank What breaks Why Leading indicator Alert
    1 Small files in the Delta sinks A 30 s trigger writes 2,880 commits/day per sink. Within weeks numFiles reaches six figures and read latency climbs DESCRIBE DETAIL numFiles, and mean file size numFiles > 5000 or mean file size < 32 MB → scheduled OPTIMIZE
    2 Streaming state growth Watermark × event rate × per-key state. Grows silently until an executor OOMs, usually at peak State-store rows and bytes per micro-batch Any monotonic growth over 30 min at steady input rate
    3 Per-partition consumer lag on a skewed key If the key is ever changed to something skewed, one partition grows while the total looks flat Per-partition lag, never the sum Lag increasing for 5 consecutive minutes on any single partition
    4 Watermark stall A silent partition (all its vehicles offline) stops advancing event time; windows never close and output silently stops Watermark timestamp per query Watermark not advancing for 3× the trigger interval
    5 Checkpoint contention / accidental sharing Two queries sharing a checkpoint path corrupt each other's offsets on restart Checkpoint path uniqueness assertion at deploy Deploy-time check, not a runtime alert
    6 Cost Autoscaling upward under sustained load, quietly, against the SAR 65,000 ceiling SAR per million events (unit cost) Unit cost rising while volume is flat

    The primary metric on the wall is per-partition consumer lag, alerting on the derivative. The secondary is watermark progression — because a stalled watermark is the failure mode where lag is zero and nothing is being produced, which is invisible to every throughput-based monitor.

    Say the general lesson: in streaming, the dangerous failures are the quiet ones. A crashed consumer pages someone. A consumer that reads everything and drops it because a device from the future poisoned the watermark does not.

    8. Solution approach — reference architecture¶

    Present this as one defensible set of trade-offs, not as the answer. Every line below gives something up.

    8.1 The twelve decisions¶

    # Decision Value Trade-off explicitly accepted
    1 Partition key vehicle_id Per-trip ordering is not guaranteed across a vehicle's trips; acceptable because no consumer needs it
    2 Partitions 24 Over-provisioned for today's 620 ev/s. Chosen because partitions cannot be reduced and increasing them breaks per-key ordering across the change
    3 Retention 7 days Cannot stream-replay beyond a week; anything older is batch-reprocessed from bronze.gps_events, which is cheaper anyway
    4 Replication RF 3, min.insync.replicas=2 3× storage (~19 GB — negligible) for single-broker-loss durability
    5 Consumer groups Two: gps-bronze-land, ops-alerts Two pipelines to operate instead of one, in exchange for independent latency, independent failure and free consumer addition
    6 Delivery semantics Exactly-once effect: at-least-once delivery + idempotent Delta sink + offsets committed atomically with the write Slightly higher latency than fire-and-forget; solves only consumer-side duplicates, so producer dedup is still required
    7 Watermark Ops 30 s · AI 10 min · training none (batch from Bronze) Ops drops 1.7% of late events (accepted, stated); AI drops 0.07% from the streaming view only — all retained in Bronze
    8 Dedup Key event_id; window = watermark; in Silver Bronze carries ~60,000 duplicate rows/day (retained deliberately as a health metric); ~40 late duplicates/day escape the window and are caught by a daily batch audit
    9 Checkpoint One per query, on durable object storage, outside the table path Operational discipline required at deploy; a shared checkpoint is a top-two cause of streaming outages
    10 Replay Ops: new consumer group from a chosen offset. AI: never replay the stream — rebuild silver/gold in batch from Bronze Batch rebuild is slower than a replay but cannot duplicate Bronze rows, which a stream replay into an append-only sink would
    11 Backpressure Bounded maxOffsetsPerTrigger; autoscale 2–6 workers; lag alert on the derivative Under a sustained burst the AI path falls behind rather than failing — a deliberate choice of late over lost. Ops path is never throttled
    12 Health metric Per-partition consumer lag (alert: increasing 5 min) + watermark progression (alert: stalled 3× trigger) Two metrics rather than one, because lag alone cannot see a poisoned watermark

    8.2 Round 1 — late events, resolved¶

    • 480-ping burst arrives as one or two micro-batches, not 480 — micro-batch boundaries are time-based, not event-based. Throughput impact is negligible (480 events against 620/s peak).
    • Within its partition the burst is ordered by arrival, so its event times are all in the past relative to neighbouring live events: it is out-of-order by construction.
    • Ops path (30 s watermark): the whole burst is late and dropped. Correct — a 40-minute-old harsh-braking alert is worthless.
    • AI path (10 min watermark): the burst is late and dropped from the streaming aggregate.
    • Nothing is lost. All 480 events are in bronze.gps_events. The next batch rebuild of gold.eta_features includes them. The watermark bounded latency and state; it did not bound retention.

    8.3 Round 2 — duplicates, resolved¶

    Bronze: retain. Silver: dropDuplicates(["event_id","ts"]) under the 10-minute watermark. Daily batch audit for escapees, repaired by MERGE. Producer-side idempotence enabled where the SDK supports it. Monitor duplicate_rate per producer_version per day as a producer health metric — a rate that triples after a firmware rollout is a device-fleet defect worth knowing about, and it is only visible because Bronze kept the duplicates.

    8.4 Round 3 — out-of-order and the lying clock, resolved¶

    gps = (spark.readStream.format("kafka").option("subscribe","masar.gps.pings").load()
  .select(from_json(col("value").cast("string"), GPS_SCHEMA).alias("e"),
          col("topic"), col("partition"), col("offset"), col("timestamp").alias("_broker_ts"))
  .select("e.*", "topic", "partition", "offset", "_broker_ts")
  .withColumn("_ingested_at", current_timestamp())
  .withColumn("_source_file", concat_ws(":", col("topic"), col("partition"), col("offset")))
  .withColumn("_clock_skew_s", unix_timestamp("_ingested_at") - unix_timestamp("ts")))

# 1. BRONZE lands EVERYTHING, including future-dated events. Nothing is dropped.
(gps.writeStream.option("checkpointLocation", BRONZE_CKPT)
    .outputMode("append").toTable("bronze.gps_events"))

# 2. SILVER: only clock-plausible rows drive the watermark; the rest are quarantined.
trusted   = gps.filter(col("_clock_skew_s") > -60)
untrusted = gps.filter(col("_clock_skew_s") <= -60)      # device claims the future

(trusted.withWatermark("ts", "10 minutes")
        .dropDuplicates(["event_id", "ts"])
        .transform(normalise_speed_by_producer_version)   # <-- CS-11's missing step
        .writeStream.option("checkpointLocation", SILVER_CKPT)
        .toTable("silver.vehicle_positions"))

(untrusted.writeStream.option("checkpointLocation", QUAR_CKPT)
          .toTable("silver.vehicle_positions_quarantine"))

    Three properties: Bronze is complete, the watermark cannot be poisoned by one device, and the 61 bad devices are counted in quarantine rather than silently discarded — so the alert on _clock_skew_s per producer_version fires within a day.

    10. Debrief (7 minutes)¶

    1. The origin insight, restated as a test. LinkedIn's move was from integration to publication: producers publish, consumers subscribe at their own offsets. The test of whether you have built that, rather than a message queue with extra steps: can a new consumer be added without any producer knowing? If yes, you have a backbone. If no, you have point-to-point integration with a broker in the middle.
    2. Replayability changes what is possible operationally. Because the log is durable and positional, reprocessing is a routine operation, not a recovery project. That single property is why a bad deploy on Friday is fixed on Monday by resetting an offset, instead of by a data-restoration ticket.
    3. The three escalations were one lesson. Late events, duplicates and out-of-order timestamps are all the same problem: event time and processing time are different things, and every streaming decision is a trade about how long you are willing to wait for the world to finish telling you what happened. The watermark is that trade, made explicit and given a number.
    4. Round 3 broke almost every design in the room, and it broke them silently. One device with a wrong clock, and the pipeline reads everything and produces nothing, with zero lag and normal throughput. In streaming, the dangerous failures are the quiet ones, which is why watermark progression must be monitored alongside lag.
    5. What survives contact with 15M events/day is not throughput — 620 events/s is unremarkable. It is file hygiene, bounded state, per-partition observability, and a design that fails late rather than lost. Scale did not break anything in this case; unbounded things did.
    6. The course question. "What happens to Masar's AI system if the streaming backbone is designed for one consumer?" The second consumer is bolted on, they share offsets, and the first outage of one becomes an outage of both — and the third consumer, the one that has not been imagined yet, requires a producer change and therefore never gets built. Architecture is mostly about what you make cheap for the person who comes after you.
    7. Hand forward to CS-11, deliberately. "Every design in this room now handles late events, duplicates, out-of-order timestamps and a lying clock. Tomorrow you will investigate an incident in which all of that worked perfectly. Every event arrived exactly once, in order, with a correct timestamp and a valid schema — and the AI model degraded by 18% anyway."

    11. The one sentence participants should leave with¶

    Kafka's contribution was not speed but decoupling: a durable, ordered, replayable log lets every consumer read at its own position and its own pace, so the real design work is not the broker but the twelve decisions around it — partition key, watermark, dedup key and layer, checkpoint, replay and the health metric — and every one of them is a trade you must name, including the failure you agree to accept.
