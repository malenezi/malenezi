    CS-01 — The Reporting Divergence at "Naql" Logistics¶

    Field Value
    Case ID CS-01
    Module M1 — Evolution of Data Architecture
    Day / Hour Day 1 · Hour 3 (after the warehouse → lake → lakehouse timeline, before LAB-01)
    Duration 45 minutes
    Format Table discussion
    Group size 4–5 per table; 3–6 tables
    Deliverable One flipchart per table: the single conformed definition of "on-time", the layer it lives in, and a three-step cutover plan with the reconciliation evidence the CFO will be shown
    Materials Evidence pack §5 printed one copy per table (5 pages); flipchart + markers; the medallion diagram on the wall
    Facilitator prep 10 min. Read §7 model answers. Decide in advance which table you will push on Q3 — that is where the case earns its time

    1. Scenario¶

    "Naql" (نقل) is a Saudi last-mile logistics firm operating in Riyadh, Jeddah and Dammam. Over six years it accumulated two data platforms:

    • a data lake — Spark on object storage, owned by the data-science group, used to train the delivery on-time model and to run ad-hoc analysis;
    • a cloud data warehouse — owned by the BI group, loaded nightly by a copy job from the lake plus two direct source extracts.

    For the week of 1–7 June 2026 the CEO dashboard shows 87.0% on-time deliveries. The data-science on-time model's evaluation report, for the same week and the same fleet, shows 91.2%.

    Neither team can explain the other's number. The COO has stopped quoting either one in the weekly operations review. An executive has asked, in writing, "which of our two data teams should we keep?"

    2. Business context¶

    Item Figure
    Deliveries in the disputed week 1,184,000
    Contractual SLA threshold with the three largest B2B customers 90.0% on-time
    Driver incentive scheme under review, annualised SAR 4,000,000
    Time the incentive decision has been frozen 1 month (and counting)
    SLA penalty exposure if the true figure is below 90% SAR 1,150,000 per quarter across three contracts
    Cost of the nightly warehouse copy job (compute + egress) SAR 46,000 / month

    The 4.2-point gap is not academic. At 87.0% Naql is in breach of all three SLAs and owes penalties; at 91.2% it is compliant and the incentive scheme is justified. The same week of the same business is simultaneously a breach and a success, depending on which system you query. That is the two-tier problem stated in money.

    3. Technical challenge¶

    Two independently maintained pipelines compute the same business metric from the same underlying events and disagree. The divergence is not a bug in either system — each is internally consistent and each passes its own tests. It is a definitional divergence that has hardened into two codebases.

    Three specific mechanisms are in play, and the teams must find all three from the evidence:

    1. Late-arriving proof-of-delivery events. The warehouse pipeline computes on-time at 02:00 for the previous calendar day and never revisits it. The lake pipeline recomputes a rolling 7-day window every run, so it absorbs POD events that arrive up to 72 hours late.
    2. Timezone handling. Source events are UTC. The warehouse load applies +03 at ingest and stores local timestamps; the lake stores UTC and converts at presentation. Deliveries between 21:00 and 24:00 UTC fall on different calendar days in the two systems.
    3. Cancellation semantics. The warehouse counts cancelled_by_customer deliveries in the denominator; the lake excludes them.

    4. Constraints¶

    • Neither system can be frozen. BI runs the daily operations review; the lake runs three production models.
    • The regulator (and the three B2B contracts) require a single auditable definition of on-time, with the ability to reproduce any historical figure on demand.
    • Arabic and English address fields (district_name_ar, district_name_en) must survive any consolidation byte-exact — a previous migration mangled Arabic to ???? and cost the firm a customer.
    • Migration budget: one quarter, two engineers.
    • No new licence spend approved.

    5. Evidence pack¶

    Hand out all five pages at minute 4. Do not narrate them — let teams find the divergence themselves. Page 5 is the trap.

    P1 — Executive dashboard tile (warehouse), week 2026-06-01 → 2026-06-07¶

    ON-TIME DELIVERY RATE                       Source: dw.fact_delivery_daily
                                            Refreshed: 2026-06-08 02:14 +03
        87.0%                               Deliveries: 1,184,003
        ▼ 1.4 pts vs prior week             On-time:    1,030,083
                                            SLA floor:  90.0%   ✖ BREACH

    P2 — Data-science model evaluation report (lake), same week¶

    on_time_rate.......... 0.9118
n_deliveries.......... 1,127,455

    Note the row counts: 1,184,003 vs 1,127,455 — a difference of 56,548 deliveries. The teams that spot this in the first five minutes are the teams that solve the case.

    P3 — The two SQL definitions, side by side¶

    Warehouse (dw_load_delivery_daily.sql, last modified 2023-11-02):

    SELECT
  delivery_date,
  COUNT(*)                                              AS deliveries,
  SUM(CASE WHEN pod_ts <= promised_ts THEN 1 ELSE 0 END) AS on_time
FROM stg_delivery
WHERE delivery_date = DATEADD(day, -1, CURRENT_DATE)      -- yesterday, once, forever
GROUP BY delivery_date;
-- stg_delivery.pod_ts was written as CONVERT_TZ(src.pod_ts_utc,'UTC','Asia/Riyadh')
-- status filter: none

    Lake (ontime_features.py, last modified 2026-04-19):

    w = spark.read.format("delta").load("s3://naql-lake/curated/deliveries")
w = (w.filter(F.col("pod_ts_utc").between(start_utc, end_utc))
       .filter(~F.col("status").isin("cancelled_by_customer", "cancelled_by_merchant")))
rate = w.agg(F.avg((F.col("pod_ts_utc") <= F.col("promised_ts_utc")).cast("int"))).first()[0]
# rolling rebuild: the last 7 days are recomputed on every run

    P4 — Late POD arrival profile (from the lake's ingestion log)¶

    Lag between physical delivery and POD event landing Share of deliveries
    < 1 hour 94.1%
    1–6 hours 3.6%
    6–24 hours 1.5%
    24–72 hours 0.7%
    > 72 hours 0.1%

    At 1,184,003 deliveries/week, the 2.3% arriving after 6 hours is 27,232 deliveries the warehouse's 02:00 job never sees for the day it belongs to. Their on-time rate is materially worse than the fleet average (they are late partly because something went wrong), which is why the warehouse number is not simply noisier — it is biased.

    P5 — Row-count reconciliation, prepared by an intern the day before the session¶

    Filter applied Row count
    All delivery rows, week of 2026-06-01, UTC boundaries 1,127,455
    All delivery rows, week of 2026-06-01, +03 boundaries 1,131,090
    ... plus cancelled_by_customer and cancelled_by_merchant 1,187,638
    Warehouse dw.fact_delivery_daily sum for the week 1,184,003

    The residual 3,635 rows between the third line and the warehouse figure are the late PODs that arrived after each nightly job ran. Every part of the 4.2-point gap is explained by this table. Teams that build this table themselves have solved the case; do not hand them the interpretation.

    6. Facilitation script (minute-by-minute)¶

    Min Activity Facilitator does Watch for
    0–3 Frame. Read the scenario aloud. Put the two numbers on the board: 87.0% and 91.2%. Ask only: "Which one do we send to the regulator?" Do not answer. Let the silence sit. Someone will say "average them." Write it on the board. You will return to it in the debrief.
    3–5 Distribute the evidence pack, one per table. State the deliverable and the clock. Circulate; make sure every table has all 5 pages. Tables that start reading P1 first and never reach P5. Nudge: "P5 was made by an intern. Is it useful?"
    5–17 Round 1 — Find the gap. Tables work to enumerate every mechanism producing divergence. Visit each table once. Ask one question and leave: "You have three mechanisms — are you sure there isn't a fourth?" Teams that stop at timezone. Timezone is the smallest of the three effects (3,635 rows); late arrivals and the cancellation filter are much larger.
    17–20 Fast round-robin. Each table names one mechanism, no repeats. Build the master list on the board. Enforce "no repeats" strictly — it forces later tables to find the subtle ones. Aim to have the board read: late arrivals · timezone · cancellation semantics · (bonus) no reconciliation ever run · (bonus) two owners of one definition.
    20–33 Round 2 — Design the fix. Tables produce the flipchart deliverable: the one conformed definition, the layer it lives in, the three-step cutover, and the CFO evidence. Push hard on layer. If a table writes the definition into a BI tool's semantic layer, ask: "Who computes it for the model, then?" The most common wrong turn is "put the definition in the BI tool." That recreates the divergence with extra steps.
    33–41 Present. Two tables present for 3 minutes each; the rest add deltas only. Pick one strong table and one that made an instructive mistake. Present the mistake second and repair it live. Keep to time. Cut the third presenter.
    41–45 Debrief. Deliver §10. Close on §11 verbatim. Erase "average them" from the board deliberately, out loud. —

    7. Discussion questions with model answers¶

    Q1. Why does having two pipelines almost guarantee eventual divergence?

    Model answer. Because a business definition is not a constant — it is a series of decisions ("do cancellations count?", "how late is too late?", "which day does a 23:40 UTC delivery belong to?"), and each pipeline makes those decisions independently, at a different time, under a different owner, in response to a different request. Divergence is not caused by a defect; it is caused by two independent decision-making processes applied to the same question. The rate of divergence is the rate at which the business changes its mind, which is never zero. A second pipeline is therefore not a redundancy — it is a guarantee of eventual disagreement, with the disagreement discovered at the worst possible moment (an SLA dispute, an audit, a board meeting).

    Push further: ask whether tests would have prevented it. They would not — each pipeline passes its own tests. Only a reconciliation between the two, run continuously, would have caught it, and nobody owns that because it belongs to neither team.

    Q2. Which of the four two-tier costs — duplication, staleness, skew, governance — is most expensive here, and why?

    Model answer. Skew, by a wide margin, and the money proves it: the SAR 4M incentive decision and SAR 1.15M/quarter of SLA exposure both hinge on which number is true. Duplication costs SAR 46k/month for the copy job (real, but an order of magnitude smaller). Staleness costs a day of decision latency (annoying, survivable). Governance is the latent worst: Naql cannot presently answer "what was our on-time rate on 3 June, and prove it" for a regulator, which is an unbounded liability — but it has not yet been called.

    The pedagogical point: the cheap-to-measure cost (duplication, SAR 46k) is the one organisations optimise, and the expensive one (skew) is the one they discover in a crisis. Accept "governance" as an answer if the team argues the unbounded-liability case well.

    Q3. How would you prove the consolidated number is correct to a sceptical CFO?

    Model answer. Not by asserting it. By producing three artefacts:

    1. A reconciliation report that starts from each of the two legacy numbers and walks, line by line, to the new one — exactly the shape of evidence page P5, but signed and repeatable. 87.0% → +2.6 pts (late PODs now absorbed) → +1.9 pts (cancellations excluded per contract wording) → −0.3 pts (UTC day boundary) → 91.2%. A CFO does not need to understand Spark; a CFO needs to see that every point of the gap is accounted for and each step cites the contract clause or engineering rule that justifies it.
    2. One reviewed definition in one place — silver.deliveries plus a single is_on_time derivation, code-reviewed, with the contract clause quoted in the model's description, versioned in git.
    3. Reproducibility on demand — time travel to the version of the table as of any filing date (this foreshadows M4 and CS-04), so that "what did we report on 8 June and why" is a query, not an archaeology project.

    Add the human step: run both old pipelines and the new one in parallel for four weeks and publish the daily delta before cutover. Trust is rebuilt by a boring, unbroken sequence of matching numbers, not by a convincing architecture diagram.

    Q4. What would you keep the warehouse for, if anything, after consolidation?

    Model answer. Legitimate residual reasons: (a) an existing BI tool with a deep licence commitment and hundreds of built reports, where the migration cost exceeds the benefit this quarter; (b) very high-concurrency, low-latency serving of a small curated mart where a specialised engine genuinely outperforms; (c) a regulatory requirement for a specific certified platform.

    The illegitimate reason — and the one Naql actually has — is "the BI team is used to it." The test to apply: does anything downstream of the warehouse compute a business definition, or does it only render one computed upstream? If it computes, the two-tier problem is intact and the divergence will return. If it only renders — a thin, scheduled, column-for-column copy of gold.fact_trip-equivalent marts with no logic — the warehouse is a cache, and caches are fine. Make teams state the cache invariant: no transformation, no filter, no CASE statement in the copy job. Anything else is a second pipeline wearing a disguise.

    Q5 (reserve, use if a table finishes early). The lake team's number is the more correct one. Should the data-science team therefore own the consolidated definition?

    Model answer. No — and this is the trap. Being right this time is not the same as being the right owner. The definition of "on-time" is a commercial definition, written in a customer contract; its owner is the commercial/operations function, with engineering as the implementer and both BI and DS as consumers. Ownership by either consumer reproduces the original failure with a different winner. The architecture must make the definition ownable by a non-engineer: one named table, one named column, one description field quoting the contract clause, one review process.

    8. Solution approach¶

    Target state. A single Lakehouse; both consumers read the same conformed layer.

    Sources ──► bronze.deliveries        (immutable, append-only, _ingested_at/_source_file/_batch_id)
   POD     bronze.pod_events          raw, no logic, nothing dropped, Arabic preserved byte-exact
   WMS         │
               ▼
          silver.deliveries          ONE conformed table. UTC storage. ONE is_on_time rule.
               │                     3-day lookback MERGE on delivery_id absorbs late PODs.
               ▼
          gold.delivery_daily  ──┬──► BI dashboard   (renders; computes nothing)
                                 └──► on-time model  (trains; computes nothing)

    Migration sequence (the deliverable teams should produce).

    1. Land both feeds into bronze, unchanged. Do not fix anything here. This is the step that makes every later step provable, because bronze is what both legacy pipelines also read from. Arabic fields land as-is; a byte-comparison test against source is part of the definition of done.
    2. Write one silver.deliveries with one reviewed rule set. UTC storage; is_on_time = pod_ts_utc <= promised_ts_utc; exclusion of cancelled_by_customer and cancelled_by_merchant (justified by quoting the contract clause in the model description); incremental MERGE on delivery_id with a 3-day lookback so the 2.3% of late PODs land in the day they belong to.
    3. Run the reconciliation in parallel for four weeks. Publish the daily three-way delta (warehouse, lake, silver). Expect the silver number to sit at ~91.2% and the deltas to be stable and explainable. Do not cut over on a good week; cut over on four boring weeks.
    4. Repoint both consumers at gold. The dashboard reads gold.delivery_daily. The model reads the same table. Neither contains a CASE WHEN.
    5. Retire the copy job. Only after step 4 has run for two weeks. Save the SAR 46k/month and, more importantly, remove the second decision-making process.
    6. Institutionalise. Every regulatory or contractual filing pins the table version used (foreshadow M4). A reconciliation test runs nightly and pages someone if BI and model inputs ever differ by more than zero.

    Why the ordering matters. Teams often want to start at step 2. Starting at step 1 is what makes step 3 possible — you cannot reconcile three numbers if only two of them are derived from a common, immutable record.

    9. What good looks like vs common wrong turns¶

    What good looks like Common wrong turn
    Finds all three divergence mechanisms and quantifies each against P5 Finds timezone, declares victory. Timezone is 3,635 rows of a 56,548-row gap
    Puts the definition in silver and treats BI + model as equal downstream consumers Puts the definition in the BI semantic layer, leaving the model to re-implement it — the original bug, relocated
    Proposes a parallel-run reconciliation before cutover Proposes a big-bang cutover with a "validation script" run once
    Names a business owner for the definition, not an engineering owner Assigns ownership to whichever team was right this time
    Keeps the warehouse only as a logic-free cache, with the cache invariant written down Keeps the warehouse "for performance" with no invariant, and the copy job quietly regrows logic
    Treats immutable bronze as the precondition for provability Fixes data on the way into bronze, destroying the ability to reconcile against the legacy numbers
    States what happens to the model when the definition changes (retrain, re-baseline) Discusses only the dashboard; forgets the model was trained on the other definition and its labels are now stale
    Handles Arabic explicitly (byte-exact test in the definition of done) Never mentions the Arabic constraint, which is how the previous migration failed
    Averages nothing "Report 89% while we investigate"

    10. Debrief (4 minutes)¶

    Deliver these four beats in order.

    1. Nothing was broken. Point at P3. Both queries are correct implementations of a defensible definition. Every test in both systems is green. The failure is architectural, not defective — which is why no amount of testing inside either pipeline would have found it.
    2. The gap was entirely explainable, and nobody had explained it. P5 took an intern one afternoon. It had never been run in six years, because reconciliation between two platforms belongs to neither platform's owner. Work that belongs to no one does not get done.
    3. This is the two-tier problem, and it is why the Lakehouse exists. Not because object storage is cheap or because open formats are pleasant — but because a single copy of conformed data with a single place to encode a business rule is the only architecture in which a company can answer "what is our on-time rate" with one number.
    4. Connect to Masar and to the course question. For the next five days you build one Lakehouse for Masar, in which gold.fact_trip serves BI and gold.eta_features serves AI, and both descend from one silver.trips. Ask the room the unifying question with this case's specifics: "What happens to Masar's ETA model if the definition of a completed trip diverges between BI and the feature pipeline?" Answer: the model trains on labels the business does not recognise, and no dashboard will ever show it.

    Then erase "average them" from the board, out loud, and say why: averaging two numbers you cannot explain produces a third number you cannot explain.

    11. The one sentence participants should leave with¶

    Two pipelines computing the same business metric is not redundancy — it is a guarantee of eventual disagreement, discovered at the worst possible moment; the Lakehouse exists so that a definition lives in exactly one place and every consumer reads it, rather than recomputing it.
