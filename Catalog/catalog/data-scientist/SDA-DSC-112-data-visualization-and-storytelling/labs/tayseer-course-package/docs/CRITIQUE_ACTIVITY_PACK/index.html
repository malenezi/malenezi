    Critique and Activity Pack¶

    حزمة النقد والأنشطة¶

    Facilitator's full script for the critique thread — SDA-DSC-112, Data Visualization and Storytelling

    What this document is¶

    The deck carries the critique thread as fifteen slides — a canon overview, the six-question protocol, ten canonical works and three activity cards — plus two more this pack also scripts: the FT Visual Vocabulary (slide 59) and Sequencing as argument (slide 135). Those slides are the version that fits on a screen. This is the version with the detail that does not — what a good answer to each critique question actually sounds like, the three or four facts about each canonical work that an instructor must not get wrong, the minute-by-minute run sheets for the four activities, and the ways each one fails in a real room.

    Every historical claim below traces to research/famous_viz_verified.md; every Tayseer figure traces to data/instructional/critique_canon.csv, data/instructional/kpi_spin_pairs.csv, data/supporting/regional_equity_latest.csv or docs/validation_report.md. Where the research flagged a claim as unverified, the hedge is in the text and is not optional — say it the way it is written or do not say it.

    Part 1 — The six-question critique protocol¶

    The protocol exists to stop critique collapsing into taste. The first three questions are answerable from the chart itself and have right answers; the last three require judgement, and only the last one is allowed to mention preference. Run it in order. The order is the discipline — a room allowed to jump to question five will produce "it's cluttered" and nothing else.

    Establish the house rule on Day 1 Hour 1, before any content: critique the chart, never the person, and every criticism arrives with a proposed fix. The first critique of the course is run by the instructor, out loud, at this pace — two minutes of silent looking, the six questions, ninety seconds of open floor. After that, participants run it in pairs.

    Question 1 — What is the claim?¶

    Say the chart's argument in one sentence. If nobody can, that is the finding: the chart has no argument, only data.
    A good answer sounds like "It claims that deaths cluster around one pump, and therefore that the water carried the cholera." — a proposition that could be false.
    A bad answer sounds like "It's about cholera deaths in London." — a topic, not a claim. Nothing in that sentence could turn out to be wrong.

    Failure mode A — the topic sentence. The room describes the subject and stops. Recovery: ask "and therefore?" until a verb of consequence appears. If none does after two attempts, the chart genuinely has no argument, and the room has found its first defect.

    Failure mode B — the claim is imported from the caption. Somebody reads the title back. Recovery: cover the title and ask again. A claim the reader can only get from the words is a claim the chart is not making.

    Question 2 — What is encoded, and in what channel?¶

    List every variable and the channel carrying it. Rank each channel against the Cleveland–McGill hierarchy from Module 1. The weakest channel carrying the most important variable is usually the defect.
    A good answer sounds like "Troop strength is band width — that is length, rank 3. Direction is hue, which is categorical and fine. Temperature is position on a common scale, rank 1, and it is the subordinate variable. The most important quantity is on the third-best channel."
    A bad answer sounds like "It uses a lot of colours and it's confusing." — a reaction, not an inventory.

    Failure mode A — "it's confusing" as an answer. Recovery: refuse it politely and ask for a list. "Name the variables first. How many are there?" Counting forces the inventory, and the inventory produces the finding.

    Failure mode B — the inventory stops at the marks. Participants list bars and lines but miss the channels doing quiet work: sort order, panel order, aspect ratio, what is grey. Recovery: ask "what is encoded by the order of these?" This is the single most productive prompt in the protocol.

    Question 3 — What is the denominator?¶

    Counts or rates? Per what? This is the question that produces the most findings per second of class time, and it is the one that transfers most directly to government reporting.
    A good answer sounds like "There is no denominator. It is a raw count of deaths per address, and roughly three quarters of the residents had left, so the population under those marks was moving while the map was being made."
    A bad answer sounds like "It's per region." — per region what? Per resident, per household, per user, per transaction, per branch? Each gives a different ranking.

    Failure mode A — the denominator is assumed to exist. Modern audiences assume normalisation has happened because dashboards usually show percentages. Recovery: ask what the number would be if the population doubled. If the mark would double too, it is a count.

    Failure mode B — the right denominator is asserted rather than argued. Recovery: make them defend it against one alternative. Per capita and per user give different answers for Tayseer, and which one is right depends on whether the question is about coverage or about behaviour.

    Question 4 — What would change the reader's mind?¶

    If the conclusion survives any plausible alternative encoding, the chart is honest. If it works only on this axis range, this window, this filter, it is not.
    A good answer sounds like "Redraw it with the baseline at zero and the effect shrinks from an apparent 128.7% to an actual 3.5%. The conclusion does not survive the alternative, so the axis is doing the arguing."
    A bad answer sounds like "Nothing, it's clear." — untested. Clarity is not robustness.

    Failure mode A — the room tests style, not structure. They propose changing colours. Recovery: restrict the allowed changes to the four that matter — baseline, window, denominator, aggregation level — and ask which of those four the conclusion survives.

    Failure mode B — the test is run in the head and reported as a result. Recovery: make somebody actually redraw it. Three of the four activities in Part 3 exist because this question is only answered by building.

    Question 5 — What does it hide?¶

    Aggregation, the baseline, the excluded segment, the cherry-picked window. Name the specific omission. "It oversimplifies" is not an answer.
    A good answer sounds like "A national 63.8% hides that nine of thirteen regions are below target and five have not moved in nine months. The distribution is not a supporting detail here; for this decision it is the finding."
    A bad answer sounds like "It leaves out context." — which context, and what would it change?

    Failure mode A — speculative hiding. Participants invent omissions the chart could not plausibly contain. Recovery: require that the omission be checkable — name the file, the column, the filter. Every one of the thirteen techniques in kpi_spin_pairs.csv names the operation that produces it, precisely so the accusation can be checked.

    Failure mode B — the room finds one omission and stops. Recovery: ask for the omission at the other end. If they have found a hidden weakness, ask what hidden strength the same aggregation buries. Five of the thirteen spin techniques have a negative gap — the wrong method understates the result — which is the argument against treating this as a list of tricks.

    Question 6 — What would you steal?¶

    End constructively and concretely: one technique you will use on the Tayseer data this week.
    A good answer sounds like "The doubling-time diagonals. I will put a target-slope reference line on the regional trend so the committee can see which regions are on a path to 65% rather than just where they are."
    A bad answer sounds like "It's inspiring." — nothing has been taken.

    Failure mode A — the theft is not portable. "I'd use a polar-area diagram." Recovery: ask which Tayseer question it answers, and against which alternative encoding. Usually the answer is none, and that is a useful discovery.

    Failure mode B — nobody answers, because the group has spent its energy on attack. Recovery: this is the failure that kills critique culture by Day 2. Protect question 6 by time-boxing questions 1–5 and reserving the last ninety seconds for it. A critique culture that never produces a usable technique dies.

    Part 2 — Where each canonical work lands¶

    Thirteen slides, spread across all six modules, each arriving at the moment its lesson is needed. For each work: the one-line setup the instructor says, the facts to get right, the flaw that must be named, the questions to put to the room, and the bridge to the Tayseer data.

    A note on numbering. The deck's ten canon slides and the ten rows of critique_canon.csv are the same ten works, but two rows are arranged differently. CAN-07 ("How the Virus Got Out") has no numbered canon slide — it carries the Sequencing as argument slide in Module 5. CAN-08 covers both NYT pieces in one row, and the deck splits them: the front page lands in Module 3 as Canon 6, the excess-deaths chart in Module 5 as Canon 8. The CSV's module_used_in column also differs from the deck's placement for four rows; the deck is the authority for where a work is taught, the CSV for what is said about it.

    Module 1 — Perception and encoding¶

    Canon 1 — Nightingale, 1858 · slide 35 · canon_03_nightingale.png¶

    Setup line. "This is the most famous statistical graphic ever made by a nurse, and almost every course that teaches it teaches the criticism backwards."

    Facts to get right.

    Fact Detail
    Title and publication Diagram of the Causes of Mortality in the Army in the East, in Notes on Matters Affecting the Health, Efficiency, and Hospital Administration of the British Army, privately printed 1858
    Structure Two circles of twelve equal-angle sectors: right-hand April 1854 – March 1855, left-hand April 1855 – March 1856. The split at March 1855 is the arrival of the Sanitary Commission at Scutari, and it is the whole argument
    Colours Blue = preventible or mitigable zymotic disease; red = wounds; black = all other causes
    The headline number January 1855, disease mortality at an annualised 1,022.8 per 1,000 — an annual rate above 100% of the force
    The encoding Wedges are superimposed from a common vertex at the centre, not stacked; area is proportional to rate, radius to √rate

    The flaw that must be named. Not the one the room expects. The famous rose is correctly area-encoded — Nightingale identified the radius error in her earlier 1858 "bat's wing", issued an erratum slip, and replaced it with the wedges; her own printed legend says the areas are what is measured. The legitimate critique is that even correct area encoding sits low on the decoding-accuracy hierarchy, readers tend to read radial extent, and so differences are perceptually exaggerated at the extremes. Second flaw: the superimposition is ambiguous without the legend, and most modern recreations read the wedges cumulatively and thereby overstate total mortality.

    Hedge. The link from the rose to the Royal Commission and the 1860 Army Medical School is well attested in secondary literature but no primary document attributing a specific reform to the diagram was verified. Say "the diagram was the persuasive centrepiece of her lobbying"; do not say "the rose caused the reform". Hugh Small argues her plain Lines diagram was probably the more influential of the two, and that is worth saying out loud — she made both charts, the persuasive one and the accurate one.

    Questions to the room. Redraw one circle as a line chart of the same rates: which reading task gets easier, and which gets harder? · The wedges share a vertex rather than stacking: what does a reader who assumes stacking conclude, and by how much are they wrong?

    Bridge to Tayseer. "Our gauge wall lost to a sorted bar chart in the Module 4 usability benchmark for exactly this reason — 72.2 seconds down to 11.2, 24% correct up to 86%. Rhetorical force and decoding accuracy are different objectives. Choose one deliberately."

    Canon 2 — Minard, 1869 · slide 36 · canon_02_minard.png¶

    Setup line. "Tufte called this the best statistical graphic ever drawn. We are going to use it twice in five minutes — once as the high-water mark, once as a warning."

    Facts to get right.

    Fact Detail
    Object Lithograph, 62 × 30 cm, Paris, dated 20 November 1869 — one of Minard's last works
    Title Carte figurative des pertes successives en hommes de l'Armée Française dans la campagne de Russie 1812–1813
    Six variables Troop number as band width (Minard's own note: one millimetre for ten thousand men), longitude, latitude, direction as colour (tan = advance, black = retreat), position relative to dates, temperature on the retreat
    The arithmetic that closes 340,000 + 60,000 + 22,000 = 422,000 crossing the Niemen; 4,000 + 6,000 = 10,000 returning
    Temperature scale Degrees Réaumur, not Celsius: °C = °Ré × 1.25. The true minimum is −37.5 °C (−35.5 °F), not −30 °C

    The flaw that must be named. Four, in this order. The retreat leg is deliberately drifted south so it does not overlap the advance — Minard called his work cartes figuratives et approximatives and explicitly rejected the "tyranny of precise geographical position". The straight linear taper between Vilnius and Vitebsk hides roughly 165,000 deaths on that leg, more than the entire retreat. The band is non-monotonic near longitude 26.4, rising from 12,000 to 14,000 — the "monotonic loss" reading cannot accommodate it. And the Réaumur relabelling: almost every popular reproduction silently prints Celsius on that axis and understates the cold by 25%.

    Hedge. Cite HistData::Minard.troops as "the standard tabulated transcription" of Minard's drawn band widths, not as Minard's manuscript figures — transcriptions differ slightly across sources. The arithmetic reconciliation to 422,000 and 10,000 was verified against that transcription.

    Questions to the room. The band width is monotonic except at one point — find it, and decide whether the exception is data or drafting. · Convert the temperature axis from Réaumur to Celsius: how much colder does the retreat become, and what does that do to the story?

    Bridge to Tayseer. "Interpolating between two reporting points can erase the worst period entirely. Plot every one of our sixty months; never draw a straight line between the endpoints. And before you leave this slide — what are the units on your dashboard's cost measure, and who last checked?"

    Module 2 — Chart choice and scale choice¶

    Canon 3 — Snow, 1854 · slide 58 · canon_01_snow.png¶

    Setup line. "Taught everywhere as the founding act of data visualisation and of epidemiology. Both halves of that sentence need qualifying, and the qualifications are the lesson."

    Facts to get right.

    Fact Detail
    Creator and medium John Snow, physician; the dot map was drawn by engraver Charles Cheffins. First shown at the London Epidemiological Society, December 1854 — after the outbreak ended. Published 1855
    The outbreak Onset 31 August 1854; 127 deaths in the first three days; over 500 by 10 September; 616 deaths in total; 12.8 per 1,000 in the affected area
    The handle Removed 8 September 1854 by the St James parish vestry, after Snow petitioned them on 7 September
    The timing that matters Snow's own daily table: attacks peaked at 143 on 1 September and had fallen to 12 on 8 September, the day the handle came off
    The anomalies 61 deaths confirmed by Snow as pump-water drinkers; the Broad Street brewery workers had a beer allowance and none died; the workhouse had its own well
    The isoline A dotted line of equal walking distance by the nearest road to the pump — a network distance boundary, not a Voronoi cell. The strict Voronoi version is a later analytic reconstruction

    The flaw that must be named. It is a raw-count map with no population denominator at all, and roughly three quarters of the residents had fled within the following week, so the denominator moved under it. The stacked bars conflate "a death" with "a death at an address", inflating apparent density at large tenements — the modern digitisation makes this explicit: 578 fatalities resolve to 321 unique addresses. The map was made after the fact and played no role in persuading the vestry; it is a rhetorical and confirmatory graphic, not a discovery tool. Snow himself wrote that the attacks had so far diminished before the water was stopped that it was impossible to decide whether the well was still infective. And his stronger evidence was the Southwark & Vauxhall versus Lambeth water-company comparison, not this map.

    Hedge. Do not assert that the walking-distance map appeared in the second edition of Snow's book — sources differ on whether the 1855 vestry report is dated 1854 or 1855 and on whether the same map appears in the book. Three figures that circulate widely could not be verified and must not be said: "83 deaths in the week ending 2 September, 73 near the pump", "535 workhouse inmates, 5 deaths", "70 brewery workers". Say "the workhouse had its own well and the brewery workers had a beer allowance" without numbers.

    Questions to the room. What is the denominator on this map, and what happens to the argument once you learn that three quarters of the residents had left? · Snow's dotted line is a walking-distance boundary, not a Voronoi cell — why is that distinction worth a slide?

    Bridge to Tayseer. "Our raw digital-transaction choropleth has exactly this defect: it finds Riyadh and Makkah because they are large. Jazan is seventh of thirteen by raw digital volume and thirteenth per capita. The denominator problem in an 1854 map is the denominator problem in a 2026 regional performance map, and Case C is where we fix it."

    The FT Visual Vocabulary · slide 59¶

    Setup line. "You do not choose a chart. You choose the relationship you need to show, and the chart follows."

    Facts to get right. A one-page poster by the FT Visual Journalism Team, mapping the relationship you want to show onto the chart forms that show it. Nine categories: deviation, correlation, ranking, distribution, change over time, part-to-whole, magnitude, spatial, flow. English, Japanese, Traditional Chinese and Simplified Chinese versions exist, plus an interactive web version and an FT4Schools variant. Source: github.com/Financial-Times/chart-doctor/tree/main/visual-vocabulary.

    The constraint that must be named. Copyright © The Financial Times Limited, all rights reserved. It is not open-licensed. Link to it; do not reproduce it in handouts or slides without clearance from FT syndication. The EU Publications Office maintains a separately-licensed derivative at data.europa.eu/apps/data-visualisation-guide/visual-vocabulary, which is the one to put in a printed pack.

    The three-minute exercise that makes it stick. Read out the five Lab 2 questions from lab2_questions.csv and have the room call out the category before anyone names a chart. They will be right about the category and wrong about the chart, which is the point — the category constrains the chart, and the data shape then refines it.

    Bridge to Tayseer. "Take three Tayseer questions to the nine categories. Does the category the question falls into match the chart we shipped?"

    Canon 4 — Financial Times, 2020 · slide 60 · canon_08_ft_log.png¶

    Setup line. "The same chart, well designed, is correct or misleading depending on which question the reader brought. This is the cleanest example in the canon."

    Facts to get right.

    Fact Detail
    Design x = days since the country's 100th confirmed case; y = cumulative cases or deaths on a log scale; diagonal doubling-time reference lines; direct line labels, no legend; East Asian countries blue, Italy black, everything else grey; narrative headline. Built in R with ggplot2
    First published 11 March 2020, by John Burn-Murdoch; prototyped the previous day
    Why log was right On a log scale the slope is the growth rate, so the reading task becomes comparing slopes against the doubling-time guides — the epidemiologically meaningful comparison. On a linear scale every country not yet in crisis is squashed into the bottom-left corner and invisible
    Why the alignment was right Aligning on "days since the 100th case" removes the confound of when an epidemic started, so how fast is comparable

    The flaw that must be named. Romano, Sotis, Dominioni & Guidi (Health Economics, 2020, 29(11), 1482–1494) found that showing COVID deaths on a logarithmic scale left people with a less accurate understanding of how the pandemic had developed, produced less accurate predictions of its trajectory, and shifted attitudes and policy preferences. They recommend a linear scale as the default for general-audience communication. Secondary criticism Burn-Murdoch answered repeatedly: daily wiggles reflect reporting schedules, not transmission — which is why seven-day rolling averages followed.

    Hedge. Quote the finding and the recommendation; do not quote a sample size or an effect size — the research could not retrieve them. The 11 March launch date comes from Burn-Murdoch's own account via a secondary profile and was not verified against the original FT page or tweet timestamp; say "first published 11 March 2020, on his own account of it".

    Do not resolve this into a rule. The honest lesson is that the question comes before the scale.

    Questions to the room. Redraw one trajectory chart on a linear axis: which reader is now better served, and which is worse? · Which scale does the Tayseer steering committee need — and justify it from the committee's decision, not from your preference.

    Bridge to Tayseer. "Decide whether the tile serves the analyst comparing rates or the committee judging magnitude, and say which on the tile."

    Module 3 — Colour, annotation, restraint¶

    Canon 5 — Our World in Data · slide 84 · canon_05_owid.png¶

    Setup line. "Identical series, identical month, identical source. Nothing was added to the data between these two panels — only design."

    Facts to get right.

    Fact Detail
    What it is A free, non-profit web publication founded May 2013 by Max Roser; a collaboration between researchers at the University of Oxford and the Global Change Data Lab, a registered charity in England and Wales, no. 1186433, which owns and maintains the site
    Grapher The in-house chart engine. Every chart is an interactive object with switchable chart types, a country selector, a time slider, a download tab for data and image, and an embed code
    The four design moves Legend replaced by direct labels at the line ends; every series but one muted to grey; the argument written on the chart as a sentence; source and licence stated on the chart itself
    Exemplars worth opening live Life expectancy vs CO₂ per capita (the Rosling scatter rebuilt static-first) and the CO₂ per-capita Marimekko, where width = population, height = per-capita emissions and area = total emissions

    The flaw that must be named. Two licensing traps rather than a design flaw. OWID's own content, charts and data are CC BY — reproduce them with attribution. But third-party data carried inside a Grapher chart keeps the third party's licence, so checking the "Learn more about this data" panel is a required step, not a courtesy. And the Grapher source code is no longer MIT: the current licence permits viewing and studying the code only and forbids copying, modifying, distributing or adapting it without written permission. Wikipedia and most secondary sources still say MIT and are out of date. Do not tell a class they can fork Grapher.

    Hedge. The licence change is confirmed from the current LICENSE.md; the date it changed is unverified. Say "the current licence", not "since 2024".

    Questions to the room. Open the Marimekko — which of the three quantities encoded there can you actually read off, and which are you estimating? · A chart you embed is CC BY but its underlying series is not: what must appear on the slide?

    Bridge to Tayseer. "Our Tayseer tiles should carry the same three things an OWID chart does: the measure definition, the source file, and the download. That is what makes a dashboard auditable rather than merely attractive. A chart that travels — and they all travel, screenshotted into other people's decks — must carry its own provenance, because the slide around it will not survive."

    Canon 6 — New York Times, 24 May 2020 · slide 85 · canon_10_incalculable_loss.png¶

    Setup line. "The most disciplined choice on this list is the one where a graphics department decided not to make a chart."

    Facts to get right.

    Fact Detail
    The object U.S. Deaths Near 100,000, An Incalculable Loss — the front page of 24 May 2020. No chart at all: 1,000 names and one-line fragments of obituary, set as continuous type filling the page
    The team Led by Simone Landon, assistant graphics editor; names compiled from online obituaries by Alain Delaquérière; print art direction by Andrew Sondern
    Stated intent To convey the vastness and variety of the tragedies by personalising them, countering data fatigue
    Afterlife Ranked 19th in Ad Age's 30 Best Creative Brand Moves of 2020; acquired by Princeton University Library's Graphic Arts Collection

    The flaw that must be named. The pre-publication tweet was later deleted after one listed person turned out to have died by homicide. Compiling from online obituaries at speed has a failure mode, and it is a sourcing failure, not a design one — which is worth saying to a room that will be tempted to treat the piece as beyond criticism.

    Note on the figure. The right-hand panel of canon_10_incalculable_loss.png is an illustrative mock-up in the spirit of the front page. The phrases are invented and no real person is represented. Say this when the slide goes up.

    Questions to the room. Is there a point in the Tayseer story where the right answer is not a chart? Defend it. · Pair it with the excess-deaths chart two modules later: same event, one rendered as a quantity and one as a thousand people. Which job are you doing?

    Bridge to Tayseer. "A slide of beneficiary counts is a slide about people who could not complete a service. Aggregation is the right tool for a budget decision and the wrong tool for a mandate. Participants should leave able to say, in a real meeting, 'this one should not be a chart' — and be right."

    Module 4 — Interaction and the dashboard¶

    Canon 7 — Gapminder, 2006 · slide 108 · canon_04_gapminder.png¶

    Setup line. "The best-known data visualisation of the century, and it is a presentation artefact. Its attention is carried by a narrator."

    Facts to get right.

    Fact Detail
    Provenance Hans Rosling (1948–2017) with Ola Rosling and Anna Rosling Rönnlund; Gapminder Foundation founded 2005, Stockholm. The 2006 TED talk was "The best stats you've ever seen", Monterey, February 2006
    Software lineage Trendalyzer → acquired by Google in March 2007 → Motion Chart Gadget (2008) and Public Data Explorer (2009); Gapminder then built Gapminder Tools as the successor
    Encodings x = income per person, log scale; y = life expectancy; size = population; colour = world region; time = animation frame with a scrub slider and trails
    The measured finding Robertson, Fernandez, Fisher, Lee & Stasko, IEEE TVCG 14(6), 2008, 1325–1332 — explicitly motivated by Trendalyzer and the TED talks

    The numbers to put on the board.

    Context Small multiples Traces Animation
    Analysis, mean time 45.69 s 55.01 s 83.10 s
    Presentation, mean time 25.30 s 27.80 s 15.80 s

    Small multiples were also significantly more accurate than animation for analysis, p < .001. Animation is roughly 82% slower than small multiples for analysis and the fastest form for presentation.

    The flaw that must be named. Without the narrator the chart becomes a change-blindness problem: the viewer must hold earlier positions in working memory, and cannot compare two non-adjacent years at all. Secondary: bubble area is a weak channel for population, and overplotting destroys readability at high bubble counts.

    Hedge. The March 2007 Google acquisition is confirmed; the financial terms were not disclosed and none were found. Do not quote a price.

    Questions to the room. Name the reading task the animation makes impossible, and the chart form that restores it. · If animation is 82% slower for analysis and fastest for presentation, which of our two Tayseer audiences gets which version?

    Bridge to Tayseer. "A play button on a dashboard optimises for the demo and penalises every subsequent use. Animate for the committee room, small-multiple for the analyst pack. Our thirteen-region trend grid is the analysis form; the animated version belongs only in a narrated session. Decide which, and build only that one."

    Module 5 — Evidence and sequencing¶

    Canon 8 — NYT excess deaths, 2020 · slide 134 · canon_07_excess_deaths.png¶

    Setup line. "The chart is a line, a dashed line and a shaded band. Every contested claim in it lives upstream of the chart."

    Facts to get right.

    Fact Detail
    The piece Missing Deaths: Tracking the True Toll of the Coronavirus Outbreak, 21 April 2020, continuously updated
    Coverage 32 countries plus selected cities, including Mumbai, Jakarta, Tokyo, Moscow and Istanbul
    The baseline method A linear model fitted to typically 2015–2019 mortality, with a linear time trend for demographic change plus a smoothing spline for seasonality
    Licence and status The NYT COVID dataset is CC BY-NC 4.0, noncommercial, attribution required. The main dataset stopped updating 24 March 2023; the excess-deaths files were last updated 18 January 2021

    The flaw that must be named. The NYT's own README states it: the expected deaths are not adjusted for how non-Covid deaths may change during the outbreak — so the baseline is contestable exactly where the claim is strongest. This is not a gotcha found by a critic; it is a documented limitation the publisher printed, which is the standard participants should hold themselves to.

    Questions to the room. Rebuild the expected-deaths line with a different baseline window — how much of the claim survives? · A five-percent move in a number nobody looks at swings the headline by more than a hundred thousand deaths: whose job was it to look?

    Bridge to Tayseer. "Our do-nothing December projection in regional_scorecard_latest.csv is a counterfactual, and the hard part is upstream of the chart. If your evidence scene rests on a counterfactual, the counterfactual gets its own annotation, its own sentence, and ideally its own backup slide — because that is the first thing a sharp committee member will attack."

    Sequencing as argument — How the Virus Got Out · slide 135¶

    Setup line. "Controlling the order in which facts arrive is itself a rhetorical act. The reader reaches the conclusion by construction rather than by assertion."

    Facts to get right. Published 22 March 2020; byline Jin Wu, Weiyi Cai, Derek Watkins, James Glanz. Scrollytelling narrative with animated dot-density maps at eight fixed dates — 1, 21, 23 and 31 January; 14 February; 1, 11 and 20 March — plus a mobility and flow visualisation of individual movements out of Wuhan built on phone-location and travel data, comparative regional insets, and a travel-volume time series. The claim: roughly seven million people left Wuhan before the 23 January cordon, so the lockdown came too late to contain global spread.

    The flaw that must be named. Sequencing is persuasion. A reader taken through a fixed order cannot interrogate an alternative order, and the fixed date stops are editorial choices that the form presents as natural. The same technique that makes the argument legible makes it hard to dissent from inside the piece.

    Hedge. Do not claim an award for this piece. The 2021 Pulitzer Prize for Public Service went to the Times' pandemic coverage as a whole, and the citation — "filled a data vacuum" — does not name graphics or visualisation. No specific award for this interactive was verified.

    Questions to the room. List the eight date stops: which are epidemiological and which are editorial? · Where in the Tayseer story does sequence do work that a single chart could not — and where would it be manipulation?

    Bridge to Tayseer. "Module 5's storyboard is this technique at committee scale. The progressive build is scrollytelling on a projector: same chart, one new mark or annotation per click. Sequence deliberately, then state the sequence out loud so the audience knows a choice was made. And apply the test: if your recommendation would have been accepted on slide one, the other six slides were self-indulgence."

    Module 6 — Uncertainty in the room¶

    Canon 9 — The NYT election needle, 2016 · slide 154 · canon_06_nyt_needle.png¶

    Setup line. "Usually told as a mistake. It was not a mistake, and the reason it failed is a design lesson rather than a statistics one."

    Facts to get right.

    Fact Detail
    What it is A live in-progress forecast gauge, not a vote-count display. Debuted election night, 8 November 2016
    Team New York Times Graphics department; the gauge was designed and built by Gregor Aisch; the election results operation has been led by Wilson Andrews since 2016
    The jitter, precisely Not decorative and not random noise around a point. The needle's range was fixed between the 25th and 75th percentile of the model's simulated outcomes, moved with Perlin noise so it read as organic, and the amplitude narrowed as the forecast tightened. The wobble was the uncertainty
    Aisch's rationale Bare probabilities fail most audiences because "it is very hard to make sense of probabilities in between" 75% and 85%
    The formal justification Hullman, Resnick & Adar, PLOS ONE 10(11), e0142444, 16 November 2015 — hypothetical outcome plots animate individual draws from the distribution and let viewers count and integrate rather than decode a statistical convention; n = 288, significantly more accurate than error bars and violin plots for bivariate and trivariate judgements

    The flaw that must be named. A broken metaphor. A gauge is a metaphor for a measured present value — speedometers and thermometers do not jitter — so viewers read the movement as a malfunction or as live returns arriving. One critic: it "implied a TREND where none existed". It became a mass anxiety object. Aisch's own concession is that the piece lacked explicit, static annotation saying what it was doing; he nonetheless defended the technique. In 2020 the Times dropped a national needle and ran needles for only Georgia, Florida and North Carolina, because record absentee balloting broke the model's assumptions.

    Note on the figure. canon_06_nyt_needle.png is a reconstruction of the mechanic, not of that night's numbers. The simulated distribution behind it is illustrative.

    Questions to the room. The wobble encodes the interquartile range — what does a viewer who has not been told that conclude instead? · Which of our dashboard tiles is a forecast dressed as a measurement, and what static annotation fixes it?

    Bridge to Tayseer. "Our December 2026 projection is a forecast with a window choice inside it. Show the range, label the method on the tile, and never let a projection wear the visual costume of a measurement. When you encode uncertainty, the encoding needs a sentence next to it saying that is what it is — an unexplained uncertainty display is read as noise or as news."

    Canon 10 — FiveThirtyEight · slide 155 · canon_09_538_uncertainty.png¶

    Setup line. "The least confident mainstream forecast in 2016 was the one most widely described as wrong. Everything they built afterwards was an answer to that."

    Facts to get right.

    Fact Detail
    2016 final probabilities Polls-only: Trump 29% / Clinton 71%; polls-plus: Trump 28% / Clinton 72%. For contrast: PredictWise gave Trump 11%, HuffPost Pollster 1.7%, DeSart & Holbrook 1.4%
    The 2020 displays A histogram or dot array of roughly 40,000 simulated outcomes across electoral-vote totals; frequency framing in the headline ("he wins X in 100 times we run this"); the snake chart, where states run along a winding path from most Democratic to most Republican, position = competitiveness, segment length = electoral votes, colour = party lean and confidence
    2020 performance Correctly called 48 states and DC, missing Florida, North Carolina and Maine's 2nd district
    The evidence for counting Kay, Kola, Hullman & Munson, CHI '16 — quantile dotplots, a 1.15× reduction in the variance of probability-interval estimates. Fernandes, Walls, Munson, Hullman & Kay, CHI '18 — 50-dot quantile dotplots produced transit decisions at 97% of optimal expected payoff (95% CI [95%, 98%]), five percentage points above control, and more consistent
    Current status FiveThirtyEight was shut down on 5 March 2025 as part of Disney/ABC layoffs; about 15 staff were let go. Successors: Nate Silver's Silver Bulletin and G. Elliott Morris's Strength in Numbers (launched May 2025)

    The housekeeping that must be said. On 15 May 2026 ABC redirected the archive away from the domain: every fivethirtyeight.com/features/… URL now returns a 302 to abcnews.com/politics. Every 538 link on every slide must go through web.archive.org. Check this before each delivery.

    Hedge. Two things must not be said. The widely repeated 2020 final figure ("Biden 89 in 100") could not be verified from a live source — confirm from an archive.org snapshot dated 3 November 2020 before putting any number on a slide. And there is no verified FiveThirtyEight critique of the hurricane cone of uncertainty; attribute that critique to Broad, Leiserowitz, Weinkle & Steketee, BAMS 88(5), 2007, 651–668, and to Alberto Cairo. The cone encodes the historical two-thirds error radius of the track forecast of the storm's centre, and viewers read it as the storm's physical size and as a safe/unsafe boundary.

    Note on the figure. canon_09_538_uncertainty.png reconstructs the techniques with simulated numbers. It is not a reproduction of any published forecast.

    Questions to the room. A committee member says the 71% forecast was "wrong" — answer in one sentence a non-statistician accepts. · Rewrite our December projection tile using frequency framing: what does it cost, and what does it buy?

    Bridge to Tayseer. "Our December projection is a probability statement. Use frequency framing in the committee — 'in X of 10 plausible futures the five regions still miss 65%' — and show the distribution, not the point. People can count dots; they cannot decode a probability."

    Activity 1 — Gallery Walk: Chart Crimes¶

    Purpose. Apply the six-question protocol to real charts at speed, in public, with a fix attached to every criticism. This is the twenty-five minutes that establishes the critique culture for the rest of the course; everything in Modules 3 to 6 that depends on participants being willing to attack a chart in front of colleagues depends on this session working.

    Duration. 25 minutes. Group size. Four. Placement. Day 1, immediately after Lab 1. Materials. Paper only — no laptops.

    Prep. Eight charts printed at A3 and pinned around the room with a blank A3 sheet beside each: four from the canon, two spun Tayseer tiles, two the instructor brings. A marker per group. The six-question protocol on the wall or on a handout.

    Minutes What happens
    0–4 Each group starts at one chart. Silent looking first — no talking. Then write the chart's claim in one sentence on the sheet beside it.
    4–12 Rotate twice, three minutes per chart. At each stop, add one new finding naming the channel and one proposed fix. Repeating an earlier group's finding scores nothing.
    12–17 Return to your first chart and read everything the room added. Rank the findings: which single change would most improve this chart?
    17–23 Plenary. One group per chart reports the ranked fix — 45 seconds each, no preamble.
    23–25 Instructor closes on the two spun Tayseer charts and names the technique behind each from kpi_spin_pairs.csv.

    The facilitation moves that make it work.

    Enforce "one new finding" ruthlessly. It is what forces the later groups past the obvious — past "the axis is truncated" — and into the encoding, the denominator and the measure definition. Without that rule the third rotation produces nothing.

    Put the two spun Tayseer charts in the rotation without telling anyone they are spun. The moment a group discovers the truncated axis on their own is the moment the ethics content of Day 2 becomes theirs rather than yours. If you pre-announce them, you have given away Case B for nothing.

    Keep the silent-looking minute. Two minutes of quiet at a chart produces findings that a group conversation never reaches, because the first person to speak sets the frame for everyone else.

    The two ways it fails.

    It fails as taste. The sheets fill up with "too busy", "ugly colours", "hard to read". Recovery: stop the room at the first rotation, read one such comment aloud without attribution, and ask the group to rewrite it naming the channel. One public rewrite fixes the whole room. If you see this coming in the first ninety seconds, re-read question 2 of the protocol before the rotation starts.

    It fails as silence. With a quiet or junior cohort, nobody wants to write the first criticism where colleagues will read it. Recovery: seed each sheet yourself with one mild, obviously-correct finding before the session starts — it gives permission and sets the register. If the room is under twelve people, abandon the rotation entirely and run it as one moving group with you facilitating at each chart; it works, and it is better than three groups of two staring at paper.

    Debrief questions. Which finding, across all eight charts, would you have missed if you had been the first group to that chart, and why? · Which of the eight charts was hardest to state a claim for — and is that the chart's fault? · Two of these were ours: what did the room find in them that we did not?

    What the instructor collects. The eight annotated A3 sheets. Keep them — pin them for the rest of the course, and use the ranked fixes from the two spun tiles as the opening exhibit for Case B on Day 2. Also note which groups produced encoding-level findings; those are the participants to seed across the Red Team teams tomorrow.

    Activity 2 — Red Team: break the honest dashboard¶

    Purpose. Attack a colleague's honest dashboard with every legitimate-looking technique available, then defend your own against the same attack. It is the single best predictor of who survives Q&A on Day 3, because it forces participants to rehearse the attack before they are in front of the committee.

    Duration. 30 minutes. Grouping. Two teams. Placement. Late Day 2, after Lab 3.

    Prep. Each team brings its own Lab 3 "after" chart — the decluttered, annotated redesign. The spin bank (kpi_spin_pairs.csv, or the thirteen-row table on deck slide 93) is available to both teams, but invented techniques score double. Scoring sheet on a flip chart.

    Ground rule, stated before anything else. This is about method, never about a person or a real report. Nothing from a participant's own organisation enters the exercise.

    Minutes What happens
    0–6 RED: make the other team's chart tell a more flattering story, changing no underlying data. Log every change and why it is defensible.
    6–12 BLUE, independently and simultaneously: list the three changes you would make if you were told "make this look better for the committee".
    12–20 Swap and compare. Score one point per technique the other team predicted, two points per technique they did not.
    20–26 Each team writes the single sentence of annotation that would make its chart immune to its own best attack.
    26–30 Plenary. The instructor collects any technique not already in kpi_spin_pairs.csv and adds it to the course bank.

    The facilitation moves that make it work.

    The scoring asymmetry is the engine. Two points for an unpredicted technique is what makes teams reach past the obvious truncated axis into measure definitions and coverage filters — which is where the real distortion lives. Five of the thirteen shipped techniques have a negative gap, meaning the wrong method understates the result; mention that only in the debrief, because it is the argument against treating the bank as a list of tricks.

    Task 4 is the deliverable that matters and it is worth protecting the six minutes. A single annotated sentence stating the denominator, the window and the target closes most of the attack surface at once. Collect those sentences; several usually belong verbatim in the capstone decks.

    Keep the frame off cynicism. Almost nobody builds a misleading dashboard on purpose — they inherit a measure definition, accept a tool default, and pick the axis range that makes the chart look like the others. The fix is a method, not a virtue. An analyst who can name the operation can decline it in a meeting without accusing anyone of dishonesty.

    The two ways it fails.

    It fails as an arms race. The teams start inventing techniques that no real analyst would deploy — outright fabrication, invented categories — and the exercise loses its transfer value. Recovery: rule early that every technique must be one the team could defend out loud to a Deputy Minister as a reasonable choice. That is the whole definition of spin, and it puts the ceiling back on.

    It fails on the personal. Someone recognises a technique from their own workplace and the room goes quiet or defensive. Recovery: you stated the ground rule for this reason — restate it, take the technique into the abstract ("so the pattern is: a filter that is real, documented nowhere, and decisive"), and move on without pursuing the example. Do not let the room start comparing organisations.

    Debrief questions. Which technique did your team predict that the other team did not use — and does that mean it is safe, or just that they missed it? · Of the techniques used against you, which would have survived your own peer review? (The ones that survive peer review are the ones nobody can detect, and those are the dangerous half of the bank.) · What is the one sentence of annotation you wrote, and which of the four things does it state: denominator, window, target, or method?

    What the instructor collects. The annotation sentences, one per team, which go into the capstone decks. Any technique not already in kpi_spin_pairs.csv — add it to the course bank with the operation that produces it, or it is not usable. The scoring sheet, which tells you which team is thinking at the level of measure definitions and which is still at the level of axis ranges.

    If the room is small. Run it instructor-versus-room, with you as red team. It works, and it is fun. You will need to be genuinely good at it, so prepare two attacks on each likely Lab 3 chart in advance.

    Activity 3 — Build It Both Ways: animation versus small multiples¶

    Purpose. Reproduce the Robertson et al. (2008) design in miniature on the participants' own data, so that the finding is something they measured rather than something they were told. A participant who has personally failed to answer a question from their own animation never over-uses one again.

    Duration. 20 minutes. Grouping. Pairs. Placement. Inside Lab 4b.

    Prep. notebooks/lab4_dashboard_starter.ipynb, the animation-versus-small-multiples section. Data: tayseer_services.csv, 60 months × 13 regions. A benchmark sheet per pair with two time columns. A stopwatch or phone timer per pair.

    Minutes What happens
    0–6 Build the animated bubble or line view with a year slider, region colour and a play control.
    6–11 Build the same thirteen series as a small-multiples grid, each panel against the other twelve in grey, ordered by latest value.
    11–16 Your partner asks, without warning: "which two regions crossed each other between month 20 and month 44?" Time both views.
    16–20 Record both times on the benchmark sheet and write which view you would ship, for which audience, and why.

    The facilitation moves that make it work.

    Make the comparison question require two non-adjacent time points. An animation handles "what happened next" perfectly well and fails only on "compare then with then". A question like "which region rose fastest last year" will not produce the effect and the activity will land flat.

    Do not tell them the expected result beforehand. The surprise is the whole mechanism. Expect the small-multiples answer in seconds and the animated one in tens of seconds, or not at all — that gap is the teaching point, not the chart.

    Close by connecting it to the dashboard they are building. Every interaction pattern should be chosen by the task, exactly as encoding channels were in Module 1.

    The two ways it fails.

    It fails on build time. Six minutes for an animated view is tight, and a pair that fights the plotting library will never reach the timed comparison, which is the only part that matters. Recovery: have the animated view pre-built in the solution notebook and let any pair that is behind at minute six switch to it. Losing the build is acceptable; losing the measurement is not.

    It fails because the answer is guessed. A pair that already knows the regional story answers the comparison question from memory rather than from the chart, and both times come out at two seconds. Recovery: assign the question from a pre-written list that uses month indices rather than region names, and have the partner — not the builder — choose it at random from an envelope.

    Debrief questions. What was your ratio between the two times, and how does it compare with 83.1 against 45.7 seconds? · Which view would you ship, for which audience — and what would you do if both audiences use the same dashboard? · Where on the Tayseer dashboard is there currently a play button, and what task is it serving?

    What the instructor collects. The benchmark sheets. Two pairs' numbers go on the board next to the Robertson figures; the room's own spread is more persuasive than the published one. Also collect the written ship-decision — one sentence per pair, naming the audience — because that sentence is the reasoning the capstone dashboard will need to defend.

    Activity 4 — Canon Quickfire¶

    Purpose. A Day 3 warm-up. Two jobs: bring back the whole canon in ten minutes after a night's gap, and then turn it on the participants' own capstone material so that the last day starts with each person knowing which canonical failure their own deck is most likely to repeat. It is deliberately fast and slightly competitive, because Day 3 Hour 1 is the hardest hour in the course to start from cold.

    Duration. 15 minutes. Grouping. Tables of four. Placement. Day 3, Hour 1, before the storytelling lecture.

    Prep. Ten canon cards, one per work, printed A5 double-sided: the figure on the front, and on the back nothing but the item id and title from critique_canon.csv. One set per table if you have four tables or fewer; otherwise one set for the instructor and project the figures instead. A blank card per participant for the final round. Timer visible to the room.

    Minutes What happens
    0–2 Set up. Explain the two rounds and the scoring. Tables pick a scribe.
    2–8 Round 1 — the canon in six minutes. Instructor shows a figure; tables have 35 seconds to write three things: the work, the flaw that must be named, and the transferable lesson. One point each, three per card, ten cards. No conferring across tables, no notes, no laptops.
    8–9 Score. Tables swap sheets and mark against the instructor's read-out. Read the ten flaws aloud at pace — this read-out is the actual revision, and the scoring is the excuse for it.
    9–13 Round 2 — turn it on yourself. Each participant takes a blank card and writes, privately: "The canon item my capstone is most vulnerable to is , because ." One sentence each. Then round the table, thirty seconds per person, and the table picks the most honest answer rather than the cleverest.
    13–15 Plenary. Four tables, one answer each, read verbatim. The instructor names which module's fix applies to each and where it lives in the notebooks.

    The facilitation moves that make it work.

    Thirty-five seconds is deliberately not enough to discuss. It forces recall rather than reasoning, which is what a warm-up is for, and it keeps the energy high without anyone having to perform.

    The read-out in minute eight is the real teaching content. Do not rush it to protect the scoring — the scoring exists to make the room listen to the read-out. Ten flaws in sixty seconds each is the right pace.

    In round 2, ask for the most honest answer, not the best. A table that nominates "our December projection is a needle — it is a forecast dressed as a measurement" has done more for its capstone than a table that nominates something safe. Say so.

    The two ways it fails.

    It fails as a quiz nobody can answer. If the cohort genuinely cannot recall the canon, six minutes of blank sheets is demoralising at the start of the hardest day. Recovery: watch the first two cards. If two of four tables are blank, switch instantly to open-book — the protocol handout and the canon overview slide become available, the timing stays the same, and the activity becomes a look-up race instead of a recall test. It still works and nobody is humiliated.

    It fails as round 2 theatre. Participants write a vulnerability they do not actually have, because it sounds good. Recovery: go first yourself with a real one from a deck you have given. Specificity from the instructor buys specificity from the room; abstraction from the instructor guarantees abstraction back.

    Debrief questions. Which of the ten was hardest to recall, and is that because it is the least useful or because it is the least visual? · Round 2 asked which canonical failure your deck repeats — what is the smallest change that removes it before Hour 4? · Three of the ten failures are about the denominator, the baseline or the window. Which one is the most common in the reporting you already produce?

    What the instructor collects. The round-2 cards, all twenty-four of them. They are the most useful single artefact of Day 3 morning: read them during the break and you know exactly which capstones need which intervention in Hour 4, and which two or three participants should be paired with which reviewer for the peer-critique round.

    Part 5 — Timing variants¶

    The critique thread costs 90 minutes of activity time plus roughly 35 minutes of canon slides across three days. Neither is padding, but both can be compressed.

    Compressed delivery — what to cut, in this order¶

    Cut Saves What you lose
    Canon Quickfire 15 min The Day 3 warm-up and the vulnerability cards. Cheapest cut — it is a revision device, not new content. Replace with a two-minute verbal run down the canon overview slide
    Build It Both Ways, build phases 11 min Pairs use the pre-built views in the solution notebook and run only the timed comparison and the ship decision. The measurement survives; the build practice does not. Keep this reduced version rather than cutting the activity
    Gallery Walk, third rotation 3 min Two stops instead of three. Findings per chart drop by about a third. Acceptable
    Canon 6 (An Incalculable Loss) and the sequencing slide ~6 min Module 3's restraint argument and Module 5's scrollytelling bridge. Both are conceptual rather than skill-building, and Module 5's progressive-build demo can carry the sequencing point alone
    Canon 10 (FiveThirtyEight) ~4 min The frequency-framing evidence. Only cut this if you have already made the frequency-framing point on slide 156, which carries it independently

    Do not cut, in a compressed delivery: the six-question protocol slide, the Gallery Walk, or the Red Team. The protocol is the spine of every later critique; the Gallery Walk is what makes critique socially possible in the room; and the Red Team is the better predictor of Day 3 performance than any other single session. If Day 2 is collapsing, the honest instructor-judgement call — and it is a judgement call, flagged as such on the pre-flight slide — is whether Red Team runs or Lab 4b runs in full. Protect Red Team if you can.
