    Data and Resource Catalogue¶

    SDA-DSC-212 · Time Series Analysis and Forecasting · Instructor reference

    This document covers what ships with the course, how it was made and why it is synthetic, what open data is worth reaching for when a cohort wants a real-data variant, which external courses and texts this one is measured against, and the content of the 15–20 minute overview of the modern forecasting landscape that keeps the course current without expanding its scope.

    Every figure describing a course-provided file was measured from the file itself. Figures describing external datasets are stated at the level of confidence they deserve; where a detail matters operationally — a licence, a row count, a redistribution term — verify it on the source page before handing anything to a cohort.

    Forecast error grows with lead time, as it should. Measured against the realised series:

    Lead 14-day file: MAE 28-day file: MAE
    1–24 h 0.79 °C 0.75 °C
    24–72 h 1.08 °C 0.95 °C

    The point for participants: the exogenous regressor that makes SARIMAX work is not an observation at serve time. It is another model's output, with its own error, and a service that never measures the effect of substituting it is reporting an accuracy it does not deliver.

    The planted defects in ksa_grid_demand.csv¶

    The raw file is 45 rows short of the true 26,280 and contains four distinct classes of defect. Every one of them is deliberate, and each teaches a specific step of Lab 1.

    Defect Measured extent What it teaches Where
    Silent gaps 64 missing hours in 34 runs — 14 one-hour, 12 two-hour, 7 three-hour, and 1 five-hour outage A CSV read straight into pandas produces a DatetimeIndex with no frequency and no visible holes. Only reindexing onto a complete hourly range materialises them as NaN. Until then, lag24 silently spans 25 hours. Lab 1, Task "build the index"; feeds the gap-policy requirement in the capstone
    Duplicated timestamps 19 duplicated rows (26,216 unique timestamps across 26,235 rows) set_index succeeds and reindex fails, or worse, an aggregation silently double-counts. Forces an explicit de-duplication rule — first, last, or mean — and forces it to be written down. Lab 1
    Sensor zeros 7 rows with demand_mw == 0.0 A grid never draws zero. These are sensor failures, not observations. They must be caught before the log transform — log(0) is -inf and propagates silently through a decomposition. Lab 1, and again in Lab 2 when the transform is chosen
    Non-monotonic rows 192 positions where the timestamp decreases; a 400-row block is shuffled Row order is not time order. Every lag, every rolling window and every .diff() is wrong until the frame is sorted, and none of them raise an error. Lab 1

    A cohort that fixes all four in Lab 1 gets a series identical to ksa_grid_demand_clean.csv except at the 64 imputed hours. The reference run in reference_results.json records 26,235 raw rows, 26,280 clean rows, 71 missing values after reindexing, a longest gap of 5 hours, and 66 imputed values — the difference between 71 and 64 being the sensor zeros that were converted to NaN before imputation.

    Structure planted in the demand series¶

    Knowing what is in the series is what makes it gradeable. From tools/make_tayyar_data.py:

    Component Design
    Level and trend Base 27,500 MW, compounding at about 2.9% per year
    Annual seasonality Two harmonics on day-of-year, peaking around mid-July — the residual summer effect after weather is accounted for separately
    Daily seasonality Three harmonics on hour-of-day, amplitude about ±15% at the fundamental
    Weekly seasonality Multiplicative: 0.945 on Friday and Saturday, 1.012 otherwise
    Weather response Piecewise around a 21 °C balance point: cooling load 1 + 0.0165·CDD + 0.00012·CDD² above it, plus a milder heating response 0.0090·HDD below 14 °C. The quadratic term is why a linear temperature coefficient under-fits the extreme afternoons.
    Ramadan A shape change, not a level change: daytime hours 06:00–15:00 multiplied by 0.930, post-Iftar hours 19:00–01:00 by 1.075
    Eid Level multiplied by 0.905 across the Eid al-Fitr and Eid al-Adha windows
    National Day Level multiplied by 0.955 on 23 September
    Structural break 1 October 2022: a permanent +3.1% step, described in the generator as a large industrial customer connecting
    Heatwaves Three planted multi-day temperature excursions: +3.4 °C for 6 days from 2021-07-28, +4.0 °C for 7 days from 2022-08-04, +3.6 °C for 5 days from 2023-07-17
    Innovations AR(1) with coefficient 0.72 on the log scale, so the remainder has genuine memory and the ACF is not white noise after seasonal adjustment
    Temperature process Annual sinusoid (mean 26 °C, amplitude 12.5 °C) plus a diurnal sinusoid (amplitude 6.5 °C) plus AR(1) weather noise with coefficient 0.93
    Seed 212, fixed. Regenerating the data reproduces it exactly.

    2. How the course dataset was built, and why it is synthetic¶

    tools/make_tayyar_data.py builds every file in data/ from a single seed in a few seconds. The construction is multiplicative — level × trend × annual × daily × weekly × calendar × weather × exp(AR(1)) — with the structural break applied last as a step, and the defects injected afterwards into a copy.

    Why a controlled synthetic series is the right choice here¶

    Ground truth is known, so the assessment can be graded. When the generator says the seasonal strength is high, the break is on 1 October 2022 and the balance point is 21 °C, an instructor can mark a stationarity report or a feature-importance interpretation against a fact rather than against a plausible-sounding narrative. With real telemetry there is no answer key, and the assessment degrades into rewarding fluency.

    It is reproducible across cohorts. Every cohort meets the same 57,851.5 MW peak, the same five-hour outage and the same 19 duplicated timestamps. Benchmarks in the module tables mean something across deliveries. A cohort in March and a cohort in November can be compared. A dataset refreshed from a live feed cannot support that.

    Each defect exists to teach one thing. Real data is messy in ways that are correlated, overlapping and often uninteresting — a hundred small artefacts of one ingestion bug. The planted defects here are four clean, separable lessons that map to four steps of Lab 1. That is a teaching property, not a realism property, and it is the right trade for a 15-hour course.

    There are no distribution restrictions. Real Saudi hourly grid telemetry is not available for redistribution to a training cohort. A synthetic series can be committed to the repository, handed to participants, pushed to their own public capstone repositories and reused without a licence conversation.

    The difficulty is calibrated. The signal-to-noise ratio was chosen so that a competent day-ahead model lands around 2–2.5% MAPE and a seasonal-naive baseline around 4%. That gap is wide enough for model comparison to be meaningful and narrow enough that no model looks miraculous. Real data with this much structure would make the labs trivial; real data with less would make them unfinishable in an hour.

    What it is not, and what must be said out loud¶

    A model that does well here has not been validated on real telemetry. It has been validated against a generative process whose functional form it can in principle recover. The weather response is a known quadratic; the Ramadan effect is a known pair of multipliers; the innovations are AR(1) by construction. A LightGBM that reaches 2.3% MAPE on this series has learned a structure that was put there on purpose.

    Four specific limits are worth naming to a cohort of practising data scientists:

    1. The noise is too well-behaved. Real load series contain measurement artefacts, metering changes, DST-like clock issues, retrospective restatements and periods where a substation's data is simply wrong for a month. None of that is here beyond the four planted defects.
    2. The break is single, clean and dated. Real series drift, break repeatedly, and break for reasons nobody records. The October 2022 step is unusually cooperative.
    3. The weather–demand relationship is stationary. In reality it moves — air-conditioning stock grows, efficiency standards change, behaviour shifts. The balance point is not a constant of nature.
    4. There is no genuine regime the model has never seen. Every heatwave in the test window is drawn from the same process as those in the training window. The extrapolation failure that tree-based models suffer on a genuine record-breaking day is under-represented here, and it is the failure that matters most operationally.

    The honest framing for participants, and the one the capstone rubric rewards: this dataset is a flight simulator. It is the right place to learn the instruments and the procedures, and passing in it is not a licence to fly. Any capstone that claims validated real-world performance on the basis of these numbers should lose marks for it.

    For instructors who want a real-data variant, and for participants continuing after the course. Licences and row counts change; verify on the source page before redistributing anything.

    ElectricityLoadDiagrams20112014 UCI Machine Learning Repository. Excellent for decomposition and seasonality (M1), for multi-series and global-model work (M4), and for showing what genuinely messy consumption data looks like — several series begin part-way through the period and read zero before they start, which is a better cleaning exercise than anything that can be planted.     Open Power System Data — time series package open-power-system-data.org, compiled from ENTSO-E and European TSO publications. That turns an abstract benchmark into a concrete one — "we beat the TSO's forecast on 43% of hours" is a sentence with meaning. It is the strongest available basis for an advanced or follow-on session on M6 and M7.
    Kaggle — Store Sales: Time Series Forecasting Kaggle competition dataset; requires an account. Check the competition rules for redistribution terms. Daily unit sales by store and product family for a grocery chain, with holiday, oil-price and promotion tables The default dataset for CS2.     Kaggle — M5 Forecasting (Accuracy / Uncertainty) Kaggle competition dataset; check the competition rules. Daily unit sales for a large hierarchical retail panel, with calendar and price tables; a large fraction of series are intermittent Richer than CS2 needs, and the right choice if the retail case is extended into a full lab. The M5 Uncertainty track is also the best public example of a competition scored on quantiles rather than point accuracy, which makes it a strong companion to M5 of this course.
    Nixtla example datasets Bundled with the Nixtla open-source libraries (statsforecast, mlforecast, neuralforecast, utilsforecast) and their documentation; the libraries are Apache-2.0, the bundled datasets are small and intended for examples. Small hourly and daily electricity and demand series, plus loaders for the M-competition datasets Lightweight benchmarking. The value is less the data than the uniform API across three paradigms — the same series fitted by a classical model, a gradient-boosted feature model and a neural model with near-identical calling conventions.     UCI — Metro Interstate Traffic Volume UCI Machine Learning Repository; confirm licence and citation on the page.     METR-LA and PEMS-BAY Standard public benchmarks in the spatiotemporal traffic-forecasting literature; distributed with several open research repositories. These are where the CS4 debrief points participants who want to continue into graph-based spatiotemporal forecasting. They are out of scope for this course and should not be introduced into it.

    Choosing between them¶

    If the requirement is "replace Tayyar with real data", use the Monash hourly electricity set: it is clean, hourly, well-documented, has published baselines and needs almost no preparation. Accept that the labs lose the Saudi calendar effects, the Fri–Sat weekend and the planted defects, which means Lab 1 and roughly half of Lab 4 need rewriting.

    If the requirement is "add a real-data session on top of Tayyar", use Open Power System Data. The comparison against a published operational forecast is the single most valuable thing on this list and it exists nowhere else.

    If the requirement is "give participants somewhere to go afterwards", point at the Monash archive and its baseline tables, and at the M5 Uncertainty track for anyone who took M5 seriously.

    4. Reference courses and texts¶

    Resource What this course should take from it
    Hyndman & Athanasopoulos, Forecasting: Principles and Practice — the Python edition The primary academic benchmark. Take the conceptual sequencing — structure and decomposition before stationarity, stationarity before ARIMA, point forecasts before intervals, and evaluation as a first-class topic rather than an appendix — and take the statistical rigour, particularly its insistence that a forecast is a distribution and that residual diagnostics are not optional. Where this course diverges (much heavier weight on gradient-boosted models and on the production pipeline), be able to say why.
    Coursera — Time Series Forecasting with Python: Models to Production Confirms that the classical-plus-ML structure of M3–M4 is the current market expectation rather than a local preference, and reinforces the production emphasis of M7. Take the framing that a model is not finished until it is served; leave the breadth, which is wider than 15 hours can carry.
    Kaggle Learn — Time Series A source of short feature-engineering exercises for M4: lags, trend and seasonality as features, Fourier terms, and the hybrid pattern of fitting a simple model to the trend and a gradient-boosted model to its residuals. Its exercises are the right length for a 10-minute mini-exercise. Do not take its evaluation practice, which is lighter than this course requires.
    DataCamp — Designing Forecasting Pipelines for Production The benchmark for turning the Day-3 capstone from a notebook into a believable service: configuration-driven runs, a shared feature module used by both training and serving, and monitoring. It works from hourly electricity data, so its examples transfer directly. Take the pipeline discipline; the capstone's "a stranger should clone it and reproduce every number" requirement is the same standard stated more sharply.

    5. The modern forecasting landscape — a 15–20 minute overview slot¶

    Placement. Day 3, at the start of Hour 4, immediately before CS1 and the capstone assembly. Participants have by then built classical models, a feature-based ML model, calibrated intervals and a rolling-origin backtest, so they can place each stage against something they have done themselves.

    Purpose, stated to the room in the first thirty seconds. This course does not teach deep learning, and that is deliberate — a 15-hour course that added neural forecasting would have to remove backtesting or calibration to make room, and both matter more. What follows is a map, so that when someone proposes a transformer or a foundation model in a meeting next month, participants can ask the right three questions instead of either deferring or dismissing.

    Structure. Four stages, roughly four minutes each. For each: what it is, when it earns its place, and one honest caveat. The through-line is a single question repeated at every stage — what does this buy, and what does it cost to keep running?

    Stage 1 — Statistical forecasting¶

    What it is. Models with an explicit stochastic structure fitted per series: naive and seasonal-naive baselines, exponential smoothing and the ETS taxonomy, ARIMA and SARIMA, SARIMAX with exogenous regressors, Theta, and multiple-seasonality extensions such as TBATS or MSTL followed by a simple model on the deseasonalised remainder. Everything in M3, plus the decomposition in M1.

    When it earns its place. When the series count is small and each series is long. When covariates are few or absent. When a component story is required — a model that can say "trend contributes this much, the daily cycle this much, temperature this much" survives a regulatory conversation that feature importance does not. When intervals must be available immediately: ETS and ARIMA produce analytic prediction intervals from the model's own error variance, with no calibration step. And, practically, when nobody will be available to maintain a feature pipeline.

    One honest caveat. The interval is only as good as the distributional assumption behind it. In this course's own reference run, SARIMAX with temperature produces the best classical point forecast (2.05% MAPE against ETS at 2.68%) and a Ljung–Box p-value of 0.000, meaning autocorrelation remains in its residuals and the model is under-specified. Its analytic 90% interval covers 96.4% of observations — wider than advertised, which happens to be the safe direction, but it is miscalibration all the same. A well-fitting classical model that fails its residual diagnostics is reporting an uncertainty it has not earned.

    Stage 2 — Feature-based machine learning¶

    What it is. Reduce forecasting to supervised regression. Build a design matrix of lags, rolling statistics, calendar variables and Fourier terms; fit a gradient-boosted tree ensemble — LightGBM, XGBoost, CatBoost. Train one global model across many series with a series identifier as a categorical feature, rather than one model per series. Choose recursive or direct multi-step strategies deliberately. All of M4.

    When it earns its place. When there are many series and few of them are individually long — the global model borrows structure across the panel and rescues the short ones. When covariates are rich and their interactions matter: temperature × hour × Ramadan is trivial for a tree ensemble and awkward for a linear specification. When training must be fast and cheap: this course's LightGBM fits in seconds on a laptop. Gradient-boosted models on engineered features have been the dominant approach in recent large-scale forecasting competitions, and they remain the default first serious model for tabular-shaped forecasting problems.

    One honest caveat. Trees cannot extrapolate. A tree's prediction is an average of training targets in a leaf, so it is bounded by the range of the target it was trained on — on a record-breaking demand day it will under-forecast the peak, systematically and silently, which is precisely the day the forecast matters most. The mitigations are known (difference the target, model a ratio to a baseline, normalise per series) and none of them is automatic. The second, larger cost is organisational: the feature pipeline is a permanent liability. It must be versioned, shared identically between training and serving, monitored for skew, and owned. A 0.3-point MAPE gain that comes with a standing engineering commitment is often a bad trade, and being able to say so is part of LO6.

    Stage 3 — Neural forecasting¶

    What it is. Models trained end to end on raw or lightly processed series, learning their own representations. The families worth naming: DeepAR, an autoregressive recurrent model producing probabilistic forecasts across a panel; N-BEATS and N-HiTS, deep stacks of fully connected blocks with interpretable trend and seasonality variants; the Temporal Fusion Transformer, which handles static covariates, known-future covariates and past covariates explicitly and produces quantile outputs; and more recent transformer and patch-based architectures such as PatchTST, with simple linear baselines like DLinear deliberately maintained alongside them as controls. The competition landmark worth mentioning is the M4 winner, a hybrid of exponential smoothing and a recurrent network, which was neither purely statistical nor purely neural.

    When it earns its place. When there are very many related series and a great deal of data — the regime where a global neural model can learn shared structure that neither a per-series statistical model nor a hand-built feature set can express. When covariate structure is complex enough that engineering features by hand is the bottleneck. When probabilistic output across many horizons is required natively rather than bolted on. And when there is a team to own it.

    One honest caveat. The published gains are frequently smaller than the abstracts suggest, and sometimes disappear under fair comparison. The DLinear result — that a simple linear model matched or beat several transformer architectures on standard long-horizon benchmarks — is worth stating plainly, not because transformers are useless but because it demonstrates how weak the baselines in that literature often were. The practical caveat is cost: tuning, hardware, longer training cycles, and a much larger surface for silent failure. For a single hourly series with three years of history, which is the situation most participants will actually face, a neural model is very unlikely to beat a well-built LightGBM by enough to justify itself.

    Stage 4 — Foundation models for time series¶

    What it is. Models pre-trained on very large and diverse collections of time series, then applied to a new series zero-shot — a forecast with no fitting on the target series at all. Representative examples: TimeGPT from Nixtla, offered as a commercial API; Chronos from Amazon, which tokenises series values and trains a language-model architecture over them, with open weights; Lag-Llama; Moirai from Salesforce; and TimesFM from Google. The architectural ideas differ, but the proposition is the same one: forecasting as a pre-trained capability rather than a per-series fitting exercise.

    When it earns its place. Three situations, and they are genuinely valuable ones. Cold start — a new series with almost no history, where every method in stages 1 to 3 has nothing to fit. Breadth at low cost — thousands of low-value series that do not individually justify a modelling effort. And as a fast baseline: getting a credible zero-shot forecast in minutes tells you the difficulty of a problem before committing to a project, which is a real and underrated use.

    One honest caveat. Zero-shot claims on public benchmarks are hard to verify, because the pre-training corpora are large and not always fully disclosed, so it can be impossible to establish that a benchmark series was not seen in training. Treat published zero-shot numbers as a hypothesis about your data, not a result on it — and test it the way this course tests everything else, with a rolling-origin backtest on your own series against your own baselines. Three further practical limits: covariate handling is weaker and less flexible than a SARIMAX or a TFT for a problem where a known-future regressor such as temperature carries most of the signal; a hosted model is an external dependency inside a 14:00 publication deadline, with the latency and availability implications that carries; and explainability is close to nil, which for a regulated operational decision may end the conversation on its own.

    Closing the slot¶

    Two sentences, and then straight into CS1:

    The progression is not a ladder where each rung replaces the one below it. It is a widening set of answers to the question "how much structure do I have to specify myself", and the right answer for a given problem is usually further down that list than the literature implies.

    Whichever stage a model comes from, it enters production through the same gate: a rolling-origin backtest, a calibrated interval, a named baseline it beats, and a written record of the decision. That gate is what this course is actually about, and it does not move.

    6. Licensing and distribution notes¶

    The course's own data¶

    Freely redistributable. Every file in data/ is generated by tools/make_tayyar_data.py from a fixed seed. It contains no real operational telemetry, no personal data and no third-party copyrighted content. It may be distributed to participants, committed to participants' own public capstone repositories, and reused in later deliveries without restriction.

    Two conditions attach, and both are pedagogical rather than legal:

    1. It must be labelled as synthetic wherever it appears. A capstone repository that presents this series as Saudi grid data, or that reports a MAPE as evidence of real-world performance, is making a claim it cannot support. Data-provenance honesty is part of the assessed work.
    2. ksa_grid_demand_clean.csv is instructor-only until Lab 1 is graded. It is the answer key. It may be released afterwards, and should be — participants comparing their cleaned series against it learn more than the mark tells them.

    External datasets¶

    Nothing in section 3 may be re-hosted by the Academy without checking its terms first. The safe default for every one of them is to link to the source and have participants download it themselves. That is also better practice, since it makes the provenance visible.

    UCI ElectricityLoadDiagrams20112014. Published under Creative Commons Attribution 4.0 International (CC BY 4.0), which permits redistribution and adaptation with attribution. Carry the attribution wherever the data or anything derived from it appears — slides, notebooks and any derived dataset:

    ElectricityLoadDiagrams20112014, UCI Machine Learning Repository. Licensed under CC BY 4.0. Available at https://archive.ics.uci.edu/dataset/321/electricityloaddiagrams20112014

    Confirm the donor name and the current citation format on the dataset page before publishing; UCI has revised its citation guidance over time.

    Monash Time Series Forecasting Archive. Datasets are distributed through Zenodo with per-record licensing; most are CC BY 4.0. Cite the archive paper (Godahewa, Bergmeir, Webb, Hyndman and Montero-Manso, 2021) alongside the individual dataset record.

    Open Power System Data. Licensing is stated per data package and derives from the underlying TSO and ENTSO-E sources. Check the specific package page and reproduce whatever attribution it specifies.

    Kaggle competition datasets (Store Sales, M5, Wikipedia Web Traffic). Governed by the competition rules accepted at download, which commonly restrict redistribution. Do not stage these on a shared Academy drive without checking. Participants download them under their own accounts.

    UCI Metro Interstate Traffic Volume, METR-LA, PEMS-BAY. Check terms on the respective source pages before any redistribution.
