    Practical Assessment 2 — The Leakage Post-Mortem¶

    SDA-DSC-212 · Time Series Analysis and Forecasting · 30 minutes · Day 3

    At a glance¶
    Assessment weight Practical Assessment 2, part of the 20% allocated to the two practical assessments
    Module fit M6 — Backtesting and Forecast Evaluation; reinforces M4
    LOs assessed LO4 primary (time-based validation), LO5 (honest measurement)
    Slot Day 3, Hour 2 — inside the M6 theory hour, immediately after rolling-origin design is introduced
    Duration 30 minutes, plus 5 for the debrief
    Format Pairs, at machines. Individual write-up.
    Materials tools/leak_demo.py, leak_results.json, figures/08_leakage.png, src/tayyar/features/build.py

    The core lesson¶

    In forecasting, a model is only as trustworthy as the temporal integrity of its evaluation.

    State it once, at the end. Do not state it at the beginning, and do not restate it. The whole design of this exercise is that participants arrive at it themselves, having measured the gap with their own hands, and a sentence they have earned is worth more than a sentence they have been given.

    Participant-facing scenario¶

    Hand this out, or read it. It is the entire briefing they get.

    Eight weeks ago your team shipped a LightGBM day-ahead forecaster for the Central Operating Area. The validation report was excellent: 1.61% MAPE on a 20% held-out sample. That number was in the deck that got the project approved, and it beat the incumbent SARIMAX comfortably.

    The service has now been live for six weeks. Operations reports errors between three and five percent — occasionally worse — and the day-ahead scheduling engineer has quietly gone back to her similar-day spreadsheet for the afternoon peak. Nothing in the code has changed since the validation run. The model that produced 1.61% and the model producing 4% are the same model, running on the same data source.

    The engineer who built it has left. You have the training script and the feature-building code. The head of the unit wants three things from you by the end of the hour, in this order:

    1. What is the true day-ahead accuracy of this model?
    2. Why did the validation report say otherwise?
    3. What has to change so that this cannot happen again?

    You will not be marked on making the model better. You will be marked on establishing what it actually does, on identifying precisely which lines of code broke temporal integrity, and on re-measuring honestly once you have fixed them. A correct diagnosis with a worse-looking number scores higher than a good number with no diagnosis.

    Your tasks¶

    1. Detect. Establish that the reported 1.61% is not achievable at serve time. Do not argue it from first principles — demonstrate it.
    2. Identify. Name every engineered feature that uses information not available at the forecast origin, and state for each one exactly which future observations it touches. A feature name without that statement does not count.
    3. Redesign. Rebuild the feature pipeline so that every feature at origin t is computable from information available at or before t. Prove it with an automated check, not by inspection.
    4. Re-run. Re-evaluate under a rolling-origin backtest at the 24-hour horizon.
    5. Compare. Report the corrected figure against the original, state the ratio between them, and write one paragraph an executive who approved the project on the original number can read.

    The rule that governs all of it¶

    At forecast origin t, the model may use any observation timestamped at or before t, and nothing else. Every failure in this exercise is a violation of that one sentence.

    The exact planted defects¶

    Facilitator reference. Do not distribute before the exercise.

    There are two independent classes of defect — three leaking features and one broken evaluation design — and participants must find both classes to score full marks on diagnosis. Finding the features and leaving the shuffle split in place is the most common partial answer.

    Defect class 1 — the leaking features¶

    All three are added in tools/leak_demo.py to a feature matrix that is otherwise clean:

    Xleak["roll_mean24_centred"]  = y.rolling(24, center=True, min_periods=1).mean()
Xleak["roll_max24_centred"]   = y.rolling(24, center=True, min_periods=1).max()
Xleak["demand_next_day_mean"] = y.shift(-24).rolling(6, min_periods=1).mean()
    Feature What it looks like What it actually contains
    roll_mean24_centred "the last day's average demand" The mean of y[t-12 … t+11]. Eleven of those twenty-four hours are after the forecast origin.
    roll_max24_centred "the last day's peak" The maximum over the same window. Worse than the mean, because a maximum is dominated by a single extreme value — so a future spike propagates into the feature almost undiluted.
    demand_next_day_mean An exogenous input "known" from a planning system mean(y[t+19 … t+24]). It contains the target itself, y[t+24], as one of six terms. This is not a subtle leak; it is the answer written into the question paper.

    The first two are the interesting ones. They are the leaks that survive code review, because the variable name is accurate about the window length and silent about its position.

    Defect class 2 — the broken evaluation design¶

    train_test_split(Xleak[ok], Y24[ok], test_size=.2, random_state=0, shuffle=True)

    A random shuffle over an autocorrelated hourly series places the hour before and the hour after most test observations into the training set. The model is not forecasting; it is interpolating between two known neighbours. This leaks even with a perfectly clean feature matrix — which is exactly what design B below is built to prove.

    What a clean pipeline does instead¶

    src/tayyar/features/build.py is the corrected reference, and it is already in the repository — participants who think to read it will find the fix in four lines:

    base = out[col].shift(1)                       # <-- the leakage guard
for w in windows:
    r = base.rolling(w, min_periods=max(2, w // 4), center=False)

    .shift(1) before the rolling window, center=False always. The module also ships assert_no_leakage(), which applies two checks: no feature correlates with the target above 0.9999, and — the check worth teaching — shuffling the future rows of the target must not change any past feature value. That second check is a genuine test of temporal integrity rather than a heuristic, and it catches the centred windows immediately.

    The measured numbers¶

    One model. One dataset. One target: demand 24 hours ahead. Only the evaluation design and one feature block change between the four rows. These are measured values from leak_results.json, reproducible by running python tools/leak_demo.py from the repository root.

    Design Split Features Day-ahead MAPE What it is
    A Random shuffle, 20% held out Leaky (three centred/future features added) 1.61% The number in the approval deck. Both defects present.
    B Random shuffle, 20% held out Clean 2.00% Isolates the split. Fixing the features alone still leaves a 2.00% figure that cannot be delivered.
    C Time-ordered, last 20% held out Clean 3.54% An honest single split. The first defensible number.
    D Rolling-origin backtest, 8 origins Clean 3.66% Decision-grade evidence. Per-origin range 2.31% to 4.89%.

    The headline: 3.66 / 1.61 = 2.27. The leaked design reported a model 2.3 times more accurate than it actually is. Operations reported three to five percent; design D says 3.66% with a per-origin range of 2.31% to 4.89%. The field observation and the honest backtest agree. The approval deck and reality do not.

    Three things in that table are worth drawing out in the debrief, and they are the reason there are four rows rather than two:

    B is the row that surprises people. Fixing the features and keeping the shuffle still yields 2.00% — a number that would still have got the project approved and would still have failed in production. Participants who only find the features have found roughly half the problem, and the half they found is the easier half.

    The gap from B to C is larger than the gap from A to B. 2.00 → 3.54 is 1.54 points; 1.61 → 2.00 is 0.39. On an autocorrelated series, the evaluation design leaks more than the features do. This inverts most people's prior, which is that leakage is a feature-engineering problem. It is primarily a validation-design problem.

    D is only slightly worse than C, and that is the point of D. The single time-ordered split gives 3.54% and the rolling-origin backtest gives 3.66% — close on average. But C is one number from one arbitrary cut point, and D is eight, with a spread from 2.31% to 4.89%. C cannot tell you that the model has weeks where it doubles its error. D can, and the operations report of "three to five percent" is legible only against D's spread.

    A second, independent measurement¶

    reference_results.json records the same effect at a different framing, from the main reference run:

    Design MAPE
    Shuffled split with a centred rolling window 1.21%
    Time-ordered split, 1-step-ahead target 1.40%
    Honest day-ahead (24-hour horizon) 2.33%

    This second table adds a distinct lesson worth thirty seconds in the debrief: even with a clean split and clean features, the horizon matters enormously. A 1-step-ahead evaluation reports 1.40% for a service that must deliver 2.33% at the horizon it actually serves. Reporting h+1 accuracy for an h+24 service is not leakage, but it is the same category of error — measuring something easier than the job.

    The subtlety worth teaching: where pandas centres an even window¶

    This is the technical heart of the exercise and it should not be asserted from the front. Make participants prove it.

    rolling(24, center=True) has to place a 24-element window around a single point, and 24 is even, so it cannot be symmetric. Pandas resolves this by taking [t-12, t+11] — twelve hours before, eleven after, plus the current hour. Not [t-11, t+12]. Most people, asked to guess, guess wrong, and almost nobody has checked.

    The consequence is direct: at forecast origin t, this feature has already read eleven hours of the future. For a next-hour target it contains the target itself. For the 24-hour-ahead target used in this exercise it does not literally contain y[t+24], but it contains eleven observations the forecaster cannot possibly have at 14:00 — and the model, quite reasonably, uses them.

    The proof, in six lines¶

    Have every pair run this and read the output aloud before they touch the model:

    import pandas as pd, numpy as np
s = pd.Series(np.arange(50, dtype=float))          # the value at index i IS i
r = s.rolling(24, center=True, min_periods=1)
print(r.min()[30], r.max()[30])       # -> 18.0 41.0   i.e. the window is [t-12, t+11]
print(s.rolling(25, center=True, min_periods=1).min()[30],
      s.rolling(25, center=True, min_periods=1).max()[30])   # -> 18.0 42.0  (odd: symmetric)
print(s.shift(1).rolling(24, min_periods=1).min()[30],
      s.shift(1).rolling(24, min_periods=1).max()[30])       # ->  6.0 29.0  (the clean version)

    Because the series holds its own index as its value, min and max over the window report the window's first and last positions directly. At t = 30 the centred 24-window spans 18 to 41 — eleven hours past the origin. The odd-length 25-window spans 18 to 42, genuinely symmetric. The .shift(1).rolling(24) version spans 6 to 29, ending strictly before t, which is the only one of the three that a forecaster may use.

    Three points to make once the output is on screen:

    1. The asymmetry is not a bug. It is a documented, arbitrary tie-break for even windows. Any convention would be wrong for forecasting; the problem is center=True at all.
    2. The magnitude of the leak depends on the window's parity in a way nobody expects. A 24-hour centred window leaks 11 hours; a 25-hour centred window leaks 12. Reasoning about this from memory is how the defect gets past review.
    3. The fix is not "use an odd window". The fix is center=False and .shift(1), and it is unconditional. There is no forecasting situation in which a centred window is correct.

    Facilitator note. Do not accept "I know how centring works" from a confident participant. Ask them, before they run the code, whether the 24-window is [t-12, t+11] or [t-11, t+12]. In most rooms the split is roughly even, which is itself the argument for running the check.

    Facilitator timing plan¶

    Thirty minutes of work, five of debrief. Pairs, at machines, with the repository checked out.

    Minutes Phase What the facilitator does
    0–3 Brief Read the scenario. Put the three questions on the board: what is the true accuracy, why did validation lie, what changes. Say that diagnosis outscores accuracy. Say nothing about leakage, centring, or splits. Resist every attempt to be asked what to look for.
    3–6 The window check Everyone runs the six-line snippet, before touching the model. Ask the room for a show of hands on [t-12, t+11] versus [t-11, t+12] first, record the split on the board, then let them run it. Three minutes, no discussion, straight into the next phase.
    6–16 Detect and identify Pairs work through tools/leak_demo.py and the feature matrix. Circulate. Give no answers; give questions instead. In escalating order of directness, for a pair that is stuck: "at 14:00 today, which rows of this dataframe exist?"; "pick your most important feature — write down which timestamps it reads"; "what does shuffle=True do to two adjacent hours?"; "there is a function in src/tayyar/features/ whose name tells you what it does." Do not go past the fourth question before minute 13. Note which pairs find the split defect as well as the feature defects — that distinction drives the debrief.
    16–25 Redesign and re-run Pairs rebuild the features with .shift(1) and center=False, run assert_no_leakage, then re-evaluate under a time-ordered split and a rolling-origin backtest. Most will reproduce something close to designs C and D; exact reproduction of 3.54 and 3.66 depends on their split and origin choices and should not be required. Watch for the pair that fixes the features, keeps the shuffle, gets ~2.0% and declares victory — that is design B, and it is the most instructive wrong answer in the room. If one appears, hold it for the debrief rather than correcting it in private.
    25–30 Write-up Individual, silent, five minutes. Each participant writes the comparison table and the executive paragraph. Enforce the silence — this is the assessed artefact and it must be individual work.
    30–35 Debrief Put all four measured designs on the board in order. Ask the room to predict the B row before revealing it; the underestimate is consistent and it makes the point better than any explanation. Then draw the two gaps: A→B is 0.39 points, B→C is 1.54. Say once, and only here: on an autocorrelated series the evaluation design leaks more than the features do. Show the D spread — 2.31% to 4.89% — against the operations report of three to five percent, and let the room notice that the honest backtest predicted the field result. Then, and not before, state the core lesson. Finish by pointing at the capstone anti-pattern list, where shuffle=True and centred rolling features each cap a criterion at 70%.

    If the room is ahead of schedule, the extension is: quantify how much of the A→B gap comes from each of the three leaking features by removing them one at a time. roll_max24_centred usually contributes more than roll_mean24_centred, and asking why before measuring is a good thirty seconds.

    If the room is behind, cut the redesign phase to fixing the features only, and present designs C and D from leak_results.json rather than having participants generate them. The diagnosis is the assessed skill; the re-run is the confirmation.

    Scoring rubric¶

    Total 100. Marked on the individual write-up, with the pair's working available as evidence.

    Leak diagnosis notes — 40%¶

    Band Description
    36–40 Both defect classes identified. All three leaking features named, each with a statement of which future observations it reads — including the correct [t-12, t+11] span for the centred windows. The shuffle split is identified as an independent defect, with the autocorrelation argument stated. The relative size of the two effects is noticed and commented on.
    28–35 Both classes identified and all three features named, but the window span is described loosely ("it sees the future") rather than precisely, or the relative magnitude is not addressed.
    20–27 All three features found; the shuffle split missed or mentioned only in passing. This is the common ceiling and it should be applied firmly — a fix that leaves the split in place ships a 2.00% number that is still wrong.
    10–19 Only the obvious defect (demand_next_day_mean) found. The centred windows, which are the ones that survive code review, were not recognised as leaks.
    0–9 Leakage asserted without evidence, or the diagnosis is "the model overfitted".

    Correct fixes — 40%¶

    Band Description
    36–40 Feature pipeline rebuilt with .shift(1) and center=False throughout. Temporal integrity proved automatically — assert_no_leakage or an equivalent check, with the future-shuffle test present, not merely a correlation threshold. Evaluation moved to a time-ordered split and then to a rolling-origin backtest with training data at every origin ending at or before that origin.
    28–35 Features and split both corrected and the backtest runs, but integrity is argued by inspection rather than demonstrated by a check that would fail if the leak returned.
    20–27 Features fixed, evaluation still shuffled; or evaluation fixed, one leaking feature left in.
    10–19 Leaking features deleted rather than replaced, so the model loses the legitimate rolling information it should have had. The pipeline is now honest and needlessly weaker, and the write-up does not notice.
    0–9 No working fix, or a "fix" that changes the reported number without changing what the features read.

    Honest re-measurement — 20%¶

    Band Description
    18–20 Corrected figure reported at the 24-hour horizon under a rolling-origin backtest, with the per-origin spread, not just the mean. The ratio to the original stated explicitly. The executive paragraph states the model is roughly 2.3 times less accurate than reported, without hedging, and says what it is nonetheless still good for.
    14–17 Corrected figure and ratio reported honestly; the spread omitted, so the write-up cannot explain the "three to five percent" field report.
    8–13 A corrected figure is reported but framed defensively — the degradation is attributed to drift, to data changes, or to production conditions rather than to the evaluation.
    0–7 The corrected figure is presented alongside the original without acknowledging that the original was wrong; or the worse number is quietly not mentioned.

    Overriding rule. A write-up that reports a corrected figure worse than the true one, but diagnoses correctly and measures honestly, scores above a write-up that reports a better number with a thinner diagnosis. Say this at the briefing and mean it. The exercise exists to make honest measurement the thing that pays.

    What this connects to¶

    • Capstone anti-pattern flags. train_test_split(shuffle=True) and centred rolling features each cap a capstone criterion at 70%. This exercise is where participants learn why before it costs them marks.
    • Milestone M-B, end of Day 2, gates on a leak check passing. Any pair that struggles here should be checked against their own Lab 4 output before Day 3 closes.
    • src/tayyar/features/build.py is the reference implementation and its module docstring names the same three traps this exercise plants. Participants who read it during the exercise have not cheated — they have done the correct thing, which is to look for whether the problem is already solved somewhere in the codebase. Say so in the debrief.
    • figures/08_leakage.png is the four-bar chart of designs A–D, generated by tools/leak_demo.py. Show it only in the debrief, after the numbers have been predicted.
