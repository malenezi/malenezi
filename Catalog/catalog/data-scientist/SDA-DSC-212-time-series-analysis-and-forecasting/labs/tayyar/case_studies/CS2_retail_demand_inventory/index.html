    Case Study 2 — Retail Demand and the Replenishment Decision¶

    SDA-DSC-212 · Time Series Analysis and Forecasting · Facilitator brief

    At a glance¶
    Primary module fit M4 — Feature-Based ML Forecasting (multi-series, rolling features, leakage)
    Secondary fit M5 (asymmetric loss motivates quantiles), M6 (WAPE, MASE, RMSSE and horizon alignment)
    LOs exercised LO3 primary; LO5 and LO6 in the debrief
    Suggested slot Day 2, Hour 5 — the first half of the M5 slot, as the motivation for probabilistic forecasting
    Alternative slot Day 2, Hour 3, as a contrast case inside M4 theory (drop the plenary to 8 minutes)
    Duration 40 minutes
    Group size 3–4 participants
    Data source Kaggle Store Sales — Time Series Forecasting, or the M5 Forecasting dataset. Runs as a paper case with no data if connectivity is unavailable. See Data below.

    This case exists to break one habit the electricity series encourages: that the model with the smallest error is the model you should ship. In retail it usually is not, and the reason is arithmetic rather than opinion.

    Scenario¶

    A grocery chain operates 54 stores across the Kingdom and carries roughly 33 product families per store. Replenishment for the ambient categories runs on a weekly cycle with a lead time of seven days: the order placed on Sunday arrives the following Sunday and has to cover the week after that. Fresh categories run daily with a two-day lead time. Nothing about the forecast horizon is a modelling preference — it is set by the logistics contract.

    Reem Al-Dossari is the demand planner for the ambient categories. Every Sunday morning she approves about 1,800 store-family order quantities. She does not approve them individually; a planning system proposes quantities from a forecast and she overrides the ones that look wrong, which in practice means the ones a store manager has complained about. Her performance is measured on two numbers that pull against each other: on-shelf availability, which the commercial team watches, and shrink and markdown, which finance watches.

    The analytics team has replaced the incumbent moving-average forecast with a gradient-boosted model. Aggregate MAE across all store-family series improved by 18%. Three months later, Reem's availability has not improved and her markdown has risen. She wants to know why a better forecast made her decision worse.

    The decision she makes differently is the order quantity per store-family for the coming week — specifically, how much cover to carry above the expected demand. Today that buffer is a flat percentage applied to every series. What she actually needs is a buffer that differs by series and by week, and a forecast that tells her which weeks are uncertain.

    The decision question¶

    How many units of each store-family should be ordered this Sunday to cover the week after next, given a seven-day lead time?

    Business context and the cost of being wrong¶

    The two errors have different names, different owners and different sizes, and that asymmetry is the entire case.

    Take one ambient store-family, using illustrative figures that should be stated as teaching numbers:

    Value
    Mean weekly demand 60 units
    Standard deviation of weekly demand 18 units
    Unit margin on a sale SAR 12
    Cost of an unsold unit (holding + eventual markdown) SAR 4

    The critical ratio is q* = c_under / (c_under + c_over) = 12 / (12 + 4) = **0.75**. The cost-minimising order is the 75th percentile of the demand distribution, not its mean.

    Under a normal approximation that is 60 + 0.674 × 18 ≈ 72 units. Ordering the mean instead — which is exactly what an MAE-optimal point forecast delivers — leaves the shelf short in about half of all weeks rather than a quarter, and costs 12 × E[shortfall] on each of them. Across 1,800 store-families the difference is not a rounding error, and it is invisible to every symmetric accuracy metric on the dashboard.

    This is why Reem's MAE improved and her outcome did not. A model tuned to the conditional mean is optimising a loss function that her business does not have. The correction is not a better point model; it is a quantile forecast at the ratio her cost structure implies, and a different ratio for a fresh category where the unsold unit is written off entirely (c_over rises, q* falls, and the optimal buffer shrinks).

    What makes this series hard¶

    It is not one series, it is thousands. Fifty-four stores times thirty-three families is around 1,780 series, most of them short, many of them sparse. Fitting an individual model per series is neither affordable nor statistically sensible for the sparse ones. This is the multi-series problem: a global model with a series identifier as a categorical feature, learning shared structure across series while retaining series-level level and shape.

    Intermittent demand. A meaningful fraction of store-family combinations sell zero units in most periods. On a zero-heavy series MAPE is undefined and MAE is minimised by forecasting zero forever — an all-zeros forecast can be the accuracy winner and is worthless. This is the case for RMSSE or MASE against a seasonal-naive denominator, and for Croston-style methods being mentioned by name even though the course does not teach them.

    Promotions are an exogenous driver that is known in the future. Promotional calendars are planned weeks ahead, which makes them one of the rare covariates genuinely available at serve time — unlike weather in CS1, which is itself a forecast. That makes promotions high-value features and high-risk ones: a model trained with an accurate promotional flag and served with a provisional one degrades quietly.

    Calendar effects that are not weekly. Pay-cycle effects near month end; Ramadan, where category behaviour diverges sharply (staples and dates rise, prepared foods shift by time of day); Eid, where the pattern is a pre-holiday surge followed by a trough; back-to-school. These are multiplicative and category-specific, and a single global holiday dummy will average them into uselessness.

    Lag leakage, in a form specific to panel data. Two variants bite here that do not arise in a single series: a scaler or target encoder fitted on the whole panel, which lets one store's future inform another store's past; and a lag computed after a groupby that has not been sorted within group. Both produce plausible, wrong numbers.

    Horizon and lead time must match. The forecast has to cover the lead-time demand — the sum of demand over the seven days between the order arriving and the next order arriving — not the demand on any single day. A daily model evaluated on daily error tells you nothing directly about the quantity that matters; the error of the sum over the lead time is the relevant quantity, and it is not the sum of the daily errors because the errors are correlated.

    Structural change from range decisions. A product delisted, a store refitted, a new private-label line introduced. Series start, stop and step for commercial reasons that no time-series diagnostic will explain.

    Technical agenda¶

    1. Frame the loss before touching a model (M5, M7). Derive the critical ratio from the margin and holding cost. State the quantile the business needs. Write it down before any model is fitted — the whole case turns on this happening first.
    2. Build the panel and the leakage guards (M4). Long format with store_id, family, date. Lags and rolling statistics computed within series, on a sorted index, with .shift(1) before every rolling window. Assert that no feature at time t uses an observation at or after t.
    3. Engineer the calendar and promotional features (M4). Day of week, day of month, pay-cycle proximity, holiday flags with lead and lag windows (the day before Eid matters more than Eid itself), promotion flags, and promotion flags for the following week where they are genuinely known at order time.
    4. Fit a global model against per-series baselines (M4). One LightGBM across the panel with series identifiers as categoricals, versus seasonal-naive per series and a simple per-series ETS. Test whether the global model helps the sparse series or only the dense ones — this is usually the most surprising result of the session.
    5. Choose metrics that survive zeros (M6). WAPE for aggregation across series of different scales; MASE or RMSSE against a seasonal-naive denominator; MAE per series for diagnostics. Demonstrate concretely that MAPE is unusable on the sparse series rather than asserting it.
    6. Re-evaluate at the decision horizon (M6). Aggregate the forecast over the seven-day lead window and score that. Compare the ranking of models under daily error against the ranking under lead-time error, and note whether the champion changes.
    7. Produce the quantile the decision needs (M5). Refit at q = 0.75 with pinball loss, or calibrate an interval and take the upper quantile. Score with pinball loss at that quantile, not with MAE, and compute the resulting inventory outcome — units short and units unsold — for both the mean forecast and the quantile forecast.

    Constraints¶

    Constraint What it does to model choice
    Lead time is fixed by contract The horizon is not tunable. A model that is excellent at h+1 and mediocre at h+7 is mediocre for this decision.
    1,800 decisions a week, one planner The output must be trustworthy enough to auto-approve most lines. That means the model's uncertainty has to be usable as an exception filter: flag the 5% of lines where the interval is widest, and let Reem spend her morning on those.
    Explainability at the line level When a store manager challenges an order quantity, the planner needs a reason in one sentence. Global-model feature importance does not answer "why 72 for this store this week". Some form of local attribution, or a simple decomposition, is an operational requirement rather than a nicety.
    Serve-time promotional data Promotional plans are provisional until locked. If the model is trained on final plans and served on provisional ones, the accuracy reported in the backtest is not the accuracy delivered.
    Cold-start series New products and new stores have no history. Whatever is chosen must have a defined behaviour for a series with three observations, and that behaviour must be stated rather than discovered in production.
    Retraining cadence 1,800 series retrained weekly is cheap for one global model and expensive for 1,800 individual ones. This usually decides the case on its own, and groups should say so rather than pretending accuracy decided it.

    Facilitation plan¶

    Forty minutes.

    Minutes Phase What the facilitator does
    0–4 Setup Give the scenario in three sentences and stop at the puzzle: MAE improved 18%, availability did not move, markdown rose. Ask the room for one-line hypotheses and write four or five on the board without evaluating any of them. Do not reveal the critical ratio.
    4–8 Framing the loss Hand each group the cost card — margin SAR 12, unsold-unit cost SAR 4, mean 60, sd 18 — and ask a single question: "how many units should she order?" Nothing else. Let them argue. Most groups will answer 60 within a minute and then start to doubt it.
    8–22 Small-group work Groups compute the critical ratio, the implied order quantity, and the expected cost of ordering the mean instead. Then they extend it: a fresh category where the unsold unit is a total write-off, and a high-margin low-volume line. Circulate with one question per group: "what does your ratio become if the item is fresh?"; "what is the ratio for a series that sells 0.3 units a week?"; "which of your model's metrics would have caught this?"
    22–33 Plenary Two minutes per group at the board. Require each group to write its ratio and its order quantity, not describe them. Collect the ratios in a single column — the spread across category types is the lesson, and it is more persuasive on the board than in speech. Then ask the question the case is built on: which accuracy metric, of the ones on the dashboard, would have distinguished the good model from the bad one? The honest answer is none of them, and it is worth letting the room reach that conclusion rather than announcing it.
    33–40 Debrief Connect forward, not backward. This is the last thing before M5, so close on the sentence that opens M5: the point forecast answers the wrong question, and the quantile answers the right one. Then name the two retail-specific traps to carry into the lab — intermittency breaking MAPE, and panel leakage through a globally fitted scaler.

    If the session runs with data on machines, replace minutes 8–22 with the hands-on variant: groups compute WAPE and MASE on a supplied panel extract, then compute units-short and units-unsold under a mean forecast and a q=0.75 forecast. This is stronger but needs 55 minutes, not 40.

    Discussion questions¶

    1. Reem's MAE improved by 18% and her outcome got worse. Is the new model worse than the old one? A good answer separates the model from the decision rule. The model may be strictly better as an estimator of the conditional mean while being worse as an input to an order quantity, because the decision rule that consumes it assumes the input is something else. The strongest answers point out that the incumbent moving average was probably biased upward on volatile series, and that its bias was accidentally doing the job of a service-level buffer — which is a common and uncomfortable finding in real replacements.

    2. What is the right critical ratio for a fresh category, and what does that do to the forecast you need? A good answer recognises that c_over rises sharply when the unsold unit is written off rather than marked down, which pushes q* below 0.5 — so the cost-minimising order is below the mean. It should notice the uncomfortable implication that the correct policy deliberately runs out of stock some of the time, and that this is a commercial decision the planner cannot make alone.

    3. On a series that sells in 15 weeks out of 52, what metric would you report, and what would you refuse to report? A good answer rules out MAPE on the grounds that it is undefined at zero and that percentage errors on tiny denominators dominate any average. It proposes MASE or RMSSE against a seasonal-naive denominator, notes that MAE alone is minimised by an all-zero forecast, and — the mark of a strong answer — questions whether a point forecast is the right output at all for a series like this, as opposed to a probability of a non-zero week and an expected size when non-zero.

    4. When should this chain fit one global model, and when should it fit 1,780 individual ones? A good answer argues from data volume per series and from maintenance cost, not from sophistication. Global models help short and sparse series by borrowing structure, and can hurt a long, idiosyncratic, high-volume series that has its own dynamics. A defensible design is often a hybrid: a global model for the tail, individual models for the twenty series that account for most of the revenue. The best answers note that the maintenance argument is doing more work than the accuracy argument, and say so.

    5. Promotions are known in advance — until they are not. How would you handle a model whose most important feature is provisional at serve time? A good answer proposes measuring the degradation rather than assuming it: backtest with the provisional plan as it stood at order time, not with the final plan as recorded. It should consider training on provisional data if that is what will be served, and should treat the divergence between provisional and final plans as a monitorable quantity in its own right.

    6. The forecast is for a seven-day lead-time total, but the model is fitted daily. Where does that go wrong? A good answer recognises that lead-time demand is a sum of correlated errors, so its variance is not seven times the daily variance, and that an interval built by naively scaling the daily interval will be wrong in a direction that depends on the sign of the error correlation. It should propose either forecasting the aggregate directly or simulating paths and aggregating them, and should note that the model ranking can change between the two evaluations.

    Expected outputs¶

    1. A one-page cost card: margin, over-stock cost, derived critical ratio, and the implied quantile, for three different category types.
    2. The order quantity each group would place for the worked example, with its arithmetic shown.
    3. A metric recommendation: which metrics the planning dashboard should carry, which it should drop, and one sentence of justification for each.
    4. A statement of the model architecture the group recommends — global, per-series, or hybrid — with the constraint that decided it named explicitly.
    5. One paragraph explaining to Reem, in language with no statistical vocabulary in it, why the number the system proposes is higher than the number it expects to sell.

    Item 5 is the hardest and the most valuable. It rehearses LO6 directly.

    Common failure modes in this case¶

    Failure mode What it looks like Facilitator's redirect
    Ordering the mean The group computes an excellent forecast and orders exactly that many units, without ever asking what the forecast is for. "You have ordered 60. Demand was 78. What did that cost, and what would ordering 72 have cost when demand was 45?" Make them do both arithmetics; the asymmetry appears immediately.
    Reaching for a bigger model The response to poor outcomes is a neural network, or more features. The loss function is never examined. "Suppose your new model is perfect at estimating the mean. Does Reem's problem go away?" It does not, and that is the fastest route to the actual lesson.
    MAPE on intermittent series The evaluation script runs, produces a number in the thousands of percent or a divide-by-zero, and the group works around it by dropping the zero rows. Dropping the zeros drops the hardest part of the problem and inflates every metric. Ask what fraction of rows were dropped, then ask what the model's accuracy is on the rows they threw away.
    Panel leakage through global preprocessing A StandardScaler or target encoder fitted on the entire panel, or lags computed without sorting within group. The backtest looks excellent. Ask them to prove temporal integrity the same way Practical Assessment 2 does: shuffle the future rows and confirm no past feature value changes. It usually fails on the first attempt.
    Evaluating at the wrong horizon Daily h+1 accuracy is reported and the seven-day lead time is never modelled. "The order arrives next Sunday. What does your h+1 accuracy tell Reem about the week after that?" Nothing, and groups see it at once.
    One holiday dummy for everything Ramadan, Eid and back-to-school share a single is_holiday flag, and the model learns their average, which describes none of them. Have them plot two categories through the same Ramadan. The curves move in opposite directions, which no shared flag can express.

    Data¶

    This case has no course-provided dataset. Three options, in order of preference:

    Option What it is Preparation
    Kaggle Store Sales — Time Series Forecasting Daily unit sales by store and product family for an Ecuadorian grocery chain, with accompanying oil-price, holiday and promotion tables. Familiar shape: store identifier, product family, date, sales, promotion indicator. Requires a Kaggle account to download; check the competition page for the current licence and redistribution terms before handing it out. Download and stage on the training machines before the session. Take a subset — 5 stores, 8 families, two years — so the panel fits in memory and a LightGBM fit takes seconds rather than minutes.
    M5 Forecasting (Walmart) dataset Daily unit sales for a large hierarchical retail panel with calendar and price tables, and a substantial fraction of intermittent series. Richer than needed for a 40-minute case, and the better choice if the case is extended into a lab. Check the current licence terms on the competition page. Subset aggressively. The full panel is far too large for a classroom exercise and the intermittency lesson is fully visible in a few hundred series.
    Paper case Run the session on the cost card alone, with no data. None. This is the default if connectivity is uncertain, and it loses less than instructors expect — the critical-ratio argument is the point of the case, and it is arithmetic, not computation.

    A note on substituting the course data. Do not try to run this case on ksa_grid_demand.csv. Electricity demand is dense, smooth and strictly positive; it has none of the intermittency, promotional response or panel structure that this case exists to teach. If a retail panel is genuinely unavailable, run the paper case rather than a bad analogy.
