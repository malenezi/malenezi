    Case Study 1 — Day-Ahead Load Forecasting for the Central Operating Area¶

    SDA-DSC-212 · Time Series Analysis and Forecasting · Facilitator brief

    At a glance¶
    Primary module fit M7 — Forecasting Case Study (integrates M1–M6)
    Secondary fit M3 (SARIMAX vs ETS), M5 (interval calibration), M6 (rolling-origin evidence)
    LOs exercised LO6 primary; LO1–LO5 as integration
    Suggested slot Day 3, Hour 4 — 50 minutes, immediately before capstone demos
    Alternative slot Day 1, Hour 1 as a 15-minute framing of the golden thread (scenario + decision question only)
    Duration 50 minutes
    Group size 3–4 participants; 5–6 groups in a room of 20
    Data source data/ksa_grid_demand.csv, data/ksa_calendar.csv, data/temp_forecast_14d.csv (this repository)

    This is the primary case. It is not a detour from Tayyar — it is Tayyar, stated as a business problem rather than a modelling exercise, and it is the last thing participants discuss before they defend their own capstone.

    Scenario¶

    The Central Operating Area covers Riyadh and its industrial belt and is the largest of the four areas the national grid operator dispatches. Its demand in the course dataset runs from about 18,500 MW on a January night to 57,852 MW on the evening of 20 July 2023 — a factor of just over three between the annual trough and the annual peak, almost all of it cooling load. Annual mean demand rose from 31,744 MW in 2021 to 34,630 MW in 2023.

    Nouf Al-Harthi is the day-ahead scheduling engineer in the operations-planning unit. Every afternoon at 14:00 she publishes the next day's hourly demand schedule. Generation units are committed against it: which plants run, which are held spinning, which are cold. Units with slow start-up curves cannot be recalled once the schedule is issued, and the interconnector schedule with the neighbouring area is agreed on the same numbers. If Nouf's schedule is too low, the control room buys expensive fast-start capacity inside the day or, in the worst case, sheds feeders. If it is too high, gas is burned turning turbines that nothing needs.

    Nouf currently uses a similar-day method: take the same weekday two weeks ago, scale by a temperature ratio, and adjust by hand for Ramadan and holidays. It works, and its weakness is exactly where it costs most — the first hot week of May, the days either side of Eid, and the afternoon of a heatwave. She has asked for a forecasting service. She has also said, plainly, that she will not put a number in front of the control room that she cannot explain, and that a model which is right on average but wrong on the four hardest weeks of the year is worse than the method she has now.

    The decision she makes differently depending on the answer is concrete: the reserve margin she carries between 12:00 and 18:00 tomorrow. A tighter, better-calibrated interval lets her carry less spinning reserve for the same risk. A wider or miscalibrated one costs money every day, or costs a feeder once.

    The decision question¶

    At 14:00 today, how much generation capacity should the operator prepare for each hour tomorrow?

    Business context and the cost of being wrong¶

    The two errors are not symmetric, and the whole design of the service follows from that.

    Take the 2023 system peak of 57,852 MW. A 1% error at peak is 579 MW — roughly one mid-size gas turbine. Using illustrative cost figures (participants should be told these are teaching numbers, not the operator's published tariffs):

    Over-forecast by 579 MW Under-forecast by 579 MW
    What happens 579 MW held spinning across a 6-hour afternoon window that nothing dispatches 579 MW of capacity short at the peak hour
    Illustrative unit cost ~USD 40 / MWh incremental fuel and O&M on idle spinning reserve ~USD 5,000 / MWh value of lost load
    Cost of the error 579 MW × 6 h × 40 = ~USD 139,000 per day 579 MW × 1 h × 5,000 = ~USD 2.9 million

    The ratio is roughly 20:1 per hour of exposure, and that ratio has a direct technical consequence. The newsvendor solution to an asymmetric loss is the quantile q* = c_under / (c_under + c_over). With 5,000 and 40 that is 0.992. This is why an operator schedules to something near the upper tail, not to the median, and why a service that reports only a point forecast is answering a question nobody asked. It is also why a symmetric metric — MAE, RMSE, MAPE — cannot by itself select the production model for this decision.

    Nouf's real ask is therefore not "the most accurate model". It is a calibrated upper quantile she can defend, plus an honest statement of where it fails.

    What makes this series hard¶

    Three seasonal periods at once. Daily (m = 24), weekly (m = 168), annual (m ≈ 8,766). In the course data MSTL puts daily seasonal strength at 0.972 and weekly at 0.610 — the daily cycle is nearly deterministic, the weekly one is real but far weaker. Any single-seasonality SARIMA specification has to choose one and let the others leak into the residuals.

    The weekend is Friday and Saturday. In pandas dayofweek (Monday = 0) that is [4, 5]. Every cohort produces at least one model that has quietly assumed Saturday–Sunday.

    Weather is not a linear driver. Demand responds to a balance point: above roughly 21 °C cooling load rises steeply with temperature and slightly convexly; below about 14 °C a much milder heating response appears; in between the response is nearly flat. Monthly mean temperature in the dataset runs from 13.8 °C in January to 38.2 °C in July, with an absolute maximum of 49.5 °C. A model given raw temp_c and a linear term will fit the middle of the year and miss both ends. Cooling- and heating-degree features (cdd = max(temp − 21, 0), hdd = max(14 − temp, 0)) encode the physics directly.

    Temperature is exogenous, and at serve time it is a forecast, not an observation. This is the single most important operational point in the case. At 14:00 today, tomorrow's temperature is itself a prediction. data/temp_forecast_14d.csv carries 48 weekly origins through 2023 with realistic error growth: mean absolute error 0.79 °C at leads of 1–24 h, rising to 1.08 °C at 24–72 h and 2.05 °C beyond a week. A model validated on realised temperature and served on forecast temperature is optimistic by an amount nobody has measured.

    Ramadan is a shape change, not a level change. In the generative design daytime demand (06:00–15:00) falls by about 7% and the post-Iftar window (19:00–01:00) rises by about 7.5%. A single is_ramadan dummy captures none of this — it needs an interaction with hour-of-day. Eid drops the level by roughly 9.5% for four to five days; National Day by about 4.5% for one. The Hijri dates move about eleven days earlier each Gregorian year, so a model that learned "April is a Ramadan month" from 2021–2022 will apply it wrongly to 2023.

    A structural break. On 1 October 2022 a large industrial customer connects and the level steps up by about 3.1%. It is not a trend, not seasonality, and not an outlier. It is a permanent regime change in the middle of the training window, and it is why the stationarity report in reference_results.json reads ADF p = 0.026 (stationary) against KPSS p = 0.01 (not stationary) — the classic difference-stationary verdict caused by a level shift.

    Data-quality traps in the raw file. ksa_grid_demand.csv has 26,235 rows against a true 26,280 hours: 64 missing hours in 34 runs (the longest is 5 hours), 19 duplicated timestamps, 7 sensor zeros in demand_mw, and rows that are not monotonic in time — 192 positions where the timestamp goes backwards. A read_csv followed straight into a model will train on a series that is silently out of order, and a log transform will fail or produce -inf on the zeros.

    Technical agenda¶

    What participants actually investigate, in order:

    1. Build a defensible index (M1). Read the raw file, sort, de-duplicate with a stated rule, reindex onto a complete tz-aware hourly DatetimeIndex in Asia/Riyadh so the 64 gaps become explicit NaN, and record a gap policy. Decide what a zero means and defend the decision.
    2. Decompose and choose the transform (M1). MSTL with periods [24, 168]. Report seasonal strengths. Test whether the seasonal swing scales with the level (in the reference run the level–swing correlation is 0.937, which argues for multiplicative structure and therefore a log transform).
    3. Reconcile ADF and KPSS, and locate the break (M2). Run both tests, reconcile the verdicts, choose (d, D) — the reference solution reaches stationarity at (d=0, D=1) on the seasonal difference — and decide separately how to handle the October 2022 step: dummy, split sample, or ignore with justification.
    4. Fit the classical candidates (M3). Seasonal-naive baseline; SARIMAX with temperature and calendar regressors; ETS. Compare like for like on a common held-out window. Read the residual diagnostics honestly — in the reference run SARIMAX beats ETS on every point metric (MAPE 2.05% vs 2.68%) but its Ljung-Box p-value is 0.000, meaning structure remains in the residuals and the model is under-specified.
    5. Reduce the problem to a supervised matrix (M4). Lags, .shift(1)-guarded rolling statistics, Fourier terms for the three periods, calendar flags, degree-day features. Assert no leakage before fitting anything. Interpret feature importance by family — the reference build splits gain roughly 33% rolling, 29% lag, 26% Fourier, 7% calendar, 5% weather.
    6. Produce and calibrate an interval (M5). Quantile LightGBM plus split-conformal calibration. The reference run moves 90% coverage from 0.786 to 0.902 at the cost of widening the mean interval from 2,418 MW to 3,235 MW — and conditional coverage in the 12:00–18:00 peak window is still only 0.845. That gap is the case's most instructive number.
    7. Backtest, then choose (M6, M7). Sixty rolling origins at a 24-hour horizon. Report MASE against seasonal-naive, a percentage metric, coverage and pinball loss. Run Diebold-Mariano against the runner-up. Then apply the constraints below and write a decision record with flip-conditions.

    Constraints¶

    Constraint What it does to model choice
    Publication deadline The schedule is issued at 14:00. Feature build, fit and forecast must complete inside a short window with room for a failed run. Refitting a large-m seasonal ARIMA at every origin does not fit; a pre-fitted model with a fast forecast step, or a gradient-boosted model with a cached feature store, does.
    Explainability Nouf must narrate the number to a control-room shift engineer and, eventually, to a regulator. A component story ("trend plus daily cycle plus 3,100 MW of cooling load at 44 °C") is worth real accuracy. Feature importance is not the same thing and must not be presented as causal.
    Serve-time data availability A SARIMAX with temperature cannot be served without a temperature forecast for every hour of tomorrow. If the weather feed fails at 13:40, what runs? The service needs a degraded-mode fallback that uses no exogenous input.
    Cost asymmetry Selection must be driven by an asymmetric or quantile loss, not by MAE. A model that wins on MAE and loses on upper-tail pinball loss is the wrong model for this decision.
    Maintenance A feature pipeline is a standing liability with an owner and an on-call rota. A 0.2 percentage-point MAPE gain that costs a permanent engineering commitment is a bad trade for a unit of this size, and participants should be willing to say so.
    Known-weak weeks Eid weeks and the first heatwave of the season are where the method being replaced already fails. A replacement that also fails there has not solved the problem, whatever its annual average says.

    Facilitation plan¶

    Fifty minutes. The facilitator's job is to keep groups on the decision and off the leaderboard.

    Minutes Phase What the facilitator does
    0–5 Setup Read the decision question aloud. Put the 579 MW arithmetic and the 20:1 ratio on the board. State the constraint list without discussing it. Assign each group a role, not a model: Group 1 argues for SARIMAX, Group 2 for LightGBM+CQR, Group 3 for ETS, Group 4 for seasonal-naive-plus-conformal. Say explicitly that groups are assigned, not choosing — the point is to make the strongest case for a position, including one they disagree with.
    5–20 Small-group work Groups build the case for their assigned family using the backtest numbers they generated in Lab 6 and the constraint table. Circulate. Ask one question per group and move on: to the SARIMAX group, "what runs at 13:40 if the weather feed is down?"; to the LightGBM group, "who owns the feature pipeline in year two?"; to the ETS group, "show me the Eid week"; to the naive group, "what is the interval and how did you calibrate it?" Do not answer. Do not correct a wrong number yet — note it for the debrief.
    20–34 Plenary — the advocacy round Three minutes per group, strictly timed, standing. Rule: each group must state one condition under which its own recommendation should be abandoned. Hold every group to it; a group that cannot name a flip-condition has not finished thinking. After the last group, ask the room for a show of hands and record the split on the board without comment.
    34–44 Plenary — the constraint sweep Take the constraints one at a time and ask which recommendations survive each. Deliberately reorder the weights halfway through: "the regulator has just asked for the model's reasoning in writing" — then ask who changes their vote. The teaching point is that the answer moves with the constraint weights and that the weights must therefore be written down, which is exactly what DECISION_RECORD.md demands.
    44–50 Debrief Correct the numbers you noted while circulating, briefly and without naming groups. Then state the one thing to carry into the capstone: the deliverable is a recommendation with evidence and flip-conditions, not a model. Show the conditional coverage figure — 0.902 marginal, 0.845 in the peak window — and let it sit for a moment before closing.

    Discussion questions¶

    1. Nouf will not publish a number she cannot explain. Does that constraint have a price, and what is it here? A good answer names the accuracy gap between the interpretable candidate and the ML candidate from the group's own backtest, converts it to MW at the peak, and then argues either that the gap is small enough to buy the explainability or that it is large enough to justify investing in an explanation layer instead. An answer that treats explainability as free, or as a soft preference to be overruled by MAPE, has missed the point.

    2. The service is validated on realised temperature but served on forecast temperature. How much of the reported accuracy is real? A good answer proposes measuring it rather than estimating it: re-run the backtest substituting temp_forecast_14d.csv at the matching lead, and report the degradation. It should note that the day-ahead lead has only about 0.79 °C mean absolute error, so the effect may be modest at h+24 and severe at h+168, and that the interval — not just the point forecast — must widen to absorb weather uncertainty. The strongest answers observe that this is a form of training–serving skew and belongs in the monitoring contract.

    3. Conditional coverage in the 12:00–18:00 window is 0.845 against a marginal 0.902. Is the service fit to publish? A good answer takes a position and pays for it. Either: no, because the hours that miss are exactly the hours the reserve decision is made, and marginal coverage is the wrong guarantee for an hour-specific decision — so calibrate conditionally, by hour block. Or: yes, provisionally, with the shortfall documented and an operational buffer applied to peak hours until conditional calibration ships. Both are defensible; "0.902 is inside 0.88–0.92 so we pass" is not.

    4. How should the October 2022 structural break be handled, and what would change your mind? A good answer enumerates the options — a step dummy, discarding pre-break data, a shorter training window, letting the model learn it through recent lags — and evaluates them against the cost of each. Discarding pre-break data removes the only two summers in the record, which matters more for a summer-peaking series than the bias from the step does. A good answer names that trade-off explicitly rather than picking an option by habit.

    5. If the asymmetric cost ratio is 20:1, why not simply forecast the 99th percentile every hour? A good answer works through what happens: the interval becomes so wide that the reserve is effectively fixed, the forecast stops informing the decision, and the daily over-forecast cost compounds into a figure comparable with the rare shedding event it is avoiding. It should also question the 5,000 USD/MWh figure itself — the value of lost load is a policy input, not a measurement, and the whole quantile choice hangs off it. The best answers ask who owns that number and observe that it should appear in the decision record as an assumption, not a constant.

    6. What single monitoring signal would tell you first that this service has stopped working? A good answer prefers a leading signal to a lagging one, and prefers a decision-relevant signal to a statistical one — rolling conditional coverage in the peak window, or rolling MASE against the seasonal-naive baseline, will move before annual MAPE does. It should name a threshold and what happens when the threshold is breached, because a monitor with no action attached is decoration.

    Expected outputs¶

    Each group hands in, on one side of paper or one slide:

    1. A named recommendation: one model family, for this decision, at this horizon.
    2. The evidence: three to five numbers from the group's own backtest, including at least one probabilistic metric and one comparison against a named baseline.
    3. The constraint weighting actually applied, written as weights or as an ordering, with the reasoning for the top constraint.
    4. Two flip-conditions — specific, measurable events that would reverse the recommendation.
    5. A degraded-mode fallback: what the service publishes at 14:00 when its primary input is missing.
    6. One sentence naming where the model loses.

    Item 6 is not optional and is graded as heavily as item 1.

    Common failure modes in this case¶

    Failure mode What it looks like Facilitator's redirect
    Leaderboard thinking The group ranks candidates by MAPE and stops. The constraint table is never opened. "Your winner is 0.3 points ahead. Show me the Diebold-Mariano test. Now show me who maintains it in 2027." Force the constraint sweep before any recommendation is spoken aloud.
    Point forecast only The recommendation is a single hourly number. The reserve margin is never mentioned. "Nouf has your forecast. Tell her how much reserve to carry." The question cannot be answered without a quantile, and groups discover that faster by being asked than by being told.
    Realised temperature treated as available The service assumes tomorrow's temperature is known because the training data contains it. "It is 14:00. Where does tomorrow's 15:00 temperature come from?" Then point them at temp_forecast_14d.csv and the 0.79 °C day-ahead error.
    Ramadan as a single dummy One binary flag, no hour interaction; the model under-forecasts every Iftar evening and over-forecasts every Ramadan morning. Ask the group to plot the average hourly profile inside and outside Ramadan on the same axes. The two curves cross, and no additive flag can represent that.
    Coverage read marginally and declared done 0.902 is inside the 0.88–0.92 band, so the interval is pronounced calibrated. "Which hours miss?" Have them recompute coverage by hour block on the spot. The 0.845 peak-window number ends the discussion faster than any explanation.
    The break is differenced away without being understood Seasonal differencing removes the level step, the KPSS test passes, and nobody has noticed a permanent +3.1% regime change. "What happened on 1 October 2022?" A model that cannot answer that question will misattribute the step to trend and extrapolate it.

    Data¶

    File Rows Use in this case
    data/ksa_grid_demand.csv 26,235 The raw series. Hourly, Asia/Riyadh, 2021-01-01 to 2023-12-31, columns timestamp, demand_mw, temp_c. Contains the planted defects — use this file, not the clean one, so the cleaning decisions stay visible.
    data/ksa_calendar.csv 1,095 Daily calendar flags: is_weekend, is_ramadan, is_eid, is_national_day, is_founding_day. Join on date, not on timestamp.
    data/temp_forecast_14d.csv 16,128 48 weekly origins through 2023, 336 hours each. Required for question 2.
    data/ksa_grid_demand_4area.csv 105,120 Optional. Four operating areas in long format, for groups that want to argue the multi-area maintenance case.

    Preparation. No preparation is needed beyond the repository itself, and by Day 3 every group already has a cleaned series and a backtest from Labs 1–6. If the case is run cold — with a group that has not done the labs — allow an extra 20 minutes and hand out ksa_grid_demand_clean.csv (26,280 rows, no defects) instead of the raw file, so the session is spent on the decision rather than on data repair.

    A word on the data's status. The series is synthetic by design. The structure it contains is planted and therefore known, which is what makes it gradeable. The consequence is that a model which performs well here has been validated against a generative process, not against real grid telemetry, and no participant should claim otherwise in their capstone. See instructor/DATA_AND_RESOURCE_CATALOG.md for the full generative design and its limits.
