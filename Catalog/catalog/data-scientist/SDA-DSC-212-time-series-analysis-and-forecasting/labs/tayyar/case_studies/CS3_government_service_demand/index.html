    Case Study 3 — Government Service Demand and Workforce Planning¶

    SDA-DSC-212 · Time Series Analysis and Forecasting · Facilitator brief

    At a glance¶
    Primary module fit M2 — Stationarity and Autocorrelation (structural breaks, level shifts, what differencing hides)
    Secondary fit M5 (uncertainty as a staffing input), M6 (backtesting across a regime change)
    LOs exercised LO1 primary; LO5 and LO6 in the plenary
    Suggested slot Day 1, Hour 4 — second half of the M2 theory hour, once ADF and KPSS have been introduced
    Alternative slot Day 3, Hour 2, as the motivating case for backtest design across a regime change
    Duration 35 minutes
    Group size 3–4 participants
    Data source No course dataset ships for this case. Run as a paper case, or generate a series with the recipe in Data below.

    The electricity series has one structural break and it is polite about it. This case has three kinds of discontinuity in two years, and none of them is an outlier to be cleaned away. It is the case that teaches the difference between a series that is non-stationary and a series that has changed.

    Scenario¶

    Manafith is a national digital services platform — a fictional composite, used here so that no real organisation's operational figures are implied. It handles licence renewals, permit applications, document attestations and status enquiries for individuals and businesses. Volume arrives through three channels: the mobile application, the web portal, and a contact centre that handles the cases the self-service channels cannot close. Roughly nine in ten transactions complete without human involvement. The tenth generates a support contact, and every support contact needs an agent.

    Faisal Al-Otaibi runs workforce planning for the contact centre. He publishes a staffing plan every Wednesday covering the following seven days, in half-hourly intervals, across three skill groups. The plan is binding: agent shifts are rostered a week ahead under a labour agreement, and changing them inside three days means overtime rates or an outsourced overflow contract at a premium. Faisal's plan is built from a demand forecast, and his forecast today is a four-week moving average of the same weekday and half-hour, adjusted by hand.

    It has failed three times in the past year, each time for a different reason. In March a new release moved a high-volume journey into self-service; call volume fell by a third and the moving average took a month to catch up, during which Faisal paid for agents who had nothing to do. In June a regulatory deadline for a mandatory registration produced a fortnight of volume that peaked at four times normal, concentrated in the last three days before the cut-off. In September a five-hour platform outage produced almost no transactions, followed by a compressed catch-up surge — and then his moving average, having ingested the outage as a genuine low, under-staffed the following week.

    The decision Faisal makes differently depending on the answer is the number of agents rostered per half-hour interval for each of the next seven days, and — separately and more importantly — how much overflow capacity to pre-contract. The first is a point decision. The second is a decision about uncertainty, and today he makes it by instinct.

    The decision question¶

    How many agents should be rostered in each half-hour of the next seven days, and how much overflow capacity should be pre-contracted against the possibility that demand exceeds the plan?

    Business context and the cost of being wrong¶

    The asymmetry here is smaller than in CS1 but it runs in the same direction, and it has a second dimension that electricity does not: queues are non-linear.

    Illustrative figures, to be stated as teaching numbers:

    Value
    Typical weekday support contacts 12,000
    Contacts handled per agent-shift 40
    Agents required at plan 300
    Fully loaded agent cost per shift SAR 440
    Overflow (outsourced) cost per contact SAR 26

    Over-forecast by 10%. Thirty agents rostered with nothing to do: 30 × 440 = SAR 13,200 for the day. Unpleasant, linear, and entirely recoverable next week.

    Under-forecast by 10%. Twelve hundred contacts arrive with no one to take them. If they go to overflow: 1,200 × 26 = SAR 31,200 — about 2.4 times the over-staffing cost. If overflow capacity was not pre-contracted, they queue, and queueing is where the linearity stops. A service operating at 95% occupancy that receives 10% more arrivals does not see 10% more waiting time; average wait rises by a multiple, abandonment climbs, and a proportion of abandoned contacts return the next day and are counted again. The under-staffing error partially creates its own demand.

    The honest summary for participants: the direct cost ratio is modest, roughly 2.4:1, but the under-forecast error has a convex tail that the ratio does not capture, and the correct response is therefore not simply "forecast a higher quantile" but "forecast a quantile and pre-contract the overflow that covers the difference between the plan and the upper quantile". Faisal needs a forecast distribution, not a forecast.

    What makes this series hard¶

    Three distinct types of discontinuity, which demand three different treatments.

    Type Example here Correct treatment What goes wrong if treated as the others
    Permanent level shift The March release moves a journey to self-service; volume steps down ~30% and stays down Step dummy, or discard pre-break history, or a short training window — a decision that must be argued Treated as an outlier, it is smoothed away and the model over-forecasts for months
    Transient known event The June regulatory deadline: a two-week ramp with a sharp terminal peak An event feature with a countdown, or an explicit intervention model Treated as a level shift, the model expects the elevated level to persist
    Anomaly to exclude The September five-hour outage: near-zero transactions, then a catch-up surge Excluded from training as a target, retained as a flag; the catch-up surge is not an anomaly and must stay Ingested as genuine demand, the level estimate collapses and the following week is under-staffed

    Participants who do not distinguish these three will apply the same tool to all of them, and that is the single most valuable discovery in this case.

    Strong multiple seasonality with a hard operating envelope. Intraday demand is not a smooth sinusoid — it is near-zero overnight, rises steeply from about 08:00, peaks mid-morning, dips around the midday prayer and lunch, and has a distinct evening shoulder. The weekly cycle is Friday–Saturday down. The annual cycle is dominated by administrative and academic deadlines, not by weather. A Fourier basis fitted to a smooth 24-hour period represents the overnight floor badly; a full set of hour-of-day dummies represents it well but costs 23 parameters.

    Campaign effects that are known in advance and are not repeatable. Awareness campaigns, ministerial announcements and app-store featuring produce spikes that are known to the communications team and not to the forecaster. A campaign flag is only useful if someone maintains it, which is an organisational constraint rather than a technical one.

    Ramadan changes the shape of the working day. Public-sector hours shorten, the intraday peak shifts earlier, and a substantial evening peak appears after Iftar that does not exist for the rest of the year. As in CS1, a single is_ramadan flag cannot express a shape change.

    Growth that is not a trend. Adoption of a new digital service is closer to a logistic curve than a line. Fitting a linear or exponential trend to the steep part of an S-curve and extrapolating seven days ahead is survivable; extrapolating a quarter ahead is not.

    Data-quality traps particular to service telemetry. Contact records are timestamped at different points in the pipeline — arrival, answer, disposition — and the three do not agree during a queueing episode, which is exactly when accuracy matters. Half-hour buckets recorded in UTC and displayed in local time drift by an hour if daylight-saving logic is inherited from an international platform, even though Asia/Riyadh has no daylight saving. And a suppressed channel (a contact form taken offline for a day) looks identical to genuinely low demand unless someone logs it.

    Technical agenda¶

    1. Plot before testing (M1). Two years of hourly data on one axis, with the three known events annotated. Most of this case is visible in that one plot, and the discipline of drawing it before running a test is the first thing to enforce.
    2. Run ADF and KPSS and interpret the disagreement (M2). A series with a permanent level shift will typically produce the pattern participants have just been taught to recognise: ADF rejecting its unit-root null while KPSS rejects its stationarity null. Have them reach the verdict and then ask whether differencing is the right response to a known, dated, explainable step.
    3. Distinguish the three discontinuities (M2, M6). Classify each of the three events using the table above, and state the treatment for each with a justification.
    4. Design a backtest that spans the break (M6). Origins placed only after March 2023 make the model look stable and prove nothing. Origins spanning the break reveal how long recovery takes. The recovery time — how many days of degraded accuracy the break causes — is itself the number Faisal cares about, because it tells him how long he is exposed.
    5. Build calendar and event features (M4). Hour-of-day treatment, day-of-week, a deadline countdown feature, holiday flags with lead and lag windows, is_ramadan interacted with hour, and a maintained campaign flag with a named owner.
    6. Produce a distribution, not a number (M5). Quantile forecasts at the roster level and at the overflow level. Two different quantiles for two different decisions, derived from two different cost structures — this is the case's cleanest illustration of LO5.
    7. Translate to a staffing plan (M7). Convert forecast contacts per interval into agents using the handling rate, then state what the plan does when demand exceeds the upper quantile. A forecast that stops before this step has not answered Faisal's question.

    Constraints¶

    Constraint What it does to model choice
    Rosters lock seven days ahead The horizon is 168 hours at half-hourly resolution. Errors at the far end of the horizon dominate, and a model whose accuracy decays steeply with lead time is unsuitable regardless of its h+1 performance.
    Two years of history, one regime change in it There is not enough data to discard the pre-break period casually — doing so removes an entire annual cycle including the previous year's Ramadan.
    Events are known but not recorded The deadline and campaign features depend on someone maintaining a calendar. If no owner exists, the model must not depend on them, and that limitation belongs in the decision record rather than being wished away.
    Explainability to a workforce committee The staffing plan is negotiated with an operations committee that will challenge specific intervals. "The model says so" fails in that room. A component story — baseline, weekday effect, deadline effect — survives it.
    The plan must degrade gracefully If the forecast service fails on a Wednesday morning, the roster still has to be published. A documented fallback (last-four-weeks same-interval median, with a stated uplift) is a deliverable, not a contingency.
    The forecast changes people's shifts Unlike a load forecast, this one determines when named individuals work. Volatile week-to-week roster changes have a human cost that no accuracy metric records, and a slightly less accurate but more stable forecast may be the better product.

    Facilitation plan¶

    Thirty-five minutes. This case is best run immediately after ADF and KPSS, while the reconciliation table is still on the board.

    Minutes Phase What the facilitator does
    0–4 Setup Show a single annotated plot of the two-year series with the three events marked but unlabelled — three arrows, no captions. Give the scenario in three sentences. Ask each group to write down, before any discussion, what they think each of the three arrows is. Collect nothing; the private commitment is what matters.
    4–8 The trap Give the ADF and KPSS results for the raw series and ask for the verdict. The room will correctly reach "difference-stationary — difference once". Let them. Then ask a single question and stop talking: "what does differencing do to the March event, and is that what you want?"
    8–22 Small-group work Groups fill in the three-discontinuity table for their series: classify each event, choose a treatment, and state what breaks if they choose wrongly. Then they design the backtest: where do the origins go, and what would the backtest report if all origins sat after March? Circulate. Questions to use: "your model has forgotten March by June — how did you check?"; "the outage produced almost no volume; is the following Tuesday's surge an anomaly too?"; "you have used a step dummy — what is its value on a date after the training window ends?"
    22–31 Plenary One group presents the classification table; the others amend rather than repeat. Then take the backtest design to the board and place the origins physically, as marks on a timeline. Ask what the reported MASE would be under each placement. Groups that placed origins only in the stable period will see their number is meaningless, and seeing it is worth more than being told.
    31–35 Debrief Close on the distinction the case exists to teach: non-stationary and changed are different diagnoses with different treatments, and a statistical test cannot tell them apart. Only knowing what happened on 1 March can. Then connect to Tayyar's own 1 October 2022 break, which participants will meet in Lab 2 within the hour.

    Discussion questions¶

    1. ADF and KPSS disagree, and the reconciliation rule says "difference once". Should you? A good answer separates the statistical verdict from the causal one. Differencing will make the tests pass, and it will also convert a known, dated, permanent and explainable level shift into an unexplained one-off shock in the differenced series — discarding information rather than using it. A step dummy keeps the explanation. The strongest answers note that the choice depends on whether more shifts of the same kind are expected: if releases like March's happen twice a year, the model should be able to represent them, which argues for the structure rather than the difference.

    2. Should the September outage hours be removed from the training data? What about the surge that followed? A good answer treats the two differently and says why. The outage is a supply failure, not a demand observation, so it is not evidence about demand and should be excluded as a target while being retained as a flag. The subsequent surge is demand — deferred demand, genuinely arriving — and removing it would teach the model that outages have no consequence. The best answers observe that this distinction requires operational knowledge no diagnostic can supply, and that the forecaster therefore needs a channel to the operations team.

    3. Faisal needs one number for the roster and a different number for the overflow contract. What are they? A good answer derives two quantiles from two cost structures. The roster balances idle agent cost against overflow cost, giving a moderate quantile. The overflow contract balances a small option premium against the queue collapse it prevents, giving a much higher one. It should note that these are the same forecast distribution consumed twice, which is the argument for producing a distribution in the first place, and that reporting only a p50 forces both decisions to be made by instinct.

    4. Two years of history and an S-shaped adoption curve. How much of that history should the model see? A good answer refuses to answer in the abstract and proposes measuring it: fit with several training-window lengths and compare backtest performance across origins that span the break. It should recognise the tension — a short window adapts quickly but has seen only one Ramadan, a long window is stable but carries a regime that no longer exists — and should consider weighting recent observations more heavily rather than choosing a hard cut-off.

    5. A more accurate forecast that changes rosters more often may be a worse product. Do you agree? A good answer takes the constraint seriously rather than dismissing it as non-technical. It might propose measuring roster churn alongside accuracy, constraining week-to-week changes, or forecasting a smoothed staffing requirement rather than raw demand. The weakest answers assert that accuracy is the objective and stability is someone else's problem; the strongest recognise that the objective was never accuracy, it was a good roster.

    6. The June deadline was known months in advance and the model still missed it. Whose failure is that? A good answer resists both easy positions — it was not purely a modelling failure, and not purely an organisational one. It identifies the missing artefact: an event calendar with an owner, a schema, and a service-level expectation for how far ahead entries appear. It should note that this makes the forecast service dependent on a process the forecasting team does not control, and that the dependency belongs in the monitoring contract and in the decision record.

    Expected outputs¶

    1. The completed three-discontinuity table: each event classified, its treatment chosen, and the failure mode of the wrong treatment named.
    2. A backtest design sketched on a timeline: origin placement, training window policy, and one sentence on what the design would fail to detect.
    3. Two quantiles with their cost derivations — one for the roster, one for the overflow contract.
    4. A feature list for the model, marked to show which features require an external owner to maintain and who that owner is.
    5. A stated fallback: what the roster is built from on a Wednesday when the forecast service is unavailable.

    Common failure modes in this case¶

    Failure mode What it looks like Facilitator's redirect
    Everything unusual is an outlier All three events are winsorised or removed, the series becomes smooth and stationary, and the model can no longer represent anything that has actually happened to this service. "Your model is now unable to forecast the next regulatory deadline. There is one in November. What does it say?"
    Differencing used as a reflex The KPSS test fails, so the group differences, the test passes, and the March release is never discussed again. Ask what the differenced series looks like on 1 March. It is a single large spike with no explanation attached — the group has converted knowledge into noise.
    Backtest origins placed only in the stable period Sixty origins, all after June 2023, an excellent MASE, and no evidence at all about behaviour under change. "Your service exists because of three events. How many of them does your backtest contain?"
    A step dummy with no serving definition The dummy is 1 after March in training; nobody has defined its value at forecast time, or it is silently 0 because the feature builder only saw historical rows. This is a training–serving skew defect and belongs to the same family as the leakage exercise. Have them trace one serving row through the feature builder by hand.
    Contacts forecast, agents forgotten The deliverable is a curve of predicted contacts. The conversion to agents, the handling rate, and the roster granularity are never addressed. "Faisal cannot roster 4,312 contacts. How many people, in which half-hour?"
    Ramadan handled as a holiday A single flag that lowers the level, when the actual effect is a compressed working day and a new evening peak. Same redirect as CS1: plot the mean intraday profile inside and outside Ramadan on one axis and let the crossing curves make the argument.

    Data¶

    No dataset ships with the course for this case. Choose one of three routes.

    Route 1 — paper case (default). Run the session on the annotated plot and the scenario alone. The classification exercise, the backtest design and the two-quantile derivation all work on paper and fit the 35 minutes comfortably. This is the recommended route for the Day 1 slot.

    Route 2 — generate a series. The following recipe produces a two-year hourly transaction series containing all three discontinuities, in the same style and timezone as the course's own generator. It runs in under a second and is reproducible from the seed. Verified output: 17,520 rows, mean 555 transactions per hour, median daily volume around 11,900, and a peak deadline day near 54,000.

    import numpy as np, pandas as pd

rng = np.random.default_rng(212)
idx = pd.date_range("2022-01-01", "2023-12-31 23:00", freq="h", tz="Asia/Riyadh")
h, dow, doy = idx.hour.to_numpy(), idx.dayofweek.to_numpy(), idx.dayofyear.to_numpy()

open_hours = ((h >= 8) & (h <= 22)).astype(float)
daily  = 0.25 + 0.75*np.exp(-((h - 11)**2)/18) + 0.55*np.exp(-((h - 20)**2)/12)
weekly = np.where(np.isin(dow, [4, 5]), 0.55, 1.0)      # KSA weekend: Fri, Sat
annual = 1 + 0.10*np.sin(2*np.pi*(doy - 30)/365.25)
trend  = (1 + 0.22) ** (np.arange(len(idx))/(365.25*24))  # ~22% YoY adoption

lam = 900.0 * trend * annual * weekly * (0.05 + 0.95*open_hours) * daily

# 1. permanent level shift: self-service release, 1 March 2023
lam = np.where(idx >= pd.Timestamp("2023-03-01", tz="Asia/Riyadh"), lam*1.35, lam)

# 2. transient known event: registration deadline, 1 July 2023
window   = (idx >= pd.Timestamp("2023-06-15", tz="Asia/Riyadh")) & \
           (idx <  pd.Timestamp("2023-07-01", tz="Asia/Riyadh"))
days_to  = (pd.Timestamp("2023-07-01", tz="Asia/Riyadh") - idx).total_seconds()/86400
lam = np.where(window, lam*(1 + 2.4*np.exp(-days_to/4.0)), lam)

# 3. anomaly to exclude: five-hour platform outage, 12 September 2023
outage = (idx >= pd.Timestamp("2023-09-12 09:00", tz="Asia/Riyadh")) & \
         (idx <  pd.Timestamp("2023-09-12 14:00", tz="Asia/Riyadh"))
lam = np.where(outage, lam*0.04, lam)

df = pd.DataFrame({"timestamp": idx, "transactions": rng.poisson(lam)})
df.to_csv("data/manafith_transactions.csv", index=False)

    Two deliberate omissions in this recipe are worth pointing out to participants. There is no catch-up surge after the outage — add one if you want the full third-discontinuity discussion, and note that having to add it by hand is itself a lesson in how much of a service's behaviour a generative model does not capture. And there is no Ramadan shape change; borrow the treatment from tools/make_tayyar_data.py, which multiplies daytime hours down and post-Iftar hours up rather than shifting the level.

    Route 3 — a public substitute. Public hourly contact-centre data is scarce, and most published call-centre datasets are small or synthetic. If a real series is wanted, the practical substitutes are web-traffic and page-request series (for example, the Wikipedia Web Traffic Time Series Forecasting dataset on Kaggle, which is daily page views for a large panel of articles and contains genuine campaign-driven spikes and level shifts). Check the current licence on the source page before distributing. This is a reasonable proxy for the shape of the problem — spikes, shifts, event-driven demand — but it carries no channel or workforce structure, so the staffing half of the case still has to be run on paper.

    Whichever route you take, say plainly that Manafith is fictional. The scenario is a composite built to be plausible for a Riyadh audience; no figure in it describes a real platform, and participants should not repeat any of it as fact.
