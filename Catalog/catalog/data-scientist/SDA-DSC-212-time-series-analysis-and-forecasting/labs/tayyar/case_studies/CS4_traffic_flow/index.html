    Case Study 4 — Short-Horizon Traffic Flow Forecasting¶

    SDA-DSC-212 · Time Series Analysis and Forecasting · Facilitator brief

    At a glance¶
    Primary module fit M4 — Feature-Based ML Forecasting (temporal features, multiple seasonality in a supervised matrix)
    Secondary fit M1 (multiple seasonal periods and their strengths), M6 (horizon-dependent evaluation)
    LOs exercised LO3 primary; LO1 and LO4 in support
    Suggested slot Day 2, Hour 3 — inside M4 theory, before Lab 4
    Alternative slot Day 1, Hour 3, as a second decomposition example alongside the load series
    Duration 30 minutes
    Group size 3–4 participants
    Data source UCI Metro Interstate Traffic Volume, or a local corridor extract. Runs as a paper case without data. See Data.

    This is the shortest of the four cases and the one with the widest horizon range. It exists to make two points that the electricity series makes less sharply: that the useful horizon is not a modelling choice but a consequence of what the forecast is for, and that a series can be dominated by a seasonal pattern and still be almost entirely unpredictable in the moments that matter.

    Scenario¶

    A metropolitan traffic management centre monitors a ring-road corridor with loop detectors and gantry cameras at roughly one-kilometre spacing. Each detector reports vehicle counts, occupancy and mean speed in five-minute bins, aggregated to hourly for planning purposes. The corridor carries about 6,500 vehicles per hour per direction at the morning peak and roughly a fifth of that overnight.

    Layla Al-Ghamdi is the duty operations manager. She makes three decisions on different clocks and the same data:

    1. Within the hour — where to pre-position the three incident-response units on shift. A unit already near a congestion front reaches an incident far faster than one dispatched from a depot.
    2. Day ahead — which of the variable-message sign campaigns and ramp-metering plans to activate tomorrow, and when to start them. Metering that begins after the queue has formed achieves very little; metering that begins too early irritates drivers and erodes compliance.
    3. Week ahead — whether to authorise a lane closure for maintenance on a given night, and which night.

    The same forecasting service is expected to inform all three. It currently does not: the centre uses a historical profile — the average of the same weekday and hour over the past six weeks — which is accurate on a dry Tuesday and useless on the days Layla actually needs help.

    The difference the forecast makes is concrete for each decision. For pre-positioning it is the minutes between an incident occurring and a unit arriving. For metering it is the start time of a plan. For maintenance it is which night the closure happens, and therefore how many vehicle-hours of delay it causes.

    The decision question¶

    Where should the three incident-response units be positioned for the next hour, and at what time tomorrow should the ramp-metering plan be activated?

    Business context and the cost of being wrong¶

    Traffic delay costs are convex in a way that makes the asymmetry sharper than the raw numbers suggest, because a corridor near capacity has almost no headroom.

    Illustrative figures, to be stated as teaching numbers:

    Value
    Corridor peak flow 6,500 veh/h
    Vehicles affected by a one-hour partial blockage ~4,000
    Mean additional delay per affected vehicle 7 minutes
    Value of travel time SAR 45 per vehicle-hour
    Delay cost of one such hour 4,000 × (7/60) × 45 ≈ **SAR 21,000**

    A widely cited traffic-engineering rule of thumb holds that every minute a lane stays blocked generates roughly four minutes of residual congestion after it clears. On that basis, an incident-response unit that arrives five minutes sooner because it was pre-positioned correctly removes something like twenty minutes of downstream congestion — on the illustrative figures above, roughly SAR 7,000 from a single incident. A corridor with two or three incidents a day, several hundred days a year, makes that a serious number.

    The asymmetry: over-forecasting flow puts a response unit somewhere it is not needed, which costs the fuel and the opportunity cost of it not being somewhere else — bounded and small. Under-forecasting flow leaves the unit at the depot when the queue forms, which costs the convex delay above. As in the other three cases, the two errors are not the same size, and a symmetric metric will not distinguish a model that fails safely from one that does not.

    There is a second and less obvious asymmetry in time. A forecast that is right about the peak's magnitude but half an hour late about its onset is, for a metering decision, almost as bad as no forecast at all. Point-in-time accuracy metrics score onset timing errors weakly, and a group that notices this has understood something most production traffic-forecasting dashboards have not.

    What makes this series hard¶

    Multiple seasonality, one of which is bimodal. The daily cycle has two peaks, not one, and they are asymmetric: the morning peak is sharper and more predictable (people leave for work in a narrow window), the evening peak is broader and flatter (people leave work across two hours or more). A single-harmonic Fourier term cannot represent a bimodal day; three or four harmonics can, and demonstrating this by fitting increasing numbers of harmonics is an excellent five-minute exercise in the M4 slot.

    The weekly cycle is a shape change, not a scale change. Friday and Saturday do not have a smaller version of the weekday profile — they have a different profile entirely, with no morning commuter peak, a later start, and a broad afternoon and evening plateau. A multiplicative weekend factor applied to the weekday shape is wrong at every hour of the day, and it is wrong in compensating directions, which means the average error looks acceptable.

    Weather affects flow and speed in opposite directions. Rain typically reduces volume slightly (some trips are deferred or cancelled) while reducing capacity substantially, so congestion rises even though counts fall. A model that treats rain as a demand shifter will get the sign of the congestion effect wrong. In a Riyadh context the equivalents are dust and low-visibility events, and extreme heat, which suppresses discretionary midday trips.

    Incidents are the dominant source of variance and are close to unforecastable. A blocked lane produces a sharp drop in downstream flow and a queue upstream. Incidents are approximately random in timing, so the conditional forecast given no incident is highly accurate and the marginal forecast is not. This gives the case its most important structural lesson: the honest output is a distribution with a heavy lower tail on flow (and a heavy upper tail on travel time), and the service's real value at h+1 is less "what will the flow be" than "how likely is it that something has gone wrong".

    Detector data is noisy in characteristic ways. Loop detectors drop out, and a dropout reads as zero flow — which is indistinguishable from a fully blocked road unless occupancy is checked at the same time. Detectors drift and under-count. Lane-level counts sum to a link total only when every lane's detector is healthy. A cleaning step that drops zeros without checking occupancy will delete exactly the incident hours the service exists to detect.

    The predictable horizon is short and it is asymmetric across the day. At h+1 on a free-flowing overnight corridor, persistence is nearly unbeatable. At h+1 during the onset of the morning peak, persistence is badly beaten by anything that knows the time of day. At h+24, the calendar carries almost all the information and the recent lags carry very little. A single model reported at a single horizon hides all of this; a horizon-by-horizon error curve, split by time of day, reveals it — and that plot is the single most useful artefact the case produces.

    Technical agenda¶

    1. Decompose and quantify the seasonal periods (M1). MSTL with periods [24, 168]. Report the strengths. Compare the daily and weekly strengths against the load series — in the course data the load series has daily strength 0.972 and weekly 0.610, and a commuter corridor typically has a higher weekly strength because the weekday/weekend difference is a shape change rather than a level change.
    2. Fit Fourier bases of increasing order (M4). One, two, three, four harmonics against the daily period. Plot the fitted daily profile at each order and identify the order at which the second peak appears. This takes five minutes and settles the "how many harmonics" question for the rest of the course.
    3. Build the supervised matrix (M4). Lags at 1, 2, 3, 24, 168; .shift(1)-guarded rolling means and standard deviations; hour, day of week, weekend flag, holiday flag; weather features at the forecast lead, not the realised value.
    4. Model weekday and weekend shape explicitly (M4). Either interact hour-of-day with a weekend flag, or fit separate Fourier bases per day type. Compare with the naive multiplicative factor and show where the multiplicative version fails.
    5. Evaluate horizon by horizon (M6). Direct models at h+1, h+3, h+6, h+12, h+24, each backtested over the same origins. Plot error against horizon, and again split by time of day. Compare each against two baselines: persistence (y_{t}) and the historical profile (same weekday and hour, averaged). Persistence wins at short horizons, the profile wins at long ones, and the crossover point is the interesting number.
    6. Handle incidents honestly (M5, M6). Flag known incident hours. Report accuracy with and without them, and state both. Then discuss whether the service should predict flow, or predict the probability of a disruption — they are different products with different evaluation regimes.
    7. Note the spatial structure without modelling it (extension). Congestion propagates upstream at a roughly constant shockwave speed, so a detector's future is partly written in its neighbour's present. Say this, show it as a lagged cross-correlation between two adjacent detectors, and stop. That single plot is the on-ramp to spatiotemporal forecasting, and it is enough for a 15-hour course.

    Constraints¶

    Constraint What it does to model choice
    Sub-minute latency at h+1 The pre-positioning decision is made in real time from a live feed. A model that needs a 40-feature build across 300 detectors before it can score is not a candidate at this horizon, whatever its accuracy.
    Three horizons, three decisions One model is unlikely to serve h+1, h+24 and h+168 well. A defensible design is different models per horizon, and groups should be willing to recommend that rather than forcing a single artefact.
    Weather is a forecast at serve time Same constraint as CS1. A model validated on observed rainfall and served on forecast rainfall is optimistic by an unmeasured amount.
    Detector availability varies Detectors fail. The model must have a defined behaviour when an input is missing, and "impute with the mean" is a choice that has to be defended given that a missing detector and a blocked road look similar.
    Operator trust is fragile and asymmetric A duty manager who is sent to the wrong place twice stops using the tool. The failure that destroys trust is a confident wrong forecast, not an uncertain one, which is a direct argument for shipping the interval alongside the point.
    The forecast competes with a human who is very good An experienced duty manager reads this corridor well. The service has to beat the historical profile and be worth the attention it costs. A marginal accuracy gain that requires monitoring a new screen is not obviously a gain.

    Facilitation plan¶

    Thirty minutes. Keep it tight; this case is a sharpener inside a theory hour, not a full workshop.

    Minutes Phase What the facilitator does
    0–3 Setup Show two plots side by side with the axes labelled and the series unlabelled: one day of the load series, one day of the traffic series. Ask the room which is which and why. The bimodal shape gives it away instantly, and that is the point — the shape is the information.
    3–6 The three decisions State Layla's three decisions and their horizons. Ask a single question: "one model or three?" Take a show of hands, record the split, and move on without resolving it.
    6–18 Small-group work Two tasks, both concrete. (a) Design the feature set for h+1 and for h+24 separately, and mark every feature that appears in only one of the two lists. (b) Predict, before running anything, which of persistence and the historical profile wins at each of h+1, h+6 and h+24, and at what horizon they cross. Circulate: "your h+1 model uses lag168 — what is it contributing that lag1 does not?"; "your h+24 model uses lag1 — where does lag1 come from at serve time when the origin is 24 hours back?"; "an incident starts at 07:40. Which of your features sees it?"
    18–26 Plenary Collect the feature-list differences on the board in two columns. The columns are strikingly different, and that difference is the case's main technical claim made visible. Then take the persistence-versus-profile predictions and, if data is available, show the actual crossover; if not, ask the groups to defend their predictions against each other.
    26–30 Debrief Two closing points, stated once each. First: the horizon is set by the decision, and the feature set follows the horizon, not the other way round. Second: show the lagged cross-correlation between adjacent detectors, say the words "spatiotemporal forecasting", say that this course does not teach it, and name where to read about it. Do not start it.

    Discussion questions¶

    1. One model for all three horizons, or three models? Defend the choice on grounds other than accuracy. A good answer argues from serving constraints and maintenance rather than from error curves. Three models mean three feature pipelines, three monitoring contracts and three ways to fail, against a single model that is mediocre at two of the three horizons. A strong answer might propose a single feature pipeline serving three lightweight direct models — one build, three heads — which gets most of the accuracy for one maintenance liability.

    2. Incidents cause most of the variance and cannot be predicted. What should the service output? A good answer stops trying to predict the unpredictable and reframes the product. Options include: a conditional forecast plus an explicit disruption probability; a lower quantile of flow as an operational planning figure; or a change-detection service that flags departures from the conditional forecast quickly rather than forecasting further ahead. The best answers note that this changes the evaluation entirely — detection latency and false-alarm rate replace MAPE — and that this is a product decision, not a modelling one.

    3. Rain reduces volume and reduces capacity. How would you build a feature set that gets both effects right? A good answer recognises that flow and congestion are different targets and that a single weather coefficient cannot serve both. It might propose modelling speed or occupancy alongside volume, or interacting the weather feature with a measure of how close the corridor already is to capacity, so that rain has a small effect on a quiet Sunday and a large one at 08:00 on a Tuesday. Answers that just add is_raining and move on have not engaged with the mechanism.

    4. At h+1 overnight, persistence is nearly unbeatable. Is a machine-learning model justified at all? A good answer separates the average case from the useful case. Persistence is excellent when nothing is happening, which is most of the time and none of the times that matter; the model earns its place at peak onset and during recovery from disruption. The right evaluation is therefore conditional — error during transitions, not error overall — and the strongest answers propose reporting exactly that, and being willing to conclude that persistence should serve overnight while the model serves peak hours.

    5. A detector reads zero. What does your pipeline do, and what is the risk of each option? A good answer refuses to answer without a second variable. Zero flow with zero occupancy is a dead detector; zero flow with high occupancy is a blocked road, which is the single most important event the corridor can produce. Imputing both cases the same way either deletes real incidents or invents them. A good answer proposes the joint check and states the fallback for each branch.

    6. Congestion propagates upstream. You have not been taught spatiotemporal models. What can you do with that fact anyway? A good answer stays inside the course's toolkit and gets real value from it: use lagged values of the downstream detector as exogenous features for the upstream one, choosing the lag from the observed cross-correlation peak. That is a dynamic regression, which the course does teach, and it captures a large part of what a graph model would. The best answers name the limitation — it does not generalise to a whole network without one model per pair — and identify that as the point where a spatiotemporal method starts to earn its complexity.

    Expected outputs¶

    1. Two feature lists, for h+1 and h+24, with the differences marked and one sentence explaining each difference.
    2. A predicted error-versus-horizon curve for the model, persistence and the historical profile, drawn by hand on one axis, with the crossover points marked and defended.
    3. A one-paragraph product statement: what the service outputs, at what horizon, for which of Layla's three decisions, and what it explicitly does not attempt.
    4. A stated rule for detector zeros, with both branches of the occupancy check.

    Common failure modes in this case¶

    Failure mode What it looks like Facilitator's redirect
    One feature list for every horizon lag1 appears in the h+24 model. The group has built a recursive forecaster without deciding to. "It is 14:00 and you are forecasting 14:00 tomorrow. What is lag1?" Either it is a prediction, which makes this a recursive model with compounding error, or it does not exist. Both are answers; not having noticed is not.
    Weekend modelled as a multiplicative factor A single weekend coefficient scales the weekday profile down. The morning peak survives, scaled — on a day when it does not exist. Overlay the mean weekday and mean weekend profile. No single multiplier maps one onto the other, and the plot ends the argument.
    Incidents cleaned out of the training data Anomalous hours are removed, the model becomes excellent at forecasting uneventful traffic, and it is now blind to the only thing Layla is paid to manage. "You have removed every hour in which your user needed you. What is the model for?"
    Detector zeros imputed blindly Zeros are forward-filled or interpolated, deleting real blockages along with dead detectors. Ask for the occupancy value at those timestamps. If it was never loaded, that is the finding.
    Single-harmonic daily seasonality One sine and one cosine at period 24. The fitted daily profile has one peak; the data has two. Have them plot the fit against the mean observed profile. The missing second peak is visible immediately, and the fix is one line.
    Chasing the spatial model The group decides the real answer is a graph neural network and spends the session designing one they cannot build or evaluate today. Redirect to the lagged cross-correlation. Get one concrete, testable exogenous feature out of the spatial insight, then move on. The ambition is right; the timescale is not.

    Data¶

    No dataset ships with the course for this case. Three routes.

    Route 1 — UCI Metro Interstate Traffic Volume. Hourly westbound traffic volume for a single interstate detector station between Minneapolis and Saint Paul, with accompanying hourly weather attributes (temperature, precipitation, cloud cover, a categorical weather description) and a holiday indicator, covering roughly 2012 to 2018 and running to approximately 48,000 hourly rows. It is a single-series dataset, freely downloadable from the UCI Machine Learning Repository, and it carries almost everything this case needs: bimodal daily seasonality, a strong weekday/weekend shape change, holiday effects and weather covariates. Confirm the current licence and citation requirements on the UCI page before redistributing.

    Preparation. The series has gaps of several months. Reindex onto a complete hourly index in the local timezone, materialise the gaps as NaN, and either restrict to a continuous span or state a gap policy — the same discipline as Lab 1. Note for the facilitator: this corridor is in a continental climate with snow, so the weather semantics differ from a Riyadh corridor. Say so; do not silently transplant the interpretation.

    Route 2 — a local corridor extract. If the delivering institution has access to a Saudi corridor's detector data, use it. It is worth substantial preparation time: a local corridor makes the Friday–Saturday weekend, the Ramadan intraday shift and the summer midday suppression visible in a way no imported dataset can. Aggregate to hourly, keep occupancy alongside volume so that the detector-zero discussion is possible, and check for a campaign or roadworks log.

    Route 3 — paper case. The feature-list exercise, the persistence-versus-profile prediction and the product-statement discussion all work with no data at all, using hand-drawn profiles. This is the default for the 30-minute M4 slot, and it loses little.

    For the spatiotemporal on-ramp. The standard public benchmarks in the graph-based traffic forecasting literature are METR-LA (loop-detector speeds from a Los Angeles freeway network) and PEMS-BAY (loop-detector speeds from the San Francisco Bay Area), both at five-minute resolution across a few hundred sensors with an accompanying road-network adjacency matrix. They are widely used as the reference datasets for spatiotemporal models. Name them in the debrief as where to read next; do not attempt to use them in this course, which has neither the hours nor the learning outcomes for graph models.
