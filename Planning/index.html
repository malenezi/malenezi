National Market Intelligence · AI & Data Capability

Saudi Arabia has built AI demand faster than AI depth

A national view of AI and data capability development for SDAIA's Capability Building Planning Department. Built from 391 independently verified findings across eight research domains, with every statistic re-checked against a second source, scored for confidence, and traceable to its origin. Where no reliable public data exists, this report says so rather than estimating: 155 specific data gaps are catalogued.

Section 01

Executive summary

The Kingdom's AI capability story splits cleanly in two. On governance, adoption, awareness and female participation, Saudi Arabia is at or near the global frontier — and can prove it. On the depth of its technical workforce, it is below the global average, and it does not currently measure the things that would show this.

The nine findings that should shape planning

Finding 01 — the central asymmetry

Saudi Arabia leads the world on the demand side of AI and trails on the supply side. More than 80% of Saudi employees use AI regularly at work against roughly 50% in North America and Europe; 89% of Saudi university students use generative AI against 67% in the US and UK; 33.1% of establishments have adopted AI. Yet relative AI skill penetration is 0.78× the global average, and Saudi Arabia does not appear in the Stanford AI Index top-15 for AI talent concentration, which bottoms out at 0.95%. The Kingdom has manufactured world-class appetite for AI without yet manufacturing the workforce to satisfy it.

Finding 02 — the measurement blind spot

The single most important labour-market indicator for AI is not measured for Saudi Arabia. The Lightcast series behind the Stanford AI Index reports AI job-posting share for Singapore (4.69%), the UAE (2.87%), the US (2.56%) and the UK (1.93%) — and does not cover Saudi Arabia. Nor does any Saudi institution publish AI vacancy counts by role family, occupational coding for AI roles, provider-level placement outcomes, or — as a dedicated second research round confirmed — an annual count of AI or computing graduates for any year from 2019 to 2026. Verification of the widely-cited "50% AI hiring gap" attributed to the Minister of Human Resources failed at primary source. A capability-planning department is currently planning without a demand instrument.

Finding 03 — the target is too small

Even the most conservative demand scenario puts 2030 requirements 43% above the NSDAI target. Modelled against GASTAT's professional and technical employment base, demand for AI and data specialists reaches 28,700–44,700 by 2030 and 56,700–137,600 by 2035, against a standing target of 20,000. The Kingdom is on course to meet a target that was set for a smaller economy, four years early, and to mistake that for sufficiency.

Finding 04 — breadth is solved; depth is the binding constraint

Of 1,563,983 cumulative SDAIA training beneficiaries, roughly 0.9% are specialist-level. SAMAI hit its one-million target roughly fourteen months after launch, with 52% female participation. Meanwhile fewer than 10% of Gulf AI vacancies involve building or training models at all, and the recognised bottlenecks — MLOps and LLMOps, evaluation and assurance engineering, data platform engineering — have no dedicated national pathway. The marginal return on another literacy seat is now close to zero.

Finding 05 — two defensible global specialisations already exist

Saudi Arabia holds the world's highest share of AI inventors working in security, privacy and cryptography (15%, ahead of India at 13% and the UAE at 12%), and the world's highest female representation among AI inventors and authors (32.3%). Neither is a communications point; both are measured positions in the Stanford AI Index. Combined with a single unified data-and-AI regulator and PDPL already in force, they make AI assurance the one domain where the Kingdom could set an international standard rather than adopt one.

Finding 06 — the numbers cannot be added up

Headline learner claims across the ecosystem exceed the adult population and cannot be reconciled. SAMAI (1,000,000), Microsoft (800,000 completed, 3,000,000 targeted by 2030), IBM (500,000), Cisco (500,000 targeted), Google (500,000 across eleven MENA countries) and MCIT programmes (250,000 targeted) have no de-duplication, mix targets with delivery, and mix regional with national. Several major commitments — NVIDIA's "thousands of developers", the Google Cloud–PIF training pledge, HUMAIN's academy — carry no number at all.

Finding 07 — the female pipeline leaks where it becomes valuable

Saudi Arabia is first in the world for female representation in AI research and 52% of SAMAI participants were women — but Saudi female labour-force participation fell from 36.3% in Q1 2025 to 33.9% in Q1 2026, and as of 2020 only 2% of Saudi female tertiary graduates were in engineering against 19% of male graduates. The Kingdom's most distinctive capability asset is being lost at the conversion point between qualification and senior technical employment.

Finding 08 — reporting discipline is a live credibility risk

Two official Saudi government sources disagree on a headline national KPI. The Ministry of Finance FY2026 Budget Statement states the NSDAI ambition as "top 17 countries in data and AI by 2030"; every other source says top 15, and no source specifies which index is meant. Separately, verification removed several widely-circulated figures from this report: a BCG Saudi maturity breakdown summing to 102%, a "150,000 tech jobs in 2026" figure with no traceable origin, and a "50% AI hiring gap" that could not be traced to primary source.

Finding 09 — reconstructed statistics are already circulating

A supplied dataset claiming a full annual series of Saudi AI and computing graduates for 2019–2023 did not survive verification, and the way it failed is instructive. Its five values sum to 37,175 while citing themselves as the basis for "38,000+"; two consecutive years are identical to the unit; and the final year-on-year step reproduces a published rounded growth rate to five decimal places. No official source carries any of the five values. The series appears to have been back-computed from a cumulative total and a growth rate, then attributed to a Ministry of Education platform that does not publish education statistics. This matters beyond one document: the absence of a real series creates demand that reconstructed series will keep filling. Section 4a sets out the three tests that catch them.

What this means for the Capability Building Planning Department

The Kingdom's capability-building machinery is unusually strong at the top of the funnel and unusually blind at the bottom of it. Three moves change the trajectory more than any additional training spend: publish the mapping between the AI qualifications framework the Kingdom already owns and the specialist count it already reports; build the instrument that measures demand and graduate supply together; and re-baseline the 2030 target against that instrument. Everything else in Section 12 depends on those three.

Section 02

Method and evidence standard

This report was produced under a strict evidence standard: cited figures only, gaps declared rather than estimated. Fourteen agents were deployed — seven domain researchers, each pipelined into an independent adversarial verifier instructed to default to doubt.

Collection

Seven specialised research passes: national policy and institutions; talent supply and the education pipeline; labour demand, hiring and compensation; the capability-building provider market; international benchmarking; skills taxonomy and technology trends; and the quantitative base for forecasting. A second round added a dedicated education-statistics pass, triggered by a supplied dataset claiming an annual graduate series (Section 4a). Source priority ran primary official → tier-1 research → reputable media → aggregators.

Verification

Every one of the 391 findings was independently re-checked, in most cases against the primary document rather than the source originally cited. Verifiers were briefed on named failure modes: monthly/annual salary confusion, regional figures presented as Saudi, targets presented as outcomes, aggregator recycling, stale figures, consultancy models presented as measurement, field-of-study and degree-level scope drift, and Hijri/Gregorian academic-year mislabelling.

Disclosure

Verification was constrained: sdaia.gov.sa blocks automated retrieval on every path tested, so no SDAIA-hosted primary document could be read directly — including the November 2025 NSDAI update, which is the single highest-value document for this report and which SDAIA staff can obtain internally. Where independent corroboration was impossible, confidence is capped at Medium and said so.

Verification outcomes

All 391 findings carry a verdict. Corrections were applied to the text before publication; disputed and unverifiable items are retained in the appendix with their conflict notes so the reader can judge.

Source composition

Primary official sources — GASTAT, SPA, Ministry of Education, MCIT, HRSD, Ministry of Finance, Vision 2030 reporting, university registrars — account for the largest share.

Reading the confidence marks

Every figure in this report carries a confidence mark. High means a primary or tier-1 source was directly verified. Medium means the figure was corroborated but only indirectly, or rests on a single credible source that could not be independently triangulated. Low means the evidence is thin and the figure should not be relied on for planning. Data gap means no reliable public figure exists and none was invented.

What this report deliberately does not do

It does not estimate figures that are not published. Where a number would be useful and does not exist — Saudi AI vacancy counts, provider unit costs, the AI wage premium inside Saudi Arabia, HUMAIN's headcount, installed versus announced compute — it appears in the data-gap register in Section 13 instead. The one exception is Section 10, the demand model, which is explicitly labelled as modelled rather than measured, with every input and every assumption stated in full.

The institutional architecture

Saudi Arabia's structural distinctiveness is that one authority holds strategy, data governance, ethics, national AI research and delivery. SDAIA encompasses the National Data Management Office, the National Information Center and the National Center for AI. Very few countries have this; most split AI strategy from data regulation from delivery across three or more bodies. It is the Kingdom's clearest institutional advantage and the reason its governance rankings run far ahead of its talent rankings.

The regulatory stack that sits on top of it creates real, measurable workforce demand. The Personal Data Protection Law is in force. The SDAIA AI Ethics Principles — fairness, privacy and security, transparency and explainability, accountability and reliability, and social and environmental benefits — remain non-binding guidance. The Global AI Hub Law went to public consultation in 2025 and was not confirmed as enacted at the time of writing. NDMO data-management standards and NCA cybersecurity controls apply across government. Each of these generates demand for a governance and assurance workforce that the training system has not yet been organised to supply.

Unresolved: which ranking target is live

The Ministry of Finance FY2026 Budget Statement describes the NSDAI ambition as ranking Saudi Arabia among the "top 17 countries in the world in data and AI by 2030". Every other source, including Saudipedia and Access Partnership, reports top 15. No source specifies which index the ranking refers to — the Tortoise Global AI Index, the Oxford Insights Government AI Readiness Index and the Stanford AI Index would each give a materially different answer. This needs internal resolution before any capability plan is published against it.

One further instrument deserves specific mention because it bears directly on the Kingdom's headline capability number. Saudi Arabia operates a Saudi Academic Framework for AI Qualifications as the national benchmark for developing and accrediting AI programmes in higher education, alongside ETEC academic standards for AI programmes. Neither document could be retrieved externally for this report, and neither has been publicly connected to the 14,495 specialist count — so the Kingdom owns a qualifications taxonomy and reports a capability figure that is not visibly mapped to it.

The measurement instruments SDAIA has built — and does not publish

SDAIA operates three assessment instruments: the National Data Index (2023, covering fourteen data-management areas), the National AI Index (July 2025, three pillars across seven dimensions, 23 sub-domains and six maturity levels), and, through the Digital Government Authority, digital-transformation measurement across 235 government entities. Only the DGA measure has any published national figure. Neither the National Data Index nor the National AI Index has published a national average, a maturity distribution, or entity-level results. The instruments exist; the outputs do not reach the planners who need them.

The degree pipeline

SDAIA's own State of AI in Saudi Arabia report — launched on 9 September 2024 at the Global AI Summit, which runs biennially, so no newer edition exists — puts AI-related degree graduates at more than 38,000 across 2019–2023, of whom approximately 6,500 studied abroad. Eighty-six per cent of Saudi universities offer AI-related bachelor programmes and 42% offer specialised AI programmes. (A reported 42% increase in graduates between 2022 and 2023 circulates from the same report but has no primary attestation, and shares its value with the programme-coverage figure; treat it with care — see Section 4a.) Twenty-four Saudi universities sit in the QS Arab Region top 100 in the 2026 edition, down from 25 in 2025 and 29 in 2024. The wider picture is more positive: 33 Saudi universities appeared in major global rankings in 2025 against 25 in 2023, and the QS 2027 world rankings list 22 Saudi institutions — but only three sit in the global top 200, against a Vision 2030 target of five. KFUPM ranks 63rd globally in the QS 2027 world rankings and reports 93% graduate employability with an average 4.8 months to employment and an average starting salary of SAR 10,600 per month.

Two structural features of this pipeline matter more than its volume. First, the composition is shifting the right way: ICT rose from 6% to 10% of all higher-education graduates between 2009 and 2020 and engineering from 4% to 11%, while STEM enrolment reached 33.03% of higher-education students in 2025 against a 29.7% target. Second, the gender split inside it is not: as of 2020, 2% of Saudi female tertiary graduates were in engineering against 19% of male graduates, while 30% of women graduated in education, arts and humanities against 13% of men. The Kingdom leads the world on women in AI research and does not yet lead on women in AI engineering.

The scholarship and vocational routes

The restructured Custodian of the Two Holy Mosques Scholarship Program targets 70,000 students in the world's top 200 universities by 2030, routing AI explicitly through its Research & Development doctoral track. Some 28,500 Saudi students are currently enrolled in top-200 institutions, up from around 12,000 previously reported, with more than 3,800 in the top 30 against roughly 2,400 before — one of the fastest-moving numbers in the whole capability system. No breakdown by field of study is published, so the AI and data-science share of it is unknown. TVTC, the largest vocational provider, enrolled 336,150 trainees and produced 98,716 graduates in 2024 across 314 institutions — but publishes no AI or data-specific breakdown, which given its scale is one of the more consequential gaps in this report.

Declared gap — the graduate pipeline is not measured in AI terms

No Saudi institution publishes an annual count of AI or data-science graduates by degree level. The 38,000 figure is a cumulative five-year total from a 2024 SDAIA report with no published methodology and no update, and roughly 6,500 of it was earned abroad. A dedicated second research round tested this conclusion against GASTAT, the Ministry of Education, the Council of University Affairs, ETEC, UNESCO's UIS and every reachable university open-data portal, and confirmed it. Section 4a sets out what each source does and does not carry, and adjudicates a supplied dataset that claimed to fill the gap.

Adjudication of the supplied dataset

A document titled Saudi Arabian Higher Education Statistics: Computing, AI & Data Science (2023–2025) was supplied for integration. Two of its nine claims are confirmed, one is corrected, three are disputed, two are unverifiable, and its central contribution — a five-year annual graduate series — should not be used. The full adjudication is below, because the reasoning is reusable: the same three tests will catch the next reconstructed series.

The three tests that caught it

One — does the series agree with its own headline? The five values sum to 37,175. The document cites them as the basis for "38,000+". A series cannot underpin a total it falls 825 short of.

Two — are any values improbably identical? 2020 and 2021 are both exactly 6,290. Real graduate cohorts do not repeat to the unit across a pandemic year.

Three — does an implied rate reproduce a published rounded rate too precisely? 10,807 ÷ 7,610 = 1.420105. That reproduces the reported "42% increase" to five decimal places, which is the arithmetic signature of a value computed from the rate rather than measured independently.

A paywalled Statista chart of the same title and period does exist (September 2024, scope: "the combined total of graduates across undergraduate, graduate, and doctoral programs"), and is the most likely proximate origin. But Statista names no originating body for it, so it cannot serve as a citation in a government planning document either.

What the round did establish

Several genuinely new and usable figures came out of the same search. On programme provision: 86% of Saudi universities offer AI-related bachelor programmes and 42% offer specialised AI programmes (SDAIA, 2024); 79% have adopted curricula integrating technology and AI, with more than 30 making AI a compulsory course (HCDP 2025); and on 28 January 2026 the Minister of Education and the SDAIA President jointly launched 40 specialised data and AI academic programmes across 14 public and private universities, spanning bachelor's, master's, academic minors and advanced professional diplomas.

On the institutional base: the Kingdom has 29 public universities and 38 private universities and colleges, with roughly 1.36 million higher-education students in 2023 and a tertiary gross enrolment ratio that rose from 25% in 2009 to 78% by the end of the 2010s. Three Saudi universities sit in the global top 200 against a 2025 target of five, though 33 appeared in major global rankings in 2025 against 25 in 2023 — breadth is improving while the top tier is static.

And on the definitional question that Recommendation 1 turns on: Saudi Arabia already operates a Saudi Academic Framework for AI Qualifications as the national benchmark for developing and accrediting AI programmes in higher education, alongside ETEC academic standards for AI programmes. The instrument that would give "specialist" a defensible meaning already exists. Neither document could be retrieved externally during this research, and neither has been publicly connected to the 14,495 headline — which turns Recommendation 1 from a request to build something into a request to publish a mapping.

Why the gap persists — the specific institutional reason

Each candidate source fails for a different and identifiable reason, which matters because it tells you where to intervene. GASTAT's Education and Training Statistics is a household survey of ages 5–19; it was never designed to carry higher-education output. The Ministry of Education's open data page publishes 217 records covering budget, procurement, workforce and enrolment ratios, but nothing at discipline level. The Council of University Affairs publishes enrolment by university and gender through an embedded dashboard rather than a statistical yearbook, and not by field of study. Estishraf, to which the supplied document attributed the data, is an SDAIA decision-support platform built over the National Data Bank — not a Ministry of Education statistical publication. UNESCO's UIS collects tertiary graduates by field of education annually in principle, but the World Bank mirror of the Saudi field-of-study series terminates at reference year 2019. The fix is a reporting mandate on one existing body, not a new survey.

Sectoral and employer demand

The heaviest verified employer demand sits in energy. Saudi Aramco has identified 442 AI use cases with more than 200 deployed, reports $1.8 billion of AI-driven realised value in 2024, and had 6,000 AI developers in training as of February 2025, with partnerships at Caltech, Imperial College and KAUST. Financial services runs second: 52.9% of Saudi finance and insurance establishments use AI. Government is the third pole, driven by the National Data Lake integrating more than 430 systems.

On the aggregate, MCIT reports the technology workforce reaching 411,000 professionals by end-2025, with women's participation in the sector rising from 7% in 2017 to 35%. The digital economy contributed 16% of GDP in 2025, and the ICT market grew from USD 28bn in 2017 to USD 53bn in 2025, the IT segment within it from USD 11.5bn to USD 30.8bn. None of these is disaggregated to AI roles.

Beyond that, employer-level AI headcount is simply not published. There is no figure for stc, Mobily, Zain, SABIC, any SAMA-regulated bank, NEOM, Qiddiya, Diriyah, Red Sea Global, ROSHN or New Murabba — nor for HUMAIN itself, despite it being the anchor institution of the Kingdom's AI ambition. On the wider labour market, Hays reports 69% of Saudi recruitment activity concentrated in Riyadh, 43% in Jeddah and 37% in the Eastern Province; 74% of firms planned to grow teams in 2026; 48% of new roles target specialist digital skills; and 93% of employers acknowledge skills gaps.

Removed at verification

Three figures in wide circulation did not survive and are not used in this report. The "50% AI hiring gap" attributed to Minister Ahmed Al-Rajhi (CNBC, August 2025) could not be located at primary source despite extensive searching. A BCG Saudi AI-maturity breakdown (40% Leaders, 35% Scaling, 27% Stagnating) sums to 102% and is not present in the BCG source, which reports a GCC-wide 39%/61% split without a Saudi breakout. A projection of "more than 150,000 technology jobs in 2026" traces only to an unsourced careers blog.

Section 06

Skills taxonomy

Fifteen skill clusters, each rated for demand direction, strategic priority to Saudi Arabia, and the strength of the underlying evidence. Filter by category and direction; every cluster carries the specific evidence behind its rating.

Existing frameworks a Saudi taxonomy could map onto

The Kingdom does not need to invent a skills classification. Four established frameworks already carry AI content and could be adopted or mapped to, which would also make Saudi data internationally comparable — currently it is not. ESCO v1.2.1 contains 3,039 occupations and 13,939 skills in 28 languages including Arabic, which makes it the most immediately usable. O*NET 29.3 covers 1,016 occupations with 18,796 task statements. SFIA 9 carries explicit AI and data skill codes — AIDE (AI and data ethics), MLNG (machine learning), DATS (data science), DENG (data engineering), DATM (data management) — organised across seven levels of responsibility, which maps cleanly onto a tiered specialist definition. UNESCO's AI Competency Frameworks for students (12 competencies, four dimensions) and teachers (15 competencies, five dimensions) are the natural anchor for the K-12 and university mandates already in flight.

A note on the "declining skills" evidence

Claims that particular AI skills are dying are usually made without evidence. This report holds them to the same standard as everything else. Only two skill groups are in genuine net decline in the WEF Future of Jobs Report 2025: manual dexterity, endurance and precision (−24% net) and reading, writing and mathematics (−4%). For prompt engineering and junior analytics, the honest position is that direct evidence of decline is thin — what exists is strong evidence of value migration: PwC finds tasks newly added to AI-exposed roles are 2.5× more likely to rely on empathy, judgement and creativity, and Coursera now ranks critical thinking above prompt engineering among the fastest-growing Data-career skills. Plan for migration, not extinction.

Where provision is duplicated and where it is absent

Heavily duplicated

  • Mass AI literacy. SAMAI, Microsoft, IBM, Cisco, Google, Mostaqbali, Fuel and Incorta all target the same population with substantially similar content. Combined headline commitments exceed five million.
  • Introductory cloud and Python. Offered by every hyperscaler academy, Tuwaiq, TVTC, the Saudi Digital Academy and multiple private providers.
  • Prompt engineering and generative AI for beginners. Now present in almost every national catalogue, including SDAIA Academy's own July 2026 beginner track — at exactly the point the international evidence says this skill is commoditising.

Structurally absent

  • MLOps and LLMOps as a dedicated national pathway. The recognised bottleneck has no flagship programme; it appears only as modules inside broader tracks.
  • AI evaluation, assurance and red-teaming. No Saudi credential exists despite the Kingdom holding the world's strongest AI-security specialisation.
  • Senior conversion for experienced professionals. Every major pathway is aimed at students and early-career entrants.
  • AI product management. Named by BCG as 70% of the adoption barrier and by OECD as the actual skill demanded in AI-exposed roles; almost unprovided.
  • Frontier-lab engagement. No frontier AI lab has a documented Saudi skilling programme. The Kingdom's frontier relationships are commercial and infrastructural, not educational.
Declared gap — supply-side capacity cannot be sized

There is no public register of total AI and data training seats offered per year in the Kingdom across all providers, no published unit cost per trainee for any government programme, and no placement or graduate-destination data for SDAIA Academy, the Saudi Digital Academy, TVTC's data tracks, or Misk. Tuwaiq is the only major provider publishing an outcome figure, and its methodology is not published — the rate appears variously as ">75% at six months", ">80% at six months" and "80% at three months".

Section 08

International benchmarking

Saudi Arabia's international position is not one position but two. Sorting the Kingdom's measured rankings by what they measure produces a consistent split: it leads on governance, adoption and inclusion, and lags on talent depth and foundational human capital.

Peer national capability programmes

The most instructive comparison is Singapore, because it is the only peer whose target, mechanism and outcome are all published and all verifiable. Singapore set out to triple its AI practitioner pool to 15,000 over five years, backed it with more than S$20 million for practitioner training, S$7 million for an accelerated masters route and up to S$500 million for compute, and built the delivery around an apprenticeship model — the AI Apprenticeship Programme pays a S$4,000 monthly stipend, has run 25 batches, and places more than 90% of graduates in AI roles within six months. Singapore now has the highest AI job-posting share in the world at 4.69%.

Two features of that design are directly transferable and currently absent in Saudi Arabia. The first is the apprenticeship model itself: paid, employer-embedded, six to nine months, with placement measured and published. Saudi Arabia's equivalents are shorter, unpaid or stipend-light, and do not publish placement in a comparable form. The second is the size of the target relative to the workforce: Singapore's 15,000 is set against a far smaller labour force, which is why its posting share is four times the Gulf blended figure.

The competitor that matters most is next door

The UAE recorded the highest increase in AI talent concentration of any country between 2019 and 2025 (+121%, against Saudi Arabia's +113%), ranks second globally for net AI talent migration, has an AI job-posting share of 2.87% that is measured, mandated AI education across all K-12 grades from 2025-26, operates a university ranked 10th globally in AI by CSRankings, and offers a Golden Visa exceptional-talent route for scientists and specialists. No equivalent Saudi AI-specific residency route was identified in this research. The two countries are competing for the same regional talent pool with asymmetric instruments.

What the international evidence says actually works

One honest caveat first: rigorous evaluation evidence comparing AI bootcamps, degrees and apprenticeships is thin. No randomised or quasi-experimental study from the OECD, World Bank or ILO comparing these delivery modes was located, and this is recorded as a data gap rather than papered over. What the descriptive evidence does support is narrower but still useful. Employer-embedded delivery outperforms classroom delivery on placement — Singapore's apprenticeship route reports >90% and Tuwaiq's employer-partnered bootcamps 80%, against 56% for a provider serving first-time jobseekers without that embedding. Teacher and trainer capacity is the binding constraint on school-level programmes — Estonia's AI Leap explicitly funds 3,000 teachers alongside 20,000 students, and Saudi Arabia's own K-12 rollout reports 13,000+ teachers trained. And legally-binding reporting changes behaviour: the EU Digital Decade is the only peer programme that attaches statutory member-state reporting to a skills target.

Section 09

Technology trends and skills churn

The rate of skills change is high but not accelerating, the fastest-growing skill cluster in the world is also the newest, and the most credible academic estimates of AI's labour impact still differ by an order of magnitude.

The rate of change is high, and slightly slowing

The WEF Future of Jobs Report 2025 estimates that 39% of workers' core skill sets will be transformed or become outdated over 2025–2030 — down from 44% in the 2023 edition. That deceleration is worth noting precisely because the prevailing narrative assumes the opposite. The arithmetic underneath it is more useful for planning than the headline: per 100 workers by 2030, 41 require no significant training, 29 can be upskilled within their current role, 19 will be reskilled and redeployed internally, and 11 will need training that is not expected to be provided. Employers headquartered in the Middle East and North Africa project that under 50% of their workforce will need training by 2030, against 67% in North America — a materially lower expectation that the Capability Building Planning Department should treat as a risk signal rather than a comfort.

Where the change is concentrated is clearer. PwC's 2026 Global AI Jobs Barometer, built on more than a billion job advertisements, finds that the skills needed for the most AI-exposed jobs are changing more than twice as fast as those for the least exposed, and that this churn gap widened by 75% over the prior year. But the direction of the change cuts against the simple automation story: tasks newly added to AI-exposed positions are 2.5 times more likely to rely on empathy, judgement and creativity, and "professionalised" roles — those AI reshapes upward — show 42% faster wage growth since 2021 and grow twice as fast in number.

The displacement estimates still disagree by an order of magnitude

Any capability plan that cites only one of these is citing selectively. At the high end, Eloundou and colleagues estimate 80% of the US workforce could have at least 10% of tasks affected by large language models, with 19% seeing at least half their tasks impacted. At the low end, Acemoglu's macroeconomic estimate puts the total-factor-productivity effect at no more than 0.66% over ten years. The ILO's refined exposure index sits between them and is the most carefully constructed: about one in four workers globally is in an occupation with some generative-AI exposure, but only 3.3% of global employment sits in the highest exposure gradient — and that exposure is gendered, at 4.7% of female workers against 2.4% of male.

The one empirical result with direct Saudi relevance is Brynjolfsson, Chandar and Chen's finding of a 16% relative employment decline for workers aged 22–25 in AI-exposed occupations since generative AI became widely adopted. The Kingdom's entry-level analytics and dashboarding pipeline points at precisely that cohort in precisely those occupations.

Technology direction to 2030

Three trends have direct workforce consequences. First, agentic systems are the growth edge and the risk edge simultaneously: Gartner's 2026 strategic technology trends lead with AI-native development platforms and multiagent systems, while Gartner also expects more than 40% of agentic AI projects to be cancelled by end-2027 and estimates only around 130 genuine agentic vendors exist among thousands claiming the label. Second, the open-weight gap has widened rather than closed — as of March 2026 the leading closed model held a meaningful Elo lead over the best open-weight model — which matters for a sovereign-AI strategy built partly on open weights. Third, compute is scaling faster than the workforce that operates it: global AI compute capacity has expanded at 3.3× annually since 2022, and global data-centre staffing demand was forecast to rise by roughly 300,000 positions across more than 230 specialist roles. Saudi data-centre IT load is estimated at 410 MW in 2025 rising to a forecast 1,030 MW by 2030, with HUMAIN planning 1.9 GW — and no published staffing ratio exists to convert megawatts into people.

Arabic AI: the state of the field

ALLaM was developed by SDAIA's National Center for AI in 7B, 13B and 70B variants, trained on 540 billion Arabic tokens, and released open-weight under Apache 2.0 — a genuinely unusual decision for a sovereign model and a significant asset for building a domestic developer base. ArabicMMLU, with 14,575 questions across 40 tasks sourced from school examinations across North Africa, the Levant and the Gulf, gives the field a proper evaluation instrument. The competitive picture is real: Qatar's Fanar (Fanar Star 7B from scratch and Fanar Prime 9B, trained on roughly a trillion tokens with Islamic and recency retrieval), and the UAE's Jais and Falcon Arabic (600 billion tokens of Arabic, multilingual and technical data). Saudi Arabia's lead in Arabic AI is real, recent, and not permanent.

How the model works

The model multiplies a projected professional and technical employment base by an AI-specialist intensity that converges toward a benchmarked end state. It deliberately avoids GDP-based or investment-based approaches: the Saudi nominal GDP series in wide circulation was found at verification to be a stale pre-rebasing vintage understating actuals by 10–17%, and the headline AI investment commitments are dominated by announcements whose delivery status cannot be established.

The denominator question

How under-targeted the Kingdom is depends entirely on what you normalise by, and this report presents both readings rather than choosing the convenient one. Against total registered employment of 12.8 million, the 20,000 target is 1.6 specialists per thousand workers — roughly a third of the density Singapore is targeting against its far smaller workforce. Against professional and technical employment of 2.87 million, the same target is 7.0 per thousand, which is broadly comparable to peers. The divergence is caused by Saudi Arabia's large lower-skill expatriate workforce diluting the total-employment denominator. The professional-and-technical base is the more meaningful one for capability planning, and it is the one used in the model — but the total-employment reading is the one that shows how much of the Saudi labour market the AI transition currently does not touch at all.

What would change these numbers

  • Upward: a Saudi AI job-posting share that turns out to be closer to the Gulf blended 3.4% than to the low end; delivery of the announced compute build-out at 1.9 GW, which has no published staffing ratio attached; enactment of the Global AI Hub Law creating a compliance workforce; and the mandatory university AI curriculum converting at higher-than-expected rates into specialist tracks.
  • Downward: Gartner's expected cancellation of more than 40% of agentic AI projects by end-2027; the MENA employer expectation that under 50% of the workforce will need training by 2030; continued substitution of imported senior talent for domestic development; and productivity effects at the low end of the Acemoglu estimate rather than the high end of the Eloundou range.
  • Unknown: attrition and emigration of trained specialists, which no Saudi source measures. A 5% annual attrition rate would add roughly 15,000 to the cumulative training requirement by 2035 in the central case. This is not modelled because there is no basis for choosing a rate.
