What the research says about AI strategy

Every figure here links to its publisher and states how it was produced. Surveys measure what leaders report, definitions of success differ, and none of these numbers is a law of nature. Read them as a pattern.

37%

of respondents attribute at least some EBIT impact to AI, essentially unchanged from 2025, even though 44% now say AI is scaling across their enterprise (up from 38%). About 6% qualify as high performers.

McKinsey, The state of AI in 2026

How to read this number

Method. Global survey of 1,719 participants in 97 nations, fielded 4 May to 8 June 2026 and weighted by share of global GDP. Nearly three-quarters of high performers have fundamentally redesigned workflows, against about a quarter of the rest. 80% of respondents say AI improved their own productivity.

Caveat. Self-reported by executives. High performers are those who attribute at least 5% of EBIT to AI and call its impact significant.

5% → 48%

of companies generating meaningful value from AI, 2025 to 2026. The share BCG calls “future-built” rose from 5% to 7.5%.

BCG, Build for the Future 2025 and Applied AI Index 2026

How to read this number

Method. 2026 index: survey of about 1,330 senior executives across 20-plus sectors. In 2025, 60% of companies reported minimal gains.

Caveat. The 2026 figure combines “future-built” (7.5%) and “scaling” (41%) companies, so it is not a like-for-like comparison with the 5%. Consulting-firm survey.

>80%

of AI projects fail by some estimates, about twice the rate of IT projects without AI.

RAND, Root Causes of Failure for AI Projects, 2024

How to read this number

Method. RAND interviewed 65 experienced data scientists and engineers to explain why projects fail, and identified five root causes. The most common is leadership misunderstanding or miscommunicating the problem to be solved.

Caveat. The 80% is an outside estimate that RAND cites, not a rate RAND measured. The value of the study is the causes.

95%

of organizations studied saw no measurable profit-and-loss return from generative AI. About 5% of custom enterprise tools reached production.

MIT Project NANDA, The GenAI Divide, July 2025

How to read this number

Method. Review of more than 300 public AI initiatives plus executive interviews and a survey, January to June 2025. The authors attribute the gap to tools that do not learn or fit real workflows, and found the clearest returns in back-office work.

Caveat. Labelled preliminary by its authors, with a strict definition of success. Widely quoted and widely contested. Treat as directional.

42%

of companies abandoned most of their AI initiatives before production, up from 17% a year earlier. On average 46% of proofs of concept were scrapped.

S&P Global Market Intelligence, 2025

How to read this number

Method. Survey of more than 1,000 respondents in North America and Europe.

Caveat. Abandonment is not always failure. Stopping weak projects early is what disciplined portfolio management looks like.

22%

of organizations have scaled AI across multiple business units or adopted an AI-first approach. About 11% could not say what their functions spent on AI.

Gartner C-suite survey, September 2026

How to read this number

Method. 1,303 respondents at organizations with at least $50 million in revenue, January to April 2026. The most widely pursued use cases were rarely the ones with the best reported returns.

Caveat. Gartner’s release does not define the threshold for a “positive return”.

28%

of AI use cases in infrastructure and operations fully succeed and meet ROI expectations. 20% fail outright.

Gartner I&O survey, April 2026

How to read this number

Method. 782 infrastructure and operations leaders, November to December 2025. Success was credited to integration with existing workflows and full executive support; 38% named data quality or availability as a direct cause of failure.

Caveat. Covers IT infrastructure and operations only. It is often misquoted as a figure for all AI use cases.

25%

of organizations have moved 40% or more of their AI pilots into production. 34% are using AI to change products, processes or business models in depth.

Deloitte, State of AI in the Enterprise 2026

How to read this number

Method. 3,235 director-to-C-suite leaders in 24 countries, August to September 2025. Two-thirds report productivity gains; 37% use AI with little or no change to existing processes.

Caveat. Respondents were chosen for direct involvement in AI, so the sample leans toward more active organizations.

1 in 5

companies has a mature governance model for autonomous AI agents, while close to three-quarters plan to deploy agents within two years.

Deloitte, State of AI in the Enterprise 2026

How to read this number

Method. Same survey as above. BCG’s 2026 index reports a similar gap: 42% expect to give agents real decision authority by 2030, and 5% have the full set of controls in place.

Caveat. “Mature” is self-assessed.

40%

of respondents at organizations with more than $1 billion in revenue are scaling AI agents in at least one function, up from 27%. At smaller organizations the share is flat at 22%.

McKinsey, The state of AI in 2026

How to read this number

Method. Same 2026 survey as the EBIT figure. About two in ten respondents report agents scaling across the whole organization, and 32% have declined to buy a software product because they could build it with agentic coding tools.

Caveat. Scaling in one function is not enterprise-wide scaling. The gap by company size suggests agent scale currently depends on resources and engineering depth.

280×

fall in the cost of querying a model at GPT-3.5 level, from $20 to $0.07 per million tokens between November 2022 and October 2024.

Stanford HAI, AI Index 2025

How to read this number

Method. Compiled from Epoch AI and Artificial Analysis pricing data, holding benchmark performance constant.

Caveat. Measures a fixed capability level. Frontier-model and agentic workloads cost far more per task.

53%

population-level adoption of generative AI within three years, faster than the personal computer or the internet. Reported AI incidents reached a record 362 in 2025.

Stanford HAI, AI Index 2026

How to read this number

Method. Annual independent compilation of third-party data. The 2025 edition recorded 78% of organizations using AI in 2024, up from 55%.

Caveat. Adoption counts use, not value. Incident counts depend on what gets reported.

The pattern across sources: access to models is no longer the constraint. Redesigned workflows, usable data, accountable owners and controls are.

Statistics in circulation that this page leaves out
  • A RAND “analysis of 2,400 enterprise AI initiatives” with an 80.3% failure rate and success rates by CEO involvement. RAND’s published study interviewed 65 practitioners and cites the 80% figure as an outside estimate. We could not trace the larger dataset or its percentages to a RAND publication.
  • “42% of companies abandoned AI initiatives” credited to Deloitte, with an average sunk cost of $7.2 million. The 42% figure belongs to S&P Global Market Intelligence. We found no primary source for the cost figure.
  • MIT’s 95% finding credited to MIT Sloan. It comes from Project NANDA at the MIT Media Lab and is labelled preliminary by its authors.
  • Quotations attributed to the Stanford AI Index or to consulting firms that do not appear in the documents they are credited to.

AI strategy, transformation, governance, implementation: four terms that get used as one

Strategy, transformation, governance and implementation answer different questions and belong to different owners. Programs stall when one is asked to do another’s job.

AI strategy answers

Where will AI change our economics, and what will we fund to make that happen?

Owner
CEO and executive committee
Produces
Ambition, a prioritized portfolio, an investment envelope, success measures
Horizon
One to three years, refreshed at least annually
Missing looks like
Many pilots, no shared definition of success, spending nobody can total

AI transformation answers

How must work, roles and operating model change so the value shows up?

Owner
Business unit leaders with a transformation office
Produces
Redesigned workflows, new roles and skills, changed incentives
Horizon
Multi-year and continuous
Missing looks like
Tools deployed onto unchanged processes, time saved that never becomes results

AI governance answers

Who may decide what, under which controls, and who answers for the outcome?

Owner
Board, risk and compliance, and a named accountable executive
Produces
Decision rights, an AI inventory, risk assessments, monitoring, audit evidence
Horizon
Standing function
Missing looks like
Shadow AI, agents acting without limits, liability discovered after an incident

AI implementation answers

How do we build, integrate, run and improve each system?

Owner
Product, data and engineering teams with the business owner
Produces
Working systems in production, pipelines, evaluations, run books
Horizon
Weeks to quarters per release
Missing looks like
Demos that impress and never reach production

AI strategy frameworks worth using, and what each is for

No single framework is an AI strategy. Each of these does one job well. Pick by the question you need answered.

MIT CISR Enterprise AI Maturity

Where are we, and what does the next stage take?

A four-stage model built on a 2022 survey of 721 companies and updated in 2025. Enterprises in the first two stages performed below their industry’s financial average; those in stages three and four performed well above it. The largest gain comes from moving between stage two and stage three.

  1. Stage 1: Experiment and prepare
  2. Stage 2: Build pilots and capabilities
  3. Stage 3: Develop AI ways of working
  4. Stage 4: Become AI future-ready

Four challenges block the move from stage 2 to stage 3: strategy (tie investment to goals and measurable value), systems (modular, interoperable platforms and data), synchronization (AI-ready people and redesigned work) and stewardship (compliant, human-centred, transparent practice by design).

Use it for

Board-level diagnosis and for arguing that more pilots will not close the gap.

Its limit

The financial link is a correlation from survey data that mostly predates generative AI.

MIT CISR research briefing, December 2024 and 2025 update, MIT Sloan summary

RAND root causes of failure

Why do AI projects fail, and what prevents it?

Drawn from interviews with 65 experienced practitioners. Useful as a pre-mortem checklist for any proposed initiative.

  1. Leaders misunderstand or miscommunicate the problem AI is meant to solve
  2. The organization lacks the data needed to train an effective model
  3. Teams chase the newest technology instead of the user’s problem
  4. Infrastructure to manage data and deploy models is underfunded
  5. AI is applied to problems that are too hard for it

RAND’s advice to leaders follows from the list: make sure technical staff understand the purpose, commit to each problem for long enough, and invest in data and deployment infrastructure before models.

Use it for

Reviewing a business case before approval.

Its limit

Qualitative and practitioner-side. It explains causes, it does not measure rates.

RAND research report RR-A2680-1, 2024

BCG 10-20-70

Where should the effort and money go?

BCG’s rule of thumb from its client work: about 10% of the value of AI comes from algorithms, 20% from technology and data, and 70% from people, organization and process. It pairs with three value plays of rising ambition.

  1. Deploy: everyday productivity tools
  2. Reshape: redesign whole functions and workflows
  3. Invent: new products, services and business models

BCG reports that leading companies expect most of their AI value from reshaping and inventing, and plan to upskill more than half their workforce.

Use it for

Challenging a budget that is mostly licences and infrastructure.

Its limit

A heuristic from one firm’s engagements, not a measured constant.

BCG, The Formula for Agentic AI Value, 2026 and BCG, The Widening AI Value Gap, 2025

McKinsey agentic organization

What does the organization look like when agents do real work?

A proposed operating paradigm in which people work alongside virtual and physical AI agents. McKinsey argues the shift is to rebuild workflows around agents, and not to bolt agents onto existing processes.

  1. Business model
  2. Operating model
  3. Governance
  4. Workforce, people and culture
  5. Technology and data

On governance the article is specific: periodic, paper-based review cannot keep up with agents that run continuously, so controls have to be embedded and real-time, with humans holding final accountability.

Use it for

Structuring a leadership discussion on agentic AI across more than technology.

Its limit

A forward-looking thesis from consulting experience. Evidence at scale is still thin.

McKinsey, The agentic organization, 2025

NIST AI Risk Management Framework

How do we run AI risk management day to day?

A voluntary framework from the US National Institute of Standards and Technology. It is a method, not a certification. A companion profile covers generative AI.

  1. Govern: policies, accountability, culture and oversight
  2. Map: context, intended use, stakeholders and impacts
  3. Measure: test, evaluate and monitor risks
  4. Manage: prioritize, respond and track residual risk

Govern cuts across the other three functions. NIST notes that version 1.0 is under revision.

Use it for

The operating method for your risk process, whatever your jurisdiction.

Its limit

It is not an enterprise strategy and gives no certificate to show a regulator or customer.

NIST AI RMF and AI RMF 1.0 (PDF)

ISO/IEC 42001

How do we prove we manage AI responsibly?

The international, certifiable standard for an AI management system, published in 2023. It follows the same management-system structure as ISO/IEC 27001: leadership commitment, defined roles, risk and impact assessment, operational controls and continual improvement.

  1. Policy, scope and leadership
  2. Roles and responsibilities
  3. AI risk and impact assessment
  4. Operational controls across the AI life cycle
  5. Monitoring, audit and improvement

A practical combination used by many organizations: run risk with the NIST method, hold it inside an ISO/IEC 42001 management system, and map legal duties such as the EU AI Act into that system so one control satisfies several requirements.

Use it for

Institutional governance that customers, partners and auditors can verify.

Its limit

Certifies that a management system exists and works. It does not certify that any individual AI system is safe or valuable.

ISO/IEC 42001:2023

EU AI Act

What are we legally required to do?

Binding, risk-tiered law for providers and deployers of AI in the EU market. Some practices are prohibited, high-risk uses such as hiring, credit and critical infrastructure carry heavy obligations, and many other systems carry transparency duties.

  1. Prohibited practices
  2. High-risk systems
  3. Transparency obligations
  4. General-purpose AI model obligations

Amendments that entered into force on 27 July 2026 postponed the high-risk obligations: 2 December 2027 for stand-alone systems and 2 August 2028 for AI embedded in regulated products. The transparency obligations largely kept their 2026 schedule. See the dates in the governance tool.

Use it for

The legal baseline for anything that touches EU residents or markets, and a reference model for risk tiers elsewhere.

Its limit

A compliance floor. Meeting it says nothing about whether the AI creates value.

Regulation (EU) 2024/1689 and 2026 amendments summarized

OECD principles and classification

How do we describe and compare AI systems and their impact?

Two instruments. The OECD AI Principles, adopted in 2019 and updated in 2024, are the most widely endorsed policy reference for trustworthy AI. The 2022 classification framework describes any AI system along five dimensions.

  1. People and planet
  2. Economic context
  3. Data and input
  4. AI model
  5. Task and output

The five principles: inclusive growth and well-being; human rights and democratic values, including fairness and privacy; transparency and explainability; robustness, security and safety; accountability.

Use it for

Policy work, national strategies, and a common vocabulary for impact assessment.

Its limit

High level by design. It tells you what to consider, not how to implement it.

OECD AI Principles and Classification framework, 2022

Gartner AI maturity model

How do we benchmark capability across functions?

Gartner’s long-standing model places organizations on five levels. Its current assessment toolkit scores seven pillars: strategy, data, governance, engineering, operating model, culture, and AI product and value.

  1. Level 1: Awareness
  2. Level 2: Active
  3. Level 3: Operational
  4. Level 4: Systemic
  5. Level 5: Transformational

Gartner’s 2025 research found that 45% of high-maturity organizations keep AI initiatives in production for three years or more, and 91% have appointed dedicated AI leaders.

Use it for

A structured gap analysis by capability when you need more granularity than four stages.

Its limit

The full instrument and benchmarks sit behind a client subscription.

Gartner AI maturity assessment and Gartner maturity survey, 2025

An AI strategy is a system of twelve decisions

These are not separate workstreams. A use case chosen without checking data, ownership and run cost looks good on a slide and fails in production. Select a dimension to see the decision it forces.

Strategic alignment

The decision

Which corporate objectives is AI meant to move, and by how much?

What it should produce

A short statement of AI ambition tied to named corporate goals.

How it fails

An “AI vision” nobody can connect to the plan the board already approved.

AI opportunity

The decision

Where are decisions slow, costly or inaccurate enough that AI changes the economics?

What it should produce

An opportunity map built top-down from strategy and bottom-up from operations.

How it fails

A list of use cases gathered from vendors.

Portfolio prioritization

The decision

Which ten to twenty use cases get funded first, and which are stopped?

What it should produce

A scored, ranked portfolio with explicit gates.

How it fails

Everything is a priority, so nothing reaches production.

Operating model

The decision

Who builds, who owns and who runs AI?

What it should produce

A defined model, usually federated: central standards and platforms, business ownership of outcomes.

How it fails

A central lab the business ignores, or business units duplicating each other.

Data strategy

The decision

Is the data each priority use case needs available, good enough and lawful to use?

What it should produce

Use-case-specific data readiness assessments and named data owners.

How it fails

A generic data-quality program with no link to any use case.

Technology architecture

The decision

What shared platform lets teams build once and reuse?

What it should produce

Reference architecture and sourcing position for models, platforms and agents.

How it fails

A different stack for every pilot and no route to production.

Talent and capabilities

The decision

Which skills do leaders, specialists and the wider workforce need, and how will they get them?

What it should produce

A capability plan covering leadership fluency, technical roles and workforce adoption.

How it fails

Training everyone on prompts while no role or workflow changes.

Governance and risk

The decision

Who approves, who monitors, and who can switch a system off?

What it should produce

Decision rights, an AI inventory, risk tiers and continuous monitoring.

How it fails

A policy document with no owner and no evidence.

Investment strategy

The decision

What is the three-year commitment, and how is it released?

What it should produce

A staged funding model tied to evidence at each gate.

How it fails

Annual budgeting that starves what works and keeps funding what does not.

Execution roadmap

The decision

What ships in which quarter, and what must be true first?

What it should produce

A sequenced roadmap with dependencies and owners.

How it fails

Pilots with no plan for integration, support or adoption.

Performance measurement

The decision

How will the board know AI is delivering?

What it should produce

A scorecard of realized value against pre-agreed baselines.

How it fails

Counting pilots, licences and people trained.

Continuous adaptation

The decision

How often is the strategy revisited as models, costs and rules change?

What it should produce

A review cadence and triggers for changing course.

How it fails

A three-year plan written once for a technology that changes quarterly.

The twelve-dimension model is the author’s synthesis, not an external standard.

    An AI strategy roadmap: twelve months from diagnosis to scaled value

    A sequence that holds for most large organizations. The durations are planning assumptions. The order matters more than the dates.

    1. Diagnose Months 1 to 2

    • Assess maturity across the twelve dimensions
    • Review corporate objectives and constraints
    • Establish baselines for the outcomes you intend to move
    • Agree the level of ambition
    Deliverable
    A maturity assessment and a stated ambition
    Gate to pass
    Agreement in the executive team on what success means, in numbers.

    2. Design Months 3 to 4

    • Score and rank the portfolio
    • Choose the operating model
    • Set architecture and sourcing positions
    • Define decision rights and risk tiers
    • Build the three-year business case
    Deliverable
    An approved strategy and investment plan
    Gate to pass
    Named executive owners for every funded use case.

    3. Build foundations Months 5 to 6

    • Stand up leadership and accountability
    • Prepare data and shared platforms for the first use cases
    • Start workforce capability building
    • Launch the initial use cases
    Deliverable
    Working foundations and first releases
    Gate to pass
    Data readiness confirmed for each launched use case.

    4. Industrialize Months 7 to 9

    • Integrate AI into redesigned workflows
    • Validate performance and controls
    • Set up production monitoring
    • Scale what works and measure realized value
    Deliverable
    A production portfolio
    Gate to pass
    Evidence of realized, not projected, value.

    5. Scale and adapt Months 10 to 12

    • Extend across business units
    • Retire what has not delivered
    • Rebalance investment
    • Introduce agentic systems where controls are ready
    • Refresh the roadmap
    Deliverable
    A value-realization report to the board
    Gate to pass
    A decision on year-two funding based on results.

    Six documented cases

    Three organizations that scaled, three that paid for a gap in strategy or control. Each links to a primary or first-hand source.

    Measured value

    DBS Bank

    DBS reports that its data and AI work delivered more than S$750 million of economic value in 2024, over double the prior year, from more than 1,500 models across more than 370 use cases. It is one of few companies to publish a figure for AI value.

    Lesson. Decide how you will measure value before you scale, then publish the number internally every year.

    DBS Annual Report 2024

    From pilot to scale

    Guardian Life

    MIT CISR describes how Guardian tracks each initiative from hypothesis to pilot to scale. Automating request-for-proposal and quoting work cut turnaround from about a week to 24 hours. Risk, legal and compliance sit inside governance, with a fast-track architecture board.

    Lesson. A value-tracking discipline and embedded control functions are what let a pilot become a platform.

    MIT Sloan on MIT CISR research, 2025

    Operations and people

    Italgas

    Europe’s largest gas distributor digitized assets from 2017 and built a shared data platform. One tool accelerated construction projects by 40% and cut inspections by 80%. Each sprint has a C-level sponsor, and the company delivered 30,000 hours of AI and data training in 2024.

    Lesson. Years of data and platform work came first. Executive sponsorship is attached to each product, not to the program in general.

    MIT CISR research briefing, August 2025

    Cost was the only measure

    Klarna

    In 2024 Klarna said its AI assistant handled two-thirds of customer chats in its first month, the work of about 700 agents. In May 2025 its chief executive said cost had weighed too heavily in the evaluation, that quality had suffered, and that the company was hiring people for customer service again.

    Lesson. Efficiency is a valid goal and a poor sole measure. Put quality and customer outcomes on the scorecard from day one.

    Bloomberg, May 2025

    Accountability

    Air Canada

    A customer relied on wrong information about bereavement fares from the airline’s website chatbot. In February 2024 a British Columbia tribunal held the airline liable for negligent misrepresentation and rejected the argument that the chatbot was responsible for its own words.

    Lesson. You are accountable for what your AI tells customers. Someone must own its accuracy.

    Moffatt v. Air Canada, 2024 BCCRT 149

    Model risk at scale

    Zillow Offers

    Zillow bought homes at prices set with the help of its forecasting models. In November 2021 it wound the business down, citing the unpredictability of home-price forecasting, wrote down about $304 million of inventory that quarter and cut roughly a quarter of its workforce.

    Lesson. When a model’s output commits capital automatically, limits and stress tests belong in the strategy, not in a later review.

    Zillow Group Form 10-Q, Q3 2021

    Six priorities, in order

    Where the evidence and the frameworks agree on what to do first.

    1. Agree what success means before any build

      RAND’s leading cause of failure is leadership misunderstanding or miscommunicating the problem. Gartner’s successful teams credit full executive support and realistic business cases. Write the outcome, the baseline and the owner down first.

    2. Judge data readiness per use case

      Data that is ready for one use case is not ready for another. In Gartner’s 2026 operations survey, 38% of leaders named data quality or availability as a direct cause of failure. Assess it for each priority use case and fund the gap.

    3. Redesign workflows, do not add tools to them

      Nearly three-quarters of McKinsey’s 2026 high performers have fundamentally redesigned workflows, against about a quarter of everyone else. Deloitte finds 37% of organizations using AI with little or no process change. BCG puts 70% of the value in people and process.

    4. Build governance as operating infrastructure

      Run risk with the NIST method, hold it in an ISO/IEC 42001 management system, and map legal duties into it once. Keep a simple matrix of control, evidence and requirement satisfied, so an audit is a lookup.

    5. Prepare for agents by fixing controls first

      Large organizations are scaling agents quickly and few have mature governance for them. Identity, permissions, approval points, limits, logs and a tested stop come before autonomy in production.

    6. Manage AI as a portfolio and measure realized value

      Gartner’s high performers track returns continuously, reallocate, and stop what underperforms. The cost of intelligence has collapsed, which makes disciplined choice of where to use it the scarce skill.

    Enterprise AI strategy and national AI strategy are different work

    The method overlaps. The stakeholders, instruments and measures of success do not.

    Enterprise and national AI strategy compared
    AspectEnterprise AI strategyNational AI strategy
    PurposeBusiness value and competitive advantageEconomic, societal and security value for a country
    DataEnterprise data and its governanceNational data infrastructure and sharing rules
    PeopleWorkforce transformationNational talent pipeline and education
    ControlEnterprise governance and complianceRegulation and institutions
    InnovationAI-enabled products and servicesA national ecosystem of research, startups and compute
    Measure of successFinancial return and realized benefitsProductivity, GDP contribution and public value

    National strategies also carry choices an enterprise never faces: compute and data infrastructure, language coverage, public-sector adoption, and the regulatory regime itself. The OECD AI Principles are the common reference point for the policy side.
