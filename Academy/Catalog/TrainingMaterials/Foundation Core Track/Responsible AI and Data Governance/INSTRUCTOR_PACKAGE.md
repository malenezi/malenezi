## Course Description

A governance-focused module aligning practice with Saudi national frameworks including PDPL, NDMO data standards, and SDAIA AI-ethics principles. Participants learn to assess fairness, transparency, privacy, and accountability across the AI lifecycle. The module prepares professionals to build and operate AI systems that are compliant and trustworthy by design.

The course is built around a single evolving artefact: the **AI Governance File for "Ameen" (أمين)** — a fictional benefit-eligibility scoring system operated by a fictional government body, the Social Support Authority (SSA). Every lab produces one section of that file: an ethics self-assessment and risk-tier record (Day 1), a PDPL/NDMO compliance checklist with a gap register (Day 1), a bias-audit report (Day 2), a privacy risk assessment and anonymisation plan (Day 2), a model card and audit-trail design (Day 3), and a risk register with a third-party AI assessment (Day 3). By the final afternoon each participant owns a complete, defensible governance file — the same dossier a real governance office would put in front of a review committee — and defends it in a panel-style capstone that doubles as the module's governance case analysis.

## Learning Outcomes

By the end of this course, participants will be able to:

1. **LO1** — Explain Saudi data-protection requirements (PDPL) and NDMO governance standards

# Module 1 — The Responsible-AI Landscape and SDAIA Ethics Principles

## Module Overview

**Purpose.** Most AI governance failures are not caused by malicious teams but by teams that treated ethics as a poster on the wall rather than a set of testable obligations. This module gives participants the map: the global responsible-AI landscape, the SDAIA AI-ethics principles as the Kingdom's operative framework, risk-based classification of AI systems, and the discipline of turning each principle into concrete controls at specific lifecycle phases. Everything later in the course — PDPL checklists, bias audits, model cards, risk registers — is a mechanism for one of these principles.

**Business relevance.** The National Strategy for Data & AI positions the Kingdom as a global AI leader, and that ambition is conditional on trust: government entities adopting AI at scale, citizens consenting to data-driven services, and international partners recognising Saudi governance standards. Organisations that can demonstrate principled AI practice win government contracts, pass procurement reviews, and avoid the reputational incidents that have set peer programs back years elsewhere. For practitioners, principle literacy is now interview material and review-gate material in every serious Saudi AI employer.

**Industry use cases.**
- A ministry's AI-procurement committee requires every proposal to state which SDAIA risk tier the system falls into and how each of the seven principles is addressed — vendors who cannot answer are disqualified before technical evaluation.
- A bank's model-risk function maps the SDAIA principles onto its existing SAMA model-governance framework so one evidence pack serves two reviewers.
- A health-tech startup classifies its triage-assist tool as high-risk early, budgets for human-oversight features from the first sprint, and passes its hospital client's ethics review on the first attempt.

**Expected competencies.** After this module a participant can name and explain the seven SDAIA AI-ethics principles, classify an AI system into a risk tier and defend the classification, map principles to lifecycle phases and concrete controls, run a structured ethics self-assessment, and open a governance gap register that survives audit.

## Technical Content

### 1. Why technically sound AI systems fail governance review

An AI system can be accurate, fast, and profitable — and still be ungovernable. The failure modes worth naming in class, because every one of them recurs in the labs:

- **Unexamined training data:** a benefit-eligibility model trained on historical decisions inherits every historical inequity in those decisions; accuracy against biased labels *is* bias, formalised.
- **Silent scope creep:** a model built to prioritise inspection visits gets reused to deny licences — same scores, different consequences, no re-assessment. Purpose limitation is an AI problem, not just a data problem.
- **Accountability vacuum:** when a citizen asks "why was I rejected?", no one can answer — the vendor points at the ministry, the ministry points at the model, and the model has no documentation. The absence of a named accountable owner is itself a finding.
- **Privacy debt:** features were added because they lifted AUC by 0.4%, not because they were necessary; three years later the retention review finds sensitive attributes nobody can justify holding.
- **Un-monitorable deployment:** the system works at launch, drifts for two years, and nobody notices because "monitoring" meant uptime, not fairness or accuracy over subgroups.

**Instructor note:** open with 5 minutes of participant-sourced examples ("what's the worst AI decision you've heard of?") before showing the canonical international incidents (recruitment screening bias, exam-grading algorithms, welfare-fraud scoring scandals — describe them generically). Participants will supply 80% of the failure taxonomy unprompted; your job is to name the categories.

### 2. The global landscape in one map

Participants need just enough of the global picture to place the Saudi framework in context:

| Framework | Type | One-line practitioner takeaway |
|---|---|---|
| **OECD AI Principles (2019)** | Intergovernmental principles | The common ancestor: human-centred values, transparency, robustness, accountability — most national frameworks, including the Kingdom's, are interoperable with it |
| **NIST AI RMF 1.0 (2023)** | Voluntary risk framework | Four functions — Govern, Map, Measure, Manage — a useful verb set for structuring an AI risk program |
| **EU AI Act** | Binding regulation (phased application) | Risk-tiered obligations: prohibited / high-risk / limited / minimal; the reference point for vendors selling into Europe |
| **ISO/IEC 42001 (2023)** | Certifiable management-system standard | "ISO 9001 for AI" — an AI management system (AIMS) that auditors can certify; increasingly requested in procurement |
| **SDAIA AI Ethics Principles** | National framework (KSA) | The operative framework for this course: seven principles, risk-based, lifecycle-mapped |

The convergence matters more than the differences: every serious framework demands **risk-proportionate controls, documented accountability, fairness evidence, privacy protection, and human oversight**. Teams that build these once can answer to any of the frameworks.

### 3. The seven SDAIA AI-ethics principles as practitioner obligations

The SDAIA AI Ethics Principles are the Kingdom's reference framework for anyone developing, procuring, or operating AI systems. Teach each principle in three parts: what it demands, what artefact evidences it, and where in this course it is operationalised.

| # | Principle | What it demands of a practitioner | Evidence artefact | Where in this course |
|---|---|---|---|---|
| 1 | **Fairness** | Identify protected/affected groups; measure and mitigate unjustified disparate outcomes across them | Bias-audit report | M3, Lab 3 |
| 2 | **Privacy & Security** | Process only necessary personal data, protect it across the lifecycle, honour data-subject rights | Privacy risk assessment; PDPL checklist | M2, M4, Labs 2 & 4 |
| 3 | **Humanity** | Keep meaningful human agency: human oversight for consequential decisions, no manipulation, human dignity preserved | Human-oversight design record | M1, M5 |
| 4 | **Social & Environmental Benefits** | Justify the system by net benefit; consider societal and environmental externalities, not only sponsor KPIs | Benefit/impact statement in the governance file | M1, Capstone |
| 5 | **Reliability & Safety** | The system performs as intended under expected and reasonably foreseeable conditions; failure modes are known and bounded | Evaluation results; monitoring plan | M5, M6 |
| 6 | **Transparency & Explainability** | Affected people know AI is involved; decisions can be explained at the level the audience needs | Model card; decision notices | M5, Lab 5 |
| 7 | **Accountability & Responsibility** | A named human/role answers for the system at every lifecycle stage; audit trails make reconstruction possible | RACI; audit-trail design; risk register | M5, M6 |

Three teaching emphases:

1. **Principles conflict, and governance is the discipline of resolving conflicts explicitly.** Transparency can tension with privacy (explaining a decision may reveal another person's data); fairness interventions can tension with reliability (constraining a model changes its error profile). Mature teams write the trade-off down and have the accountable owner sign it — that written record is the difference between a defensible judgement and negligence.
2. **Every principle needs an artefact.** "We care about fairness" is not evidence; a dated bias-audit report with named reviewers is. The course's governance file is precisely the artefact set for the seven principles.
3. **Principles apply to bought systems as much as built ones.** Procuring a third-party service does not outsource the obligation (M6 makes this concrete).

### 4. Risk-based classification of AI systems

Both the SDAIA framework and international practice scale obligations to risk. Teach a four-tier working model:

| Tier | Description | Examples | Governance consequence |
|---|---|---|---|
| **Unacceptable** | Systems whose intended use violates rights or law | Social scoring of citizens' general behaviour; manipulative targeting of vulnerable groups | Do not build or buy; escalate if requested |
| **High risk** | Consequential effects on individuals' rights, safety, access to essential services | Benefit eligibility (Ameen), hiring screening, credit scoring, medical triage, law-enforcement uses | Full governance file: impact assessment, bias audit, human oversight, documentation, monitoring, periodic review |
| **Limited risk** | Meaningful interaction or influence, limited individual consequence | Citizen-facing chatbots, recommendation systems, productivity assistants on internal data | Transparency obligations (disclose AI involvement), baseline documentation, proportionate review |
| **Minimal risk** | Negligible effect on individuals | Spam filtering, predictive maintenance on equipment, internal document search | Inventory entry + standard engineering hygiene |

The classification decision itself must be **documented and reviewable**: a one-page decision record stating the system's purpose, affected population, decision consequence, autonomy level (decides / recommends / informs), and the resulting tier with rationale. Classification is revisited whenever purpose, population, data, or autonomy changes — scope creep re-opens the question (failure mode #2 above).

**The classification heuristic that survives contact with reality:** ask what happens to a specific person when the system is wrong in each direction. A false rejection in Ameen means an eligible family loses support — that single sentence justifies the high-risk tier faster than any scoring matrix.

### 5. Principles across the AI lifecycle

The SDAIA framework expects principles to be applied across the AI lifecycle, not bolted on at the end. Use a four-phase working lifecycle and make participants place controls in phases:

| Lifecycle phase | Key governance questions | Example controls |
|---|---|---|
| **Plan & design** | Is the purpose legitimate and proportionate? Which tier? Who is accountable? What data is truly necessary? | Risk-tier decision record; benefit statement; data-minimisation review; DPIA trigger check |
| **Prepare data** | Is the data lawfully sourced, representative, classified, and minimised? | RoPA entry; NDMO classification; representativeness analysis; datasheet for the dataset |
| **Build & validate** | Does the model meet accuracy *and* fairness targets? Are limits documented? | Bias audit; evaluation on subgroups; model card drafted; human-oversight design |
| **Deploy & monitor** | Do real-world outcomes stay within tolerances? Can decisions be reconstructed? When do we retire? | Audit trail; drift and fairness monitoring; incident and appeal channels; periodic re-assessment; decommissioning plan |

Two consequences matter most:

1. **Governance effort front-loads.** The cheapest place to fix a fairness problem is the purpose statement; the most expensive is production. Teams that skip Plan & Design controls pay for them later with interest.
2. **Every phase leaves evidence.** The governance file is not written at the end — it accretes phase by phase. This is exactly how the course's labs are sequenced.

### 6. Common mistakes & operational considerations

**Mistakes to name explicitly (each is seeded somewhere in the course materials):**
1. **Ethics-washing:** publishing principles without artefacts, owners, or gates. Detectable in one question: "show me the last decision this framework changed."
2. **Classification by convenience:** rating a system limited-risk because high-risk is expensive. The decision record and its reviewability are the antidote.
3. **One-time assessment:** running the ethics review at launch and never again, while data, population, and purpose drift.
4. **Principle-to-slide mapping instead of principle-to-control mapping:** a deck that quotes all seven principles but a delivery workflow with zero gates.
5. **Accountability diffusion:** "the committee is responsible" — committees advise; named roles answer. Every artefact in this course carries an owner field for this reason.
6. **Copying foreign frameworks wholesale:** importing an EU-shaped process that ignores PDPL specifics, NDMO classification levels, and Saudi institutional structures — locally non-compliant and organisationally alien.

**Operational considerations.** Position the governance function as an enabler with teeth: it should make the *right* path the *fast* path (templates, checklists, pre-approved patterns) while holding real veto power at defined gates. Budget governance effort proportionate to tier — a minimal-risk system should clear review in minutes with an inventory entry; reserving human attention for high-risk systems is itself a governance design decision.

## Worked Examples

### Worked Example 1 — Risk-tier decision record for Ameen (filled)

```
AI SYSTEM RISK-TIER DECISION RECORD                    SSA-GOV-001 v1.0
──────────────────────────────────────────────────────────────────────
System name:        Ameen — benefit-eligibility scoring
Owner (accountable): Director, Benefits Operations (named individual in the real file)
Purpose:            Score applications to the SSA family-support programme to
                    prioritise caseworker review and recommend eligibility
Affected population: All applicant households (~1.2M applications/year)
Decision consequence: Recommendation feeds caseworker decision on monthly
                    financial support; false rejection delays or denies
                    support to an eligible family
Autonomy level:     RECOMMENDS (caseworker decides; >92% of recommendations
                    accepted unchanged — treat as de-facto deciding)
Special categories: Household income, family composition, disability status
                    (sensitive), region, nationality
──────────────────────────────────────────────────────────────────────
TIER: HIGH RISK
Rationale: consequential effect on access to essential support; sensitive
data; scale; high de-facto autonomy given caseworker acceptance rates.
Consequences: full governance file required (impact assessment, bias audit,
model card, human-oversight design, audit trail, annual re-assessment).
Review triggers: any change to purpose, data sources, model family,
population, or autonomy level; else annual.
Reviewed by: AI Governance Office        Date: (course date)    Next: +12m
```

**Teaching point:** the parenthetical on autonomy is the record's most important line. A "recommendation" system with a 92% acceptance rate is a decision system wearing a disclaimer — governance classifies by *effect*, not by label.

### Worked Example 2 — Ethics self-assessment scorecard (template + filled excerpt)

Scoring scale: **0** = absent · **1** = ad-hoc/undocumented · **2** = documented but unevidenced or unowned · **3** = documented, evidenced, owned, and reviewed.

| Principle | Probe question (one of 3–5 per principle) | Score | Evidence / gap |
|---|---|---|---|
| Fairness | Has disparate impact been measured across defined groups within the last 12 months? | 0 | Never measured → **GAP-01 (critical)** |
| Privacy & Security | Is there a current RoPA entry and a completed privacy impact assessment? | 1 | Data flows known informally; no RoPA entry → **GAP-02 (critical)** |
| Humanity | Can a caseworker override the recommendation, and is override friction measured? | 2 | Override exists; acceptance rate not monitored → **GAP-03 (major)** |
| Social & Env. Benefits | Is there a written benefit statement weighing societal cost/benefit? | 1 | Business case exists; no societal analysis → GAP-04 (minor) |
| Reliability & Safety | Are accuracy tolerances defined, with action thresholds, per subgroup? | 1 | Global AUC tracked; no subgroup tolerances → GAP-05 (major) |
| Transparency & Explainability | Are applicants informed that scoring is algorithm-assisted? | 0 | No disclosure in application journey → **GAP-06 (critical)** |
| Accountability & Responsibility | Is there a named owner and a reconstructable decision trail? | 1 | Owner named; logs insufficient to reconstruct decisions → GAP-07 (major) |

**Reading the scorecard:** the profile (three criticals concentrated in Fairness, Privacy, Transparency) is typical of systems built model-first. The gap register, not the scores, is the deliverable — scores prioritise; gaps get owners and dates.

### Worked Example 3 — Principle-to-control mapping (excerpt used as the Lab 1 answer key)

| Lifecycle phase | Fairness | Transparency & Explainability | Accountability & Responsibility |
|---|---|---|---|
| Plan & design | Define protected groups + fairness metric *before* modelling | Plan disclosure text in the application journey | Name the accountable owner; define appeal path |
| Prepare data | Representativeness check vs applicant population | Datasheet documenting sources and known skews | Data-source sign-off recorded |
| Build & validate | Bias audit vs thresholds (M3) | Model card with subgroup results (M5) | Validation sign-off by someone who did not build it |
| Deploy & monitor | Quarterly fairness re-measurement on live decisions | Notice + explanation on every adverse decision | Immutable audit trail; override analytics (M5/M6) |

|---|---|

**Troubleshooting guidance**

| Symptom | Cause | Fix |
|---|---|---|
| Pair classifies Ameen as limited-risk | Anchored on "it only recommends" | Point them to the acceptance-rate fact; ask the "wrong in each direction" question |
| Scores cluster at 2 everywhere | Politeness bias / vague probes | Enforce the evidence rule: score 2 requires naming a document; score 3 requires naming a reviewer and date |

That moment ("one operational fact changed the classification") is the lab's core lesson. Fast finishers: draft the one-sentence disclosure text that would close GAP-06 and critique it against principle 6.

## Mini Exercises

**Quiz (5 questions)**
1. Name the seven SDAIA AI-ethics principles. → Fairness; Privacy & Security; Humanity; Social & Environmental Benefits; Reliability & Safety; Transparency & Explainability; Accountability & Responsibility.
2. What single question most efficiently tests a system's risk tier? → What happens to a specific person when the system is wrong in each direction?
3. Why does a high recommendation-acceptance rate change governance analysis? → De-facto autonomy: the system effectively decides, so oversight must be designed and measured, not assumed.
4. Give one example of two principles in tension and how the tension is resolved. → Transparency vs privacy in explanations; resolved by a documented trade-off signed by the accountable owner.
5. True/False: procuring an AI system transfers the ethics obligations to the vendor. → **False** — obligations follow the deployer; the vendor assessment (M6) is how they are discharged.

**Classification drill.** Eight one-line system descriptions (mosque-capacity forecaster, CV-screening tool, chatbot for visa FAQs, exam-proctoring vision system, traffic-signal optimiser, credit-limit adjuster, news recommender, general-behaviour citizen scoring). Individually tier them, then reconcile in pairs. Answer key marks the citizen-scoring item unacceptable and forces the "who is affected" argument for exam proctoring.

**Gap-hunting exercise.** Given a glossy (fictional) "AI Ethics Charter" one-pager, find the five governance smells (no owners, no gates, no metrics, no review cadence, no scope statement).

**Discussion questions.**
- Your sponsor says governance review adds three weeks and "the model is ready now." What proportionate fast-path do you offer for limited-risk systems, and what do you refuse to fast-path?
- Should the ethics self-assessment be done by the building team, an independent office, or both? What does each answer cost?

## Case Study — Complaint-Triage AI at a Major Municipality

**Scenario.** A large Saudi municipality deploys "Murshid", an AI triage system that reads citizen complaints (potholes, waste, lighting, safety hazards) from its service app and assigns priority and department routing. Eighteen months in, a journalist's data request shows that average resolution time in three lower-income districts is 2.4× the city average, and that complaints written in informal Arabic or by non-native speakers are systematically classified as lower priority.

**Business context.** The municipality's leadership had celebrated Murshid publicly (40% faster average triage). The disparity story lands during a national push on municipal service equity. The mayor's office asks the digital team for a response within two weeks: is the system fair, can we prove it either way, and what changes?

**Technical challenge.** No fairness measurement was ever defined; "priority" labels came from historical dispatcher decisions (which themselves under-prioritised the same districts); the NLP component was trained mostly on formal Arabic; district identifiers were never treated as a governance-relevant attribute.

**Constraints.** The team cannot take the system offline (complaint volume doubled since launch and manual triage can't absorb it); the response must be publishable, not just internal; the vendor's contract has no fairness or audit clauses (M6 foreshadowing).

**Solution approach (facilitate, don't lecture).** (1) Emergency measurement first: resolution time, priority distribution, and misrouting rate by district and language register — you cannot manage what you never measured. (2) Classify Murshid properly: consequential access to public services → high risk → full file. (3) Short-term mitigation: human review for the affected districts' complaints while the model is corrected. (4) Root-cause the label problem: historical dispatcher behaviour is the bias source — relabeling a stratified sample with explicit criteria is the fix, not a bigger model. (5) Publish a plain-language note: what was found, what changed, when it will be re-measured — transparency as trust repair.

**Discussion questions.**
1. Which SDAIA principles were violated, and at which lifecycle phase did each violation enter? (Fairness at Prepare-data via labels; Transparency at Deploy; Accountability throughout.)
2. The 40% average improvement is real. How do you talk about a system that is better on average and worse for specific groups?
3. What three clauses should the renewal contract add? (Audit rights, fairness SLAs, data access for measurement — bridge to M6.)
4. Draft the first sentence of the public note.

# Module 2 — PDPL and NDMO Data-Governance Frameworks

## Module Overview

**Purpose.** AI governance stands on data governance: no model is compliant if the data feeding it is unlawfully collected, over-retained, unclassified, or shared without basis. This module makes participants operationally literate in the Kingdom's two foundational instruments — the Personal Data Protection Law (PDPL) and the NDMO data-management and personal-data-protection standards — and teaches the working artefacts that evidence compliance: the PDPL obligations checklist, the record of processing activities (RoPA), the data-classification record, and the gap register.

**Business relevance.** PDPL is enforceable law with real administrative penalties, and SDAIA is the competent authority supervising it; NDMO standards structure how government and government-linked entities must manage data. For any organisation building AI in the Kingdom, these are not optional reference material — they are procurement gate, audit scope, and licence to operate. Practitioners who can complete a compliance assessment correctly save their organisations from the two most expensive outcomes: a regulator finding, and an AI programme frozen mid-flight because nobody can evidence the lawful basis of its training data.

**Industry use cases.**
- A retail group building a recommendation engine must establish the lawful basis for each data source, publish an adequate privacy notice, and honour deletion requests — including inside already-trained models' training sets.
- A government entity preparing data for a national AI platform must classify every dataset to NDMO levels before it can be shared, and document the sharing decision.
- A hospital group's research arm needs a defensible position on whether its "de-identified" patient extract is still personal data under PDPL (M4 completes this question).

**Expected competencies.** Participants can state PDPL's scope, legal bases, data-subject rights, and controller obligations; complete a PDPL compliance checklist and RoPA entry for a real data flow; classify data assets to national classification levels; and consolidate findings into a prioritised, owned gap register.

## Technical Content

### 1. PDPL orientation: what it is and who it reaches

The Personal Data Protection Law (issued by Royal Decree M/19 of 1443H / September 2021, amended by Royal Decree M/148 in March 2023) is the Kingdom's comprehensive data-protection law. It came into force in September 2023, with a transition period for entities to reach compliance, and is supported by Implementing Regulations and separate Personal Data Transfer Regulations. SDAIA is the competent authority supervising its application.

Scope points practitioners must hold precisely:

- **Personal data** is any data — in any form — that identifies an individual or renders them identifiable, directly or indirectly: name, ID numbers, addresses, phone numbers, photos, and combinations of attributes that single a person out. The "indirectly" clause is why M4's re-identification analysis is a legal exercise, not just a technical one.
- **Sensitive data** is a stricter category, including data revealing racial or ethnic origin, religious, intellectual or political belief, criminal and security data, biometric data, genetic data, health data, credit data, and data indicating that a person's parents are unknown. Sensitive data attracts tighter rules (e.g., legitimate interest is not an available basis).
- **Territorial reach:** the law applies to processing of personal data of individuals residing in the Kingdom, including processing by entities outside the Kingdom. Cloud location does not opt anyone out.
- **Roles:** the **controller** determines purpose and means; the **processor** processes on the controller's behalf. AI vendors are typically processors — until they reuse your data for their own model improvement, at which point they have become controllers of that processing and the contract must say what is allowed (M6 returns here).

### 2. Legal bases, rights, and the consent question in AI

Processing requires a legal basis. Consent is the default basis, and PDPL recognises alternatives including: processing that serves the **actual interest of the data subject** where contacting them is impossible or difficult; processing under **another law** or to implement an agreement to which the data subject is party; processing by a **public entity** for security or judicial purposes; and — since the 2023 amendment — **legitimate interest** of the controller for non-sensitive data, provided it does not prejudice the data subject's rights and expectations.

Data subjects hold rights that AI systems must be able to honour in practice, not just in policy: the right to be **informed** of the legal basis and purpose; the right to **access** their data; the right to obtain a **copy** in readable format; the right to **correction**; and the right to **destruction** of data no longer needed. Consent, where relied on, must be withdrawable as easily as it was given.

**The AI-specific teaching points:**

- **Purpose limitation bites hardest in AI.** Data collected to administer benefits was not thereby collected to train an eligibility model — teams must verify the original purpose covers model training or establish a new basis. "We already have the data" is an infrastructure statement, not a legal one.
- **Rights reach into pipelines.** A destruction request must propagate to feature stores, training extracts, backups within policy, and — the hard question — models trained on the data. Teach the practical position: maintain training-set lineage so you can state which model versions a subject's data influenced, and have a documented, defensible retraining/refresh policy.
- **Consent quality:** bundled, coerced, or buried consent fails its function. For a government benefit service, consent is often the *wrong* basis anyway (the power imbalance undermines voluntariness) — a statutory basis is usually the honest one. This nuance separates practitioners from checkbox-fillers.

### 3. Controller obligations that shape AI delivery

The obligations most relevant to AI teams, each with its artefact:

| Obligation | Practical meaning for an AI team | Artefact |
|---|---|---|
| **Privacy notice** | Individuals know what is collected, why, on what basis, and that algorithmic scoring is involved | Notice text in the application journey |
| **Records of processing (RoPA)** | Every processing activity is inventoried: purposes, categories, recipients, retention, safeguards | RoPA entry (Worked Example 2) |
| **Data minimisation & retention** | Collect only what the purpose needs; keep only as long as needed; justify every feature | Minimisation review (M4); retention schedule |
| **Accuracy** | Wrong inputs produce wrong decisions about people; correction rights are an accuracy control | Data-quality rules; correction workflow |
| **Security** | Organisational and technical measures proportionate to sensitivity; align with NCA controls where applicable | Security assessment reference |
| **Impact assessment** | Assess privacy impact for processing likely to pose high risk to data subjects — AI scoring of individuals is the canonical trigger | DPIA (M4 completes it) |
| **Breach notification** | Notify SDAIA of personal-data breaches per the Implementing Regulations' timeline (72 hours), and affected individuals where the breach may cause them harm | Incident procedure + register |
| **Processor contracts** | Binding terms on processors: purpose limits, security, sub-processing, audit | DPA clauses (M6 checklist) |
| **Cross-border transfers** | Transfers outside the Kingdom only under the Transfer Regulations' conditions: adequacy, appropriate safeguards (e.g., standard contractual clauses, binding common rules), or listed exceptions — with minimisation of what is transferred | Transfer assessment record |

Non-compliance carries administrative fines (up to SAR 5 million per violation, subject to doubling for repeat violations) and, for unlawful disclosure of sensitive data with intent to harm, criminal penalties including imprisonment. Use penalties to motivate, not to teach — the durable motivation is that every obligation above is also simply good AI engineering.

### 4. NDMO standards: the national data-management spine

The National Data Management Office (NDMO), within SDAIA, issued the National Data Management and Personal Data Protection Standards that government entities (and entities handling government data) implement. Teach the structure, not a clause-by-clause reading: the standards organise data management into **fifteen domains**, spanning data governance; data catalog and metadata; data quality; data operations; document and content management; data architecture and modelling; reference and master data; business intelligence and analytics; data sharing and interoperability; data value realization; open data; freedom of information; data classification; personal data protection; and data security and protection.

For AI work, four domains do most of the daily lifting:

1. **Data classification.** National practice classifies data by the impact of unauthorised disclosure into four levels — **Top Secret, Secret, Restricted, Public** (with Arabic labels used in official contexts). Classification drives everything downstream: storage controls, sharing decisions, cloud eligibility, anonymisation requirements. An unclassified dataset is an unmanaged risk.
2. **Data catalog & metadata.** You cannot govern data you cannot find. AI teams inherit this domain as feature/dataset documentation — the datasheets and lineage that M5's documentation practice extends.
3. **Data quality.** Completeness, accuracy, timeliness rules with owners — for AI, quality defects become decision defects about people (the accuracy obligation made operational).
4. **Data sharing & interoperability.** Sharing between entities follows documented request/approval flows with classification-appropriate safeguards. "Send me the CSV" is replaced by a sharing record that audit can reconstruct.

**How the two instruments interlock:** PDPL states *what the law requires* for personal data; NDMO standards provide *the management system* in which those requirements are executed and evidenced. In the compliance checklist (Worked Example 1) the two appear as one instrument with two sections — which is exactly how a practitioner should experience them.

### 5. Running a compliance assessment (the craft)

A compliance assessment is a structured comparison of *what is actually happening* against *what the framework requires*, producing findings with evidence. The method taught (and used in Lab 2 and PA-1):

1. **Fix the scope in one sentence.** "The Ameen application-scoring data flow, from application submission to score storage" — not "the SSA".
2. **Draw the data flow first.** Sources → processing → stores → recipients. Every arrow is a question: basis? classification? retention? Most findings live on arrows, not boxes.
3. **Walk the checklist against evidence, not intentions.** The answer to "is there a privacy notice?" is a link or a NO — "the team is aware" scores as absent.
4. **Write findings as: requirement → observed state → gap → severity → owner.** A finding without an owner is a complaint.
5. **Resist solutioning during assessment.** Fixes come after the full picture; premature fixing biases the assessment toward easy findings.

### 6. Common mistakes & operational considerations

1. **Consent-collecting reflex:** demanding consent where another basis is proper (statutory duty, contract), creating both friction and a false impression that withdrawal stops the processing.
2. **RoPA rot:** an inventory built once for a compliance push, never updated as pipelines change — detectable by comparing RoPA recipients against actual egress.
3. **Classification inflation:** rating everything Secret "to be safe", which paralyses legitimate sharing and pushes teams into shadow channels — the opposite of control.
4. **Notice–reality divergence:** the privacy notice describes the system as designed in 2023; the pipeline as built in 2026 does more. The notice is a contract with the public; drift is a finding.
5. **Treating anonymisation claims as exit doors:** labelling an extract "anonymised" without analysis (M4 tests these claims and most fail).
6. **Cross-border blindness:** a vendor's support engineers in another jurisdiction accessing production data *is* a transfer; SaaS telemetry can be a transfer. Map access, not just storage.

**Operational considerations.** Keep the checklist versioned and dated — assessments are snapshots. Schedule reassessment on triggers (new data source, new recipient, purpose change) rather than calendar-only. In government contexts, align evidence formats with what national audit and SDAIA supervision expect: dated documents, named owners, decision records.

## Case Study — Marketing Personalisation at a Gulf Retail Bank

**Scenario.** "Bank AlKhaleej" (fictional), headquartered in Riyadh, builds a personalisation engine that scores customers for product offers using transaction history, app behaviour, and — via a data-enrichment vendor — inferred lifestyle segments. The engine works; conversion doubles. Then a customer complaint escalates: a customer received a personal-finance offer that could only be explained by the bank knowing about his recent medical spending pattern. The complaint reaches SDAIA as a data-protection query and internal audit opens a file.

**Business context.** The bank must answer the regulator, decide whether the enrichment vendor relationship is lawful, and keep a revenue-critical system running. SAMA supervision adds a second regulator watching the same facts.

**Technical challenge.** Reconstruct: what data categories feed the engine (transaction categories that reveal health-adjacent patterns behave like sensitive data even if not labelled "health"); what the notice actually told customers; what basis covers enrichment; whether the vendor's "publicly sourced" segments were lawfully compiled at all.

**Constraints.** Marketing owns the vendor contract and resists change mid-campaign; the notice was last updated before the engine existed; the enrichment data cannot be verified for source legality; the regulator's questions have deadlines.

**Solution approach (facilitate).** (1) Freeze enrichment-derived features immediately — reversible, defensible, keeps the core engine running on first-party data. (2) Data-inventory sprint: rebuild the RoPA entry for the engine; categorise transaction-derived features by revealed sensitivity, not by column name. (3) Basis analysis: first-party personalisation may stand on legitimate interest with opt-out; health-adjacent inference cannot. (4) Vendor interrogation with M6's checklist: source legality, consent chain, contract terms — expect failure and plan exit. (5) Notice rewrite + a customer response that admits the inference issue plainly.

**Discussion questions.**
1. Column names said "merchant category"; the pattern revealed health. Where should sensitivity be assessed — schema or effect? What process implements the answer?
2. Was there any notice text that could have made the enrichment lawful? Or is this a basis problem no notice fixes?
3. Sequence the regulator response: what do you send first, and what do you *not* volunteer prematurely while investigation is ongoing?
4. What single checklist item, done at design time, would have prevented the entire incident? (P-01 basis-per-purpose, applied to the enrichment feed.)

# Module 3 — Bias, Fairness, and Impact Assessment

## Module Overview

**Purpose.** "The model is accurate" and "the model is fair" are different claims, measured differently, and a system can hold the first while badly failing the second. This module gives participants the working machinery of fairness analysis: where bias enters the lifecycle, the core group-fairness metrics and their inescapable trade-offs, the disparate-impact test, and the craft of running and writing a bias audit that stands up to committee scrutiny. It is the evidence engine behind the SDAIA Fairness principle.

**Business relevance.** Fairness failures are the most publicly explosive AI failures: they turn a technical system into a front-page equity story overnight (Module 1's Murshid case is the pattern). For government AI — eligibility, licensing, enforcement — disparate impact across regions, genders, or nationalities is a political and legal event, not a model metric. Organisations that audit before deployment control the narrative and the remediation cost; organisations audited *by others* control neither. The bias audit is also fast becoming a procurement deliverable: buyers of AI systems increasingly demand subgroup performance evidence before contract award.

**Industry use cases.**
- An HR platform screening thousands of applications must demonstrate that its shortlisting rates satisfy the four-fifths rule across gender and that error rates are balanced before a large employer will deploy it.
- A bank's credit model is audited for disparate impact across regions and age bands as part of model-risk review; the audit's remediation (removing a proxy feature) costs 0.3 AUC points and is approved as a documented trade-off.
- A hospital readmission-risk model is found to under-predict risk for patients from remote regions (sparser historical data); clinical oversight rules are tightened for that subgroup while data collection improves.

**Expected competencies.** Participants can identify bias sources by lifecycle phase, choose and justify fairness metrics for a given decision context, compute approval-rate and error-rate disparities from a scored dataset, apply the 80% rule, reason about metric trade-offs, propose remediations at the correct point in the pipeline, and write a bias-audit report with findings and recommendations.

## Technical Content

### 1. Where bias enters: a lifecycle taxonomy

Bias is not one thing; it is a family of mechanisms, each entering at a specific phase — which matters because **remediation must target the mechanism, not the symptom**:

| Bias type | Enters at | Mechanism | Ameen example |
|---|---|---|---|
| **Historical** | Before the data | Past decisions encoded past inequities; the label *is* the bias | Historical caseworker decisions under-approved remote-region applicants; the model learns it as ground truth |
| **Representation** | Prepare data | Groups under-sampled → model underfits them | Few training examples from newly added application channels |
| **Measurement** | Prepare data | Features/labels measured with different fidelity across groups | Income verified via employer records for salaried applicants, self-reported for informal workers |
| **Aggregation** | Build | One model for populations with different feature–outcome relationships | Household-size effects differ across regions; a single global coefficient misfits both |
| **Evaluation** | Validate | Benchmarks unrepresentative; only global metrics checked | Validation on last year's applicants after eligibility rules changed |
| **Deployment** | Operate | Usage context shifts; humans interact with outputs unevenly | Caseworkers override low scores for familiar districts more often than remote ones |

**Proxy features** deserve their own emphasis: removing the protected attribute does not remove the bias when other features encode it (district encodes income and origin; employment sector encodes gender in segmented labour markets). "We don't use gender" is the beginning of the analysis, never the conclusion — and removing the protected attribute also removes your ability to *measure* disparity, which is why governance practice retains protected attributes for audit while excluding them from features.

### 2. Group fairness metrics from confusion matrices

All core group metrics fall out of per-group confusion matrices. Fix notation: for each group, from predicted-positive/negative × actually-positive/negative counts we get the **selection rate** (share predicted positive), **TPR** (true positive rate — of the truly eligible, how many the model approves), and **FPR** (false positive rate — of the truly ineligible, how many it approves).

- **Demographic parity:** selection rates equal across groups. Blind to qualification differences; right when the "positive" is an opportunity to be distributed (outreach, screening-in), misleading when base rates legitimately differ.
- **Disparate-impact ratio (DIR):** selection rate of the disadvantaged group ÷ selection rate of the advantaged group. The **four-fifths (80%) rule** — DIR below 0.8 signals adverse impact — originates in US employment practice but functions worldwide, including in Saudi practice, as a useful *screening threshold*: a tripwire that triggers investigation, not a verdict.
- **Equal opportunity:** TPR equal across groups — among the *truly eligible*, each group has the same chance of approval. Usually the primary fairness claim for benefit eligibility: the harm that matters most is the eligible family wrongly rejected.
- **Equalized odds:** TPR *and* FPR equal across groups — stricter, and often the committee's expectation once stated in plain language ("errors of both kinds shouldn't concentrate in one group").
- **Calibration within groups:** among applicants scored 0.8, about 80% should be truly eligible — in every group. Decision-makers consume scores as probabilities; group-dependent meaning misleads them silently.

**The impossibility result, taught in one breath:** when base rates differ between groups, a (non-trivial) model cannot simultaneously satisfy calibration and equalized odds — this is a mathematical fact, not an engineering shortfall. Consequence: fairness-metric choice is a *policy judgement* about which harms matter most, made explicitly, recorded in the governance file, and owned by the accountable owner — exactly the M1 discipline. The practitioner's job is to compute all of them, explain the tension, and force the explicit choice.

**Individual vs group fairness:** group metrics can all pass while similar individuals are treated differently (and vice versa). Consistency checks — near-identical applications scored very differently — belong in the audit's exploratory section.

### 3. The bias-audit procedure

The audit method used in Lab 3 and expected in the capstone:

1. **Scope & stakes.** System, decision, affected groups, and the harm direction that matters (for Ameen: false rejections of eligible families).
2. **Define groups & justify.** Protected/affected attributes chosen with policy input (gender of head-of-household, region, nationality, disability) — including intersections where volume permits (region × gender); small intersections get flagged, not silently dropped.
3. **Choose primary metric(s) before computing.** Pre-registration discipline: deciding the metric after seeing results invites motivated selection.
4. **Compute:** per-group selection rates, DIR against the most-selected group, TPR/FPR gaps, calibration by score band. Minimum cell sizes noted; confidence intervals or at least n's on everything.
5. **Diagnose:** map each disparity to a lifecycle bias source (Section 1's taxonomy) using targeted checks — label re-review samples for historical bias, per-group feature missingness for measurement bias, per-group learning curves for representation bias.
6. **Recommend:** remediations matched to sources (Section 4), each with expected effect, cost, and residual risk.
7. **Report:** findings with evidence, written for a mixed audience (Worked Example 2's structure).

### 4. Remediation: match the fix to the mechanism

| Intervention point | Techniques | When right | Cautions |
|---|---|---|---|
| **Data** | Relabel with explicit criteria; collect/oversample under-represented groups; fix measurement asymmetries; remove/repair proxy features | Historical, representation, measurement bias | Slowest but only fix that addresses cause; relabeling needs governance of the *new* criteria |
| **Training** | Reweighing samples; fairness-constrained optimisation | Residual disparity after data fixes | Constraint choice = metric choice = policy judgement; document it |
| **Post-processing / thresholds** | Group-specific thresholds; score recalibration per group | Fast mitigation while causes are fixed | Explicitly treats groups differently — needs legal/policy sign-off and transparency; treat as bridge, not destination |
| **Process** | Human review bands; override monitoring; appeal channels; tightened oversight for affected subgroups | Deployment bias; interim protection | Costs caseworker capacity; override behaviour itself needs monitoring (deployment bias again) |

**The governance framing:** remediation decisions trade fairness, accuracy, cost, and speed. The audit's job is to make the trade-offs explicit and priced; the accountable owner's job is to choose and sign. An audit that ends in "the team will look into it" has failed its purpose.

### 5. Impact assessment beyond metrics

Metrics measure the model; impact assessment considers the *system in the world*: Who is affected who never appears in the data (non-applicants deterred by the process)? Does the appeal channel function equally across groups (language, digital access, literacy)? What does an error cost a family in time and dignity, not just money? A short structured section in the audit — affected-stakeholder table with harms, severity, likelihood, and existing safeguards — connects fairness analysis to the M1 benefit statement and the M6 risk register. For high-risk government systems this section is what committee members actually read first.

### 6. Common mistakes & production considerations

1. **Fairness through blindness:** deleting protected attributes and declaring victory — proxies remain, and measurement becomes impossible.
2. **Metric shopping:** computing five metrics, reporting the one that passes.
3. **Ignoring base rates:** declaring bias from selection-rate gaps alone when qualification rates genuinely differ — or, conversely, using base-rate differences to excuse gaps *among the qualified* (the equal-opportunity gap survives that excuse).
4. **Small-cell theatre:** headline claims from subgroups of 40 people; report n's, aggregate responsibly, flag what cannot be concluded.
5. **One-time audits:** fairness at launch drifts like accuracy drifts; live-decision re-measurement on a schedule (quarterly for Ameen) with tolerance thresholds wired to the M6 risk register.
6. **Auditing the model, ignoring the humans:** override patterns can reintroduce every disparity the model fixed; deployment-phase audits must include the human layer.
7. **Publishing raw audit tables without narrative:** numbers without harm framing get misread in both directions; the report structure exists for a reason.

**Production considerations.** Keep an immutable snapshot of the scored dataset behind every audit (reproducibility = credibility); version audit code with the model version audited; require an independent reviewer who did not build the model (M1's validation sign-off pattern); pre-agree publication policy — internal, committee, or public summary — before results exist, so the decision is not contaminated by them.

## Worked Examples

### Worked Example 1 — Bias-audit walkthrough on Ameen scores (complete arithmetic)

Scored validation extract: 10,000 applications, ground-truth eligibility from a caseworker re-review panel. Groups: head-of-household gender (A = male, N=6,000; B = female, N=4,000). Threshold: score ≥ 0.60 → recommend approve.

**Per-group confusion matrices:**

| | Group A (N=6,000) | Group B (N=4,000) |
|---|---|---|
| Truly eligible | 3,000 | 2,000 |
| Recommended approve | 3,720 | 1,920 |
| True positives (TP) | 2,730 | 1,560 |
| False positives (FP) | 990 | 360 |
| False negatives (FN) | 270 | 440 |
| True negatives (TN) | 2,010 | 1,640 |

**Metric computation:**

| Metric | Group A | Group B | Gap / ratio | Reading |
|---|---|---|---|---|
| Selection rate | 3,720/6,000 = **0.62** | 1,920/4,000 = **0.48** | DIR = 0.48/0.62 = **0.774** | Below 0.8 → four-fifths tripwire fires |
| TPR (equal opportunity) | 2,730/3,000 = **0.91** | 1,560/2,000 = **0.78** | gap = **0.13** | Eligible women-headed households are rejected at 22% vs 9% — the primary harm |
| FPR | 990/3,000 = **0.33** | 360/2,000 = **0.18** | gap = **0.15** | Errors in the *favourable* direction also concentrate in group A |
| Precision | 2,730/3,720 = **0.73** | 1,560/1,920 = **0.81** | −0.08 | A approved-recommendation means more in group B — calibration asymmetry signal |
| Accuracy | 0.79 | 0.80 | ≈ 0 | **Global accuracy is identical — and hides everything above** |

**Diagnosis (from the seeded course data):** feature-missingness analysis shows income verification succeeds for 88% of group A but 61% of group B (measurement bias — informal-sector employment concentrates in group B's applications); label re-review of 300 stratified cases shows historical under-approval of group B at equal documented need (historical bias in labels).

**Recommendation set (as priced options for the owner):** (1) fix verification asymmetry via the income-service integration for informal workers — addresses cause, ~2 quarters; (2) relabel a 5,000-case stratified sample with explicit criteria and retrain — addresses label bias, ~1 quarter; (3) interim process control now: mandatory caseworker review of all group-B rejections in score band 0.45–0.60 (the band where the TPR gap concentrates); (4) re-audit quarterly with tolerance TPR-gap ≤ 0.05, DIR ≥ 0.85.

**Teaching point:** the identical accuracy row is the slide to leave on screen. Every governance failure in this module hides behind a healthy global metric.

### Worked Example 3 — Toolkit snippet (the audit computation, for cohorts using the notebook)

```python
# bias_audit/compute_metrics.py — Lab 3 notebook core (pre-installed environment)
# Works on the synthetic ameen_scored.csv; spreadsheet version provided too.
import pandas as pd
from fairlearn.metrics import MetricFrame, selection_rate, true_positive_rate, false_positive_rate

df = pd.read_csv("data/ameen_scored.csv")        # synthetic; no real persons
df["approved"] = (df["score"] >= 0.60).astype(int)

mf = MetricFrame(
    metrics={
        "selection_rate": selection_rate,
        "tpr": true_positive_rate,               # equal-opportunity view
        "fpr": false_positive_rate,
        "n": lambda y_true, y_pred: len(y_true),
    },
    y_true=df["eligible_truth"],
    y_pred=df["approved"],
    sensitive_features=df["hoh_gender"],         # kept for AUDIT, never a model feature
)
print(mf.by_group.round(3))

rates = mf.by_group["selection_rate"]
print("Disparate-impact ratio:", round(rates.min() / rates.max(), 3))
# Expected on course data: DIR ≈ 0.774, TPR gap ≈ 0.13 — matches Worked Example 1
```

## Case Study — Hiring-Screening AI at a National Employer

**Scenario.** "Tawteen Talent" (fictional), a Saudi conglomerate hiring at scale, deploys a CV-screening model trained on ten years of its own hiring decisions to shortlist for engineering and operations roles. A year in, the recruitment analytics team notices women's shortlisting rate for technical roles is 31% versus 52% for men with similar qualification profiles; separately, applicants who list only non-Saudi universities show a shortlisting penalty even at equal experience.

**Business context.** The employer is a flagship participant in national workforce-development programmes: women's participation targets are strategic commitments with board visibility. HR asks whether to switch the model off; the talent-acquisition lead notes screening volume (400k applications/year) makes manual triage impossible.

**Technical challenge.** The training labels are the company's own historical shortlisting decisions — the model learned the past. CV text features encode gender through activity words, dates of study, and institution names even with the name/gender fields removed. The qualification ground truth needed for a proper equal-opportunity analysis does not exist and must be constructed via structured re-review.

**Constraints.** Hiring cannot pause; the audit must not become a discovery risk (privileged handling of interim results); the fix must survive next year's hiring-pattern changes; works councils/HR policy require any differential process treatment to be documented and approved.

**Solution approach (facilitate).** (1) Immediate: human review of all auto-rejected female-candidate CVs for technical roles (interim process control). (2) Construct ground truth: blind structured re-review of a 2,000-CV stratified sample by trained assessors scoring against published criteria. (3) Audit against the constructed truth: selection rates, TPR gaps, proxy analysis on text features. (4) Structural fix: retrain on re-reviewed labels + criteria-based features (skills, certifications) instead of raw CV text; validate that institution-name signal is gone. (5) Governance: quarterly re-audit wired to HR's diversity reporting; model card documents the historical-label decision (M5 tie-in).

**Discussion questions.**
1. The model reproduced the firm's own history. Whose bias is the finding — the model's or the organisation's — and what does the answer change about remediation ownership?
2. Constructing ground truth by re-review imports the re-reviewers' judgement. How do you govern *that*? (Criteria publication, inter-rater agreement stats, panel diversity.)
3. Volume makes "switch it off" unrealistic. Construct the interim risk position you would sign.
4. Which parts of this audit belong in the public diversity report, and which in privileged review? Defend the line.

## Technical Content

### 1. Data minimisation as an engineering discipline

Minimisation is the most underrated privacy control because it is the only one with zero residual risk: data you never collected cannot leak, cannot be misused, and never appears in a breach notification. Operationally it is three recurring reviews:

- **Collection review — "necessity per feature":** for every field, the team must state the purpose it serves and what breaks without it. The honest test: *would we pay SAR 10,000/year to keep this field?* — because in risk terms, you do. AUC-lift alone is not necessity; a 0.2% lift from a sensitive field fails proportionality.
- **Access review — "who sees what grain":** the data scientist needs distributions, not names; the caseworker needs one applicant, not the register; the dashboard needs aggregates. Grain-matching access to role is minimisation applied to people.
- **Retention review — "necessity over time":** every dataset gets a review date and a default-delete posture. "Keep for future models" is a purpose without a limit and fails the test (Ameen's GAP-10).

For AI specifically, minimisation lands at four points: feature selection (fewest personal attributes that meet the documented performance bar), training extracts (sampled and stripped, not full-register copies), logging (M6 of the software-practice module's lesson recurs — no raw personal attributes in logs), and model outputs (return the decision and reasons, not input echoes).

### 2. Pseudonymisation vs anonymisation: the line that decides everything

- **Pseudonymisation** replaces direct identifiers (name, national ID) with tokens, keeping a re-linking key or a deterministic mapping. It is a valuable *security* measure — and the data **remains personal data** under PDPL, with every obligation intact, because identification is still reasonably possible (by the key holder or by anyone who links quasi-identifiers).
- **Anonymisation** severs identifiability such that the individual is no longer identifiable, directly or indirectly, by any means reasonably likely to be used. Genuinely anonymous data exits data-protection obligations — which is exactly why the claim must be *demonstrated*, and why every governance regime treats the word "anonymised" on a file name as a claim awaiting evidence, not a property.

The practical consequence taught as a rule: **a dataset is treated at its source classification until an anonymisation analysis says otherwise** (Module 2's inheritance rule). The analysis has a name and a method — re-identification risk assessment — and it is the next section.

### 3. Re-identification risk and k-anonymity

Direct identifiers are easy; **quasi-identifiers** are the problem: attribute combinations that single people out. The famous pattern — date of birth + gender + postal area identifying most of a population — generalises: in the Ameen extract, {age, district, nationality, household size} nearly fingerprints many applicants even with IDs removed.

**k-anonymity:** every record is indistinguishable from at least k−1 others on the quasi-identifier set. Computed by grouping on the quasi-identifiers and finding the minimum group size. Raised by:

- **Generalisation:** age 34 → band 30–39; district → region; exact income → income band.
- **Suppression:** delete records in groups that stay small after reasonable generalisation (rare nationality × remote district cells), or blank the offending cells.

**k is necessary, not sufficient.** Two refinements participants must know by attack, not by formula:

- **Homogeneity attack → l-diversity:** if all k records in a group share the sensitive value (all five applicants in the cell have the same disability status), the attacker learns it without singling anyone out. l-diversity demands diversity of sensitive values within each group.
- **Background-knowledge and composition attacks:** overlapping releases (this year's and last year's extracts) can be differenced; auxiliary public data (social media, leaked datasets) links what each release alone would not. This is why anonymisation decisions consider the *release environment*, not the file in isolation — and why "publish once, assess every time" is the rule for recurring open-data releases.

**The utility ledger:** every generalisation costs analytical resolution. A privacy decision without a utility statement is half a decision; Lab 4 requires recording both (k achieved *and* what analyses the generalisation forecloses).

### 4. The modern toolbox at decision depth: differential privacy and synthetic data

Participants need working fluency, not implementation skill:

- **Differential privacy (DP)** gives a mathematical guarantee about *queries/outputs*, not datasets: calibrated noise ensures any single individual's presence changes results by at most a bounded amount (the privacy budget ε; smaller = stronger). Right tool for **published statistics and aggregate releases** (national statistics offices use it for census products) and increasingly for training (DP-SGD) where membership inference is a real threat. Costs: accuracy loss concentrated on small subgroups — which collides directly with M3's need for small-group fairness measurement; naming that collision is the mark of a governance-literate practitioner. Budget management (ε accumulates across queries) is an operational discipline, not a one-time setting.
- **Synthetic data** — generated records mimicking the statistical structure of real data — is useful for development environments, demos, and sharing *shape* without *people* (this course's own datasets are the demonstration). It is **not automatically anonymous**: a generator overfitted to its training data can memorise and emit near-copies of real records. A synthetic release needs the same re-identification scrutiny, plus generator-specific checks (nearest-neighbour distance to real records, membership-inference testing). "It's synthetic" is a starting claim with the same standing as "it's anonymised."
- **Choosing:** aggregates for the public → DP; record-level research release → generalisation/suppression to k with l-diversity checks + contractual controls; dev/test environments → synthetic; and often the best answer is **don't release records at all** — bring the analysis to the data (secure research environments, federated queries) rather than the data to the analyst.

### 5. The privacy impact assessment (DPIA)

The DPIA is where Module 2's legal trigger ("high-risk processing must be assessed") meets this module's techniques. Structure taught (and used in Lab 4):

1. **Description:** processing, purposes, data categories, flows (imported from the RoPA — artefacts compose).
2. **Necessity & proportionality:** the minimisation review's output; alternatives considered ("could aggregates suffice?").
3. **Risk identification:** to *data subjects*, not to the organisation — unauthorised access, re-identification, function creep, discriminatory effect (cross-reference the M3 audit), chilling effects.
4. **Risk evaluation:** likelihood × severity, before controls.
5. **Mitigations:** the techniques of this module, mapped risk-by-risk, with residual risk stated honestly.
6. **Consultation & sign-off:** DPO consulted; accountable owner accepts residual risk in writing; review triggers defined.

The DPIA's quality marker is the honesty of its residual-risk statement. "All risks mitigated" is the signature of an assessment written to be filed, not used.

### 6. Common mistakes & production considerations

1. **The redaction reflex:** removing names/IDs and calling it anonymised — quasi-identifiers untouched. The single most common privacy failure in analytics.
2. **Anonymising the extract, keeping the pipeline:** the "anonymised" dataset is regenerated weekly by a job that reads the identified source — the *system* is identified even if one file is not; assess the pipeline.
3. **Hash-and-hope pseudonymisation:** hashing national IDs without salt/pepper — enumerable input spaces make unsalted hashes reversible by brute force; and even done well, it is still pseudonymisation, not anonymisation.
4. **Utility silence:** shipping a k=10 dataset nobody can use, discovering it three months later; the utility ledger exists to force the conversation at decision time.
5. **One-release thinking:** each quarterly extract safe alone, the sequence linkable — composition is assessed at the *programme* level.
6. **DP theatre:** quoting "we use differential privacy" with ε=15 (weak) or without budget tracking — the guarantee is the parameters, not the phrase.
7. **Synthetic-data laundering:** using synthetic generation to dodge review entirely; the generator's training data was real and the output needs testing.

**Production considerations.** Version anonymisation configurations like code (the generalisation hierarchy is a policy artefact); log releases in a register (who received what, under which analysis, when — composition tracking depends on it); pre-agree the re-identification testing protocol with the receiving party in sharing agreements; and keep the identified source's controls unchanged — anonymisation of outputs never weakens obligations on inputs.

### Worked Example 2 — k-anonymity walkthrough on the SSA research extract

Quasi-identifier set: {age, district, nationality, household size}. Extract: 8,000 records.

**Before generalisation** (grouping on raw QIs): minimum group size **k = 1** — 214 records are unique; the "de-identified" extract is a re-identification kit.

Sample of exposed records:

| Age | District | Nationality | HH size | Group size |
|---|---|---|---|---|
| 67 | Al-Aziziyah | (rare nationality) | 9 | **1** |
| 23 | (remote district) | Saudi | 11 | **1** |
| 41 | Al-Rawdah | (rare nationality) | 2 | **2** |

**Generalisation pass 1** — age → 10-year bands, district → governorate: k rises to **3** (38 groups below 5).
**Generalisation pass 2** — nationality → {Saudi, GCC, Other}, household size → {1–3, 4–6, 7+}: k = **5** for 7,912 records; **88 records** in stubborn small groups → **suppressed**.

**Result:** k ≥ 5 on 98.9% of records, 1.1% suppressed. **Utility ledger:** governorate-level analysis preserved; district-level programme targeting foreclosed (flagged to the research team — they accepted); age effects now resolvable only to decade.

**l-diversity check:** 3 of the k≥5 groups are homogeneous on disability status → within-group learning of the sensitive value. Fix: merge those groups one level up on age band. Final release: k ≥ 5, sensitive-attribute diversity verified, release logged in the register.

## Case Study — Hospital Research Extract at a Regional Health Cluster

**Scenario.** A health cluster in the Eastern Province prepares a research extract for a university partnership on diabetes outcomes: 60,000 patient episodes, "de-identified" by removing names and medical record numbers. A pre-release review (prompted by a new research-office hire) finds the extract contains admission dates, exact ages, district, nationality, occupation, and rare-diagnosis codes. A test linkage against publicly available information re-identifies several individuals in under an hour — including a public figure whose hospitalisation was reported in the press.

**Business context.** The partnership is signed, the university team is waiting, and the cluster's leadership wants research output for its academic-status ambitions. A breach here would be a sensitive-data incident under PDPL with mandatory notification analysis — health data is squarely in the sensitive category.

**Technical challenge.** Longitudinal episodes make anonymisation genuinely hard: the *sequence* of admissions is itself a fingerprint; rare diagnoses defeat generalisation; dates enable linkage to press reports and social media. Aggregation destroys the survival-analysis utility the researchers need.

**Constraints.** The research questions genuinely need episode-level data; the university's environment is outside the cluster's control; the data-sharing agreement predates the review and specifies "de-identified data" without defining it; publication pressure is real and the researchers are not adversaries — but their laptops travel.

**Solution approach (facilitate).** (1) Stop the release; the agreement's undefined "de-identified" is the cluster's problem, not its protection. (2) Reframe the sharing model: instead of exporting records, stand up a **secure research environment** — analysts come to the data, no record-level egress, output checked against small-cell rules before leaving. (3) Where export is unavoidable, apply the full discipline: date shifting (consistent per patient), age/geography generalisation, rare-diagnosis handling (suppress or coarsen), sequence-length capping — then measure k on the episode-level QI set and test-linkage again. (4) Rewrite the agreement: define anonymisation by *test outcome*, not adjective; specify the re-identification protocol, output rules, and incident duties. (5) Notification analysis for the test re-identifications: reviewed internally under privilege — the review found the risk before an external party did, and the record of that finding and fix is the defensible position.

**Discussion questions.**
1. The extract was "de-identified" under any casual reading. Write the one-sentence contractual definition of anonymisation that would have prevented this.
2. Secure research environments cost more and slow research. Construct the proportionality argument for when export is acceptable anyway.
3. The public figure's episode was re-identified via press coverage — background knowledge no generalisation of the dataset controls. What does this imply about anonymisation of *famous* individuals' data, and who else shares that exposure profile?
4. Health data is sensitive under PDPL. Walk the breach-analysis: was there a notifiable breach here, and what turns on the answer?

## Technical Content

### 1. Transparency has audiences: one system, four documents

The most common transparency failure is writing one document for four audiences. Teach the audience matrix first, then the artefacts:

| Audience | Question they ask | Artefact | Register |
|---|---|---|---|
| Affected person | "Why was *my* application rejected, and what can I do?" | Decision notice + appeal path | Plain language (Arabic for citizens — flag the language note) |
| Operator (caseworker) | "How should I use — and when should I distrust — this score?" | Operating guide + model card §use | Practical, procedural |
| Governance/audit | "Was this system validated, for whom does it fail, who owns it?" | Model card + datasheet + audit trail | Structured, evidenced |
| Technical successor | "How do I maintain, retrain, or retire this?" | Model card + pipeline docs + decision log | Technical |

The model card is the hub document; notices and guides are derived views. Getting this dependency right means one source of truth and no divergence between what the committee reads and what the citizen is told.

### 2. Model cards: structure and the honesty disciplines

The model card (popularised by Mitchell et al., 2019, and now industry-standard from major labs to procurement checklists) is a short structured document answering: what is this model, what is it for, what is it *not* for, on what was it trained and evaluated, how does it perform — *for whom* — and what are its limits. The course structure (used in Lab 5 and Worked Example 1):

1. **Model details** — name, version, type, owner, dates, contact.
2. **Intended use** — the decision it supports, the users, the operating context.
3. **Out-of-scope uses** — explicitly named misuses (the section that prevents M1's scope creep; make it *specific*: "not validated for housing-support prioritisation").
4. **Training & evaluation data** — sources, period, size, known skews (links to the datasheet).
5. **Performance** — headline metrics *and the subgroup table from the M3 audit*; a card without subgroup results is pre-2020 practice.
6. **Fairness & limitations** — known disparities, small-cell caveats, conditions of degraded performance.
7. **Ethical considerations & mitigations** — the trade-offs made and controls attached (imported from the governance file).
8. **Maintenance** — retraining cadence, monitoring thresholds, review triggers, decommissioning criteria.
9. **Version history** — what changed and why, including threshold changes (M3's simulation lesson).

**The honesty disciplines:** (a) every claim cites its evidence artefact — the card is an index, not an essay; (b) the limitations section is written *by the builders, reviewed by a non-builder* — self-reported limitations are systematically flattering; (c) the card versions with the model — a card describing v2 while v3 serves is worse than no card, because it manufactures false confidence.

**Datasheets for datasets** (Gebru et al.) apply the same discipline upstream: motivation, composition, collection process, preprocessing, known skews, recommended and discouraged uses. For Ameen, the datasheet is where the verification asymmetry (M3's diagnosis) gets permanently recorded so no future team rediscovers it the hard way.

### 3. Explainability: methods, honestly positioned

Participants need decision-maker fluency in the explanation toolbox, including its failure modes:

- **Global explanations** — what drives the model overall: feature importance, partial-dependence summaries. Right for governance review and the model card's performance narrative. Failure mode: importance rankings differ across methods and can be unstable under correlated features; treat as descriptive, not causal.
- **Local explanations** — why *this* prediction: SHAP-style attributions, counterfactual statements ("had verified income been X, the recommendation would differ"). Counterfactuals are usually the most honest citizen-facing form because they state what would change the outcome — which is what the person actually needs. Failure modes: attributions on proxy features "explain" with the proxy (an explanation of a biased model is a well-explained biased decision — explanation is not exculpation); counterfactuals must be *actionable* (do not tell a family to change household size).
- **Inherently interpretable models** — scorecards, small rule sets, monotonic GBMs: for high-stakes decisions the governance question "could an interpretable model do this job?" must be asked and its answer recorded *before* accepting black-box + post-hoc explanation. Post-hoc explanation of an unnecessary black box is complexity chosen, then apologised for.
- **The explanation ≠ transparency point:** a perfect SHAP plot does not tell an applicant that AI was involved, what data was used, or how to appeal — those are notice obligations (Section 4), not explanation methods. Systems need both.

### 4. Decision notices: transparency at the person

The notice is where the whole course faces a human being. Design rules taught through the Ameen adverse-decision notice (Worked Example 2):

1. **Disclose the mechanism:** the person is told that an automated scoring system contributed to the decision — plainly, not in clause 14(b).
2. **Give reasons that are true and useful:** the top decision factors, stated in the person's terms ("reported income above the programme threshold"), not model jargon ("feature x7 SHAP −0.34").
3. **State the action path:** what to correct, how to appeal, to whom, by when — an appeal path that requires digital literacy the affected population lacks fails M3's impact-assessment test.
4. **Do not leak:** reasons must not expose other people's data, fraud-detection logic that enables gaming (a real tension — resolve it explicitly and record the resolution), or sensitive inferences.
5. **Language and access:** citizen-facing notices in Arabic (bilingual best practice), reading-level checked, available through non-digital channels for the populations that need them.

### 5. Audit trails: reconstructability as a design requirement

The governance test of an audit trail is one question: **can we reconstruct this specific decision, as it was made, with what was known then?** Working backwards from that test gives the event schema:

- **Decision event:** timestamp, application ID (pseudonymous key), input snapshot *reference* (not inline PII — M4/M6 log discipline), feature-pipeline version, model version, score, threshold in force, recommendation.
- **Human-action event:** caseworker ID, action (accept/override), recorded override reason (from a structured reason list plus free text policy), timestamp.
- **Change events:** model deployments, threshold changes (with approver), pipeline changes, policy changes — the M3 threshold simulation showed why these are fairness events; here they become logged events.
- **Access events:** who viewed which case — audit trails are themselves personal-data processing; retention, access control, and PDPL obligations apply to them too (the recursive point participants forget).

**Integrity properties:** append-only storage, clock discipline, retention aligned with the appeals/audit window, and separation of duties (the team operating the model does not administer the trail). The trail's *analytics* close the loop: override-rate by caseworker and by group is the deployment-bias monitor M3 called for.

### 6. Common mistakes & operational considerations

1. **The aspirational model card:** describing the system the team *meant* to build; every unevidenced claim is a future audit finding.
2. **Card rot:** v3 serving, v2 card published — versioning the card with the model is a release-gate rule, not a good intention.
3. **Explanation theatre:** shipping SHAP plots to citizens who need "what can I do?" — audience mismatch (Section 1's matrix exists for this).
4. **Gaming-proof secrecy as a blanket excuse:** refusing all reason-giving because "people will game it." Some factors are game-proof (verified income), some are not (free-text patterns); the analysis must be factor-by-factor and recorded.
5. **Logs that log everything except the decision:** infrastructure logs galore, but no threshold history, no override reasons — reconstruct the server, not the decision.
6. **Free-text-only override reasons:** unanalysable; the structured reason list is what turns overrides into monitorable data.
7. **PII-rich audit trails:** inlining full applications into logs recreates M4's problem in the compliance layer itself.

**Operational considerations.** Make card completion a **release gate** (no card, no deploy — enforced in the M6 workflow); assign each card an owner and a review date; store cards in the same registry as models so drift is structurally impossible; and treat notice text changes as governed changes (legal + DPO review) with their own version history.

## Case Study — Credit-Decision Explanations at a Saudi Retail Bank

**Scenario.** "Bank AlJisr" (fictional) automates consumer-credit decisions with a gradient-boosted model. Declined customers receive a generic SMS ("application declined per bank policy"). Complaints to the bank's consumer-protection function rise; a sample review finds three systemic problems: customers cannot learn what to fix, front-line staff improvise explanations (sometimes wrong, occasionally leaking model logic), and two declined customers were given *different* explanations for near-identical profiles by different branches.

**Business context.** SAMA-supervised consumer-protection expectations require clear communication of decline reasons; the bank also wants fewer repeat applications with unchanged profiles (cost) and fewer escalations (reputation). The model itself audits acceptably on fairness — this is a pure transparency failure on a compliant model.

**Technical challenge.** Generate decline reasons that are: derived from the actual model factors (not a parallel fiction), stable across channels, actionable, gaming-analysed, and deliverable in Arabic at scale through SMS, app, and branch scripts — with an audit trail proving which reasons each customer was given.

**Constraints.** Reason codes must survive model retraining (codes map to factor *families*, not raw features); branch staff need scripts, not SHAP dashboards; legal insists on identical wording across channels; the fraud-adjacent factors are excluded from reasons and that exclusion must be a signed decision.

**Solution approach (facilitate).** (1) Build the reason-code layer: top model factors mapped to ~12 stable, plain-language reason families, reviewed by legal + consumer protection. (2) Every decision logs its reason codes (audit-trail event) — the "different explanations" problem dies by design. (3) Channel views generated from one source: SMS (short), app (full + what-to-do), branch script (verbatim + FAQ). (4) Gaming analysis per factor family, documented, signed; excluded families listed in the internal card. (5) Measure the fix: repeat-application-with-no-change rate and explanation-related complaints as the KPIs; report both to the consumer-protection committee quarterly.

**Discussion questions.**
1. The reason-code layer is a translation between model truth and human language. What governs drift between them across retrains? (Card version history + a mapping test in the release gate.)
2. Two customers, near-identical profiles, different outcomes — explanation consistency exposed it. Is that a transparency bug or a fairness bug? Trace the M3 connection.
3. Write the branch-staff one-paragraph script for the "income could not be verified" family.
4. What does the audit trail need to prove *which explanation each customer received*, and why might that matter in a dispute two years later?

# Module 6 — AI Risk Management and Compliance Workflows

## Module Overview

**Purpose.** Everything the course has built — principles, checklists, audits, privacy assessments, documentation — becomes durable only when wired into an operating system: a risk register that stays current, governance gates embedded in the delivery workflow, monitoring that closes the loop from production back to the register, and a disciplined method for the hardest modern case — third-party AI services, where you govern what you did not build and cannot fully inspect. This module supplies that operating system and completes the Ameen Governance File.

**Business relevance.** The centre of gravity of AI risk has moved to procurement: most organisations now consume more AI than they build — LLM APIs, embedded vendor models, "AI-powered" SaaS features that appear in products overnight. An organisation that can only govern in-house models governs a shrinking fraction of its exposure. Meanwhile, regulators and auditors evaluate *systems of control*, not heroic individual documents: the question is never "do you have a bias audit?" but "what makes a bias audit happen every time, and what happens when it fails?" Compliance workflows are the answer to that question, and they are what distinguish an AI governance *program* from an AI governance *binder*.

**Industry use cases.**
- A ministry procuring a citizen-service chatbot built on a third-party LLM must assess data residency, training-data reuse rights, sub-processors, and exit terms before award — and reject the vendor whose standard contract sends prompt logs abroad for "service improvement".
- A bank embeds AI-risk gates into its existing model-risk workflow: no model reaches production without a completed card, audit, and DPIA — enforced by the deployment platform, not by memory (the same gate philosophy as CI/CD quality gates, applied to governance).
- An insurer's risk register review catches a silent vendor model update that shifted claim-triage behaviour — because the third-party monitoring control required the vendor to notify version changes, and the register review checked.

**Expected competencies.** Participants can build and score an AI risk register; design governance gates into a delivery workflow with a RACI that names accountable roles; run a third-party AI assessment across data, model, operational, and contractual dimensions; specify contract clauses that make vendor assurances enforceable; and design the monitoring loop that keeps the whole system live.

## Technical Content

### 1. The AI risk register: structure and scoring

The register is the memory of the governance program. Structure per entry: **ID · risk statement · lifecycle phase · category (fairness / privacy / reliability / transparency / third-party / security) · likelihood (1–5) · impact (1–5) · inherent score (L×I) · existing controls · residual score · owner · review date · status.**

Craft rules that separate working registers from theatre:

- **Risk statements name a mechanism and a harm:** "verification asymmetry causes higher false rejections for informal-sector applicants" — not "model may be biased." A register row you cannot design a control against is a category, not a risk.
- **Impact is scored on affected people first, organisation second.** A risk matrix that only scores reputational damage inverts the SDAIA accountability lens; score both, decide on the max.
- **Residual risk is a claim about control effectiveness** — it requires the control to be *operating*, not designed. A quarterly fairness re-measurement that has never run provides zero residual reduction; registers mark controls as designed/implemented/verified.
- **The register is fed by everything else:** M1's gap register seeds it; M3's audit findings and M4's DPIA risks flow in with their artefact references. The Lab 6 exercise is largely a *consolidation* — the course has been building the register all along, which is the lesson.

### 2. Governance gates in the delivery workflow

Controls that depend on memory fail; controls embedded in the workflow fire every time. The gate pattern taught (deliberately parallel to CI/CD quality gates, which many practitioners already trust):

| Gate | When | Cannot pass without | Who signs |
|---|---|---|---|
| **G0 — Intake** | Idea approved for exploration | Risk-tier decision record (M1); inventory entry | Governance office |
| **G1 — Data** | Before data acquisition/extract | Basis-per-purpose check (M2); minimisation review + classification (M2/M4); DPIA trigger check | Data owner + DPO consult |
| **G2 — Pre-deployment** | Before production exposure | Bias audit vs tolerances (M3); DPIA complete (M4); model card + notices + audit trail verified (M5); third-party assessment if applicable | Accountable owner |
| **G3 — In-production** | Quarterly / on triggers | Fairness + drift re-measurement vs tolerances; override analytics; register review | Governance office |
| **G4 — Change** | Any model/threshold/data/purpose change | Change-scaled reassessment (a threshold change ≠ a purpose change); card + notice propagation (M5's rot lesson) | Owner per change class |
| **G5 — Retirement** | Decommissioning | Data disposition per retention schedule; audit-trail preservation for the appeal window; dependent-system notice | Owner + records authority |

**Proportionality is designed in:** high-risk systems traverse all gates with full evidence; limited-risk systems get a fast path (G0 + lightweight G2); minimal-risk systems get G0 only. The fast path is what buys the governance office the political capital to hold the slow path — M1's "right path = fast path" principle, operationalised.

**The RACI that makes gates real (excerpt):** Accountable = the named business owner (never the committee, never the vendor); Responsible = the delivery team producing evidence; Consulted = DPO, legal, security; Informed = the governance office (who also audits gate compliance). One RACI rule prevents most failures: **the person who builds cannot be the person who signs G2.**

### 3. Third-party AI: the assessment discipline

The structural problem: obligations follow the deployer (M1), but visibility stops at the vendor's API. The assessment compensates with four dimensions of structured interrogation (the Lab 6 checklist):

**A. Data dimension** — Where is our data stored and processed (regions, sub-processors)? Is it used to train or improve the vendor's models — opt-out or absent by contract? Retention of prompts/payloads/logs? Cross-border access paths incl. support staff (M2's transfer lesson)? Deletion on exit — demonstrated how?

**B. Model dimension** — Provenance: what is this model, what was it trained on (at whatever grain the vendor will state), and what documentation exists (vendor model card — its absence is itself a finding)? Evaluation: will the vendor share evaluation results relevant to *our* population and language (Arabic performance is a first-class question in the Kingdom, not a footnote)? Version stability: are we notified of model updates, can we pin versions, can we regression-test before switchover?

**C. Operational dimension** — SLAs and their remedies; incident notification duties and timelines (aligned to our 72-hour PDPL clock — the vendor's duty must be *shorter*); audit rights (questionnaire? third-party attestation? on-site?); certifications as evidence-not-conclusion (ISO 27001, SOC 2, national frameworks; and NCA cloud requirements where government data is involved); business continuity and our exit path (data export format, transition support, contractual notice periods).

**D. Contractual dimension** — the clause set that converts answers into obligations (Worked Example 2). The one-line rule participants must retain: **an assurance that is not in the contract is a conversation, not a control.**

**Scoring and decision:** each dimension scores red/amber/green with findings; the output is not a grade but a *decision recommendation* — approve / approve-with-conditions (named conditions, owners, dates) / reject — plus register entries for every accepted amber. Conditions without owners and dates are ambers laundered into greens.

### 4. Monitoring, incidents, and the closed loop

The register decays without inputs. Wire four feedback channels:

1. **Scheduled re-measurement:** the G3 quarterly fairness/drift checks write results *into* the register (tolerance breach → risk status escalates automatically, not when someone remembers).
2. **Operational signals:** override-rate shifts (M5's trail analytics), appeal volumes and outcomes by group, complaint themes — leading indicators that metrics lag.
3. **Change notifications:** internal (G4) and vendor (contractual duty) — every change event triggers a scaled reassessment decision, recorded even when the decision is "no reassessment needed, rationale: …".
4. **Incidents:** an AI-incident procedure that extends (not duplicates) the corporate incident process, with AI-specific triage questions — is this a data breach (PDPL clock starts), a fairness event (affected-group analysis), a safety event (suspend?), or a transparency event (were people told wrongly)? Every incident closes with a register update and, where warranted, a gate-hardening change: the register is how organisations learn.

### 5. The compliance workflow as evidence system

Reframe for the capstone: every gate produces dated, signed, versioned artefacts filed in a predictable structure — which means **the governance file assembles itself as a by-product of delivery**. When the M2 regulator drill arrives in real life, the answer is a file path, not a war room. Participants have lived this all course: their Ameen file *is* the gate-evidence trail of a well-governed system, produced in the order a real delivery would produce it. Say this explicitly on Day 3 — it converts the course from six techniques into one system.

### 6. Common mistakes & operational considerations

1. **Register as annual ritual:** built for the audit, untouched between audits — detectable by comparing register review dates against model deployment dates.
2. **Likelihood×impact theatre:** precise-looking 4.2×3.8 scores hiding unexamined assumptions; the scoring conversation matters more than the arithmetic — record the *rationale*, not just the number.
3. **Gates without teeth:** a "mandatory" review the deployment platform does not enforce becomes optional under deadline; enforce technically where possible (no registry entry → no deploy).
4. **Certification outsourcing:** "the vendor is ISO-certified" ending the analysis — certifications scope *their* management system, not your use case, your data categories, or your Arabic-language performance.
5. **The silent-update blind spot:** vendor model changes shifting behaviour with no internal trigger — the contract clause (notification + version pinning) plus a lightweight behavioural regression suite is the only defence.
6. **Shadow AI:** teams adopting AI SaaS features outside any gate — countered by an easy G0 intake (make registration trivial), periodic discovery scans, and procurement-channel controls, in that order of effectiveness.
7. **Conditions that evaporate:** approve-with-conditions where nobody tracks the conditions — the register owns them, with dates, like any other risk.

**Operational considerations.** Right-size to the organisation: a 40-person agency does not need a bank's three-lines-of-defence apparatus — it needs G0/G2 gates, one register, one accountable owner per system, and a quarterly review that actually happens. Maturity grows by adding rigor to a working loop, never by designing the full apparatus first. Budget register review into someone's actual job description; unowned recurring work is how every register dies.

## Case Study — Ministry Procurement of a Third-Party LLM Service

**Scenario.** A service ministry plans "Mujeeb", a citizen-facing assistant answering service questions and drafting case responses, built on a global provider's LLM via a regional integrator. Procurement has a preferred bidder; the governance office is brought in two weeks before award — late, as usual. Review finds: prompt logs retained abroad for 24 months; the integrator's demo used real citizen inquiries harvested from a previous project; Arabic evaluation consists of a marketing benchmark on formal MSA while the inquiry base is heavily dialectal; and the provider reserves the right to update the underlying model without notice.

**Business context.** The minister's office has announced the assistant publicly with a launch quarter. The integrator is politically well-connected. The governance office's leverage is real but spendable: it can delay award, attach conditions, or escalate — each at a cost.

**Technical challenge.** Distinguish the fixable from the structural: retention and residency are contract-fixable; the harvested-demo-data issue is an incident requiring its own response (the integrator processed personal data without basis — M2 obligations); dialect performance is an evaluation gap fixable with a proper test set; silent updates are fixable only if the provider offers version pinning — a capability question, not a negotiation question.

**Constraints.** The launch date is political; the provider's standard terms resist edits below a spend threshold; the integrator relationship will outlive this procurement; citizens will type sensitive data into the box regardless of any disclaimer (the Bayan CX insight at national scale).

**Solution approach (facilitate).** (1) Separate the incident from the procurement: the harvested-data issue gets an immediate M2-track response (basis analysis, notification assessment) regardless of award. (2) Convert findings into award conditions with the C1–C10 checklist — conditions in the contract, not the meeting minutes. (3) Commission the dialect evaluation on a ministry-built 1,000-inquiry test set as a pre-launch gate with published tolerances. (4) Scope the launch to match residual risk: agent-reviewed drafting first, direct citizen answers only after G3 evidence accumulates — the phased launch protects the announced date *and* the citizens. (5) Register everything, including the political-pressure risk itself (owner: governance office head; control: this documented decision trail).

**Discussion questions.**
1. Governance arrived two weeks before award. What structural change makes it arrive at G0 next time, and who has to want that change?
2. The phased-launch compromise trades scope for date. Construct the one-slide version the minister's office actually reads.
3. The integrator harvested real inquiries for a demo. Walk the M2 analysis: basis, breach question, notification clock, and what it means for their fitness as a processor.
4. Write the tolerance sentence for the dialect evaluation gate ("launch proceeds if …") — precise enough that nobody can argue about it in launch week.

## Assessment Criteria Notes for Instructors

- Grade *from the file first*, the presentation second — the course's thesis is that governance lives in evidence, not eloquence. A polished presentation over a thin file scores below a plain presentation over a rigorous file.
- The score-reuse request is the discriminator between bands: strong pairs recognise it re-opens classification (new purpose, new population, new consequence → G0), cite their own out-of-scope card section, and describe the lawful path; weak pairs treat it as a feature request.
- Anti-pattern flags that cap a criterion at 70%: residual risks all "Low"; conditions without owners/dates; invented answers in panel Q&A (test once per panel: ask one question whose answer is *not* in any artefact — the correct response names the gap and the process to close it, not a guess).
- Panels: 3–4 pairs per panel; peers score with the short rubric card; instructor score prevails on disputes. Keep the committee question deck visibly in play — participants met it in Lab 5, and recognising returning questions rewards preparation.

# Assessment Package

## Quiz Bank (20 questions — use 10 per cohort, 15 min, closed book)

**Q1.** Name the seven SDAIA AI-ethics principles. → Fairness; Privacy & Security; Humanity; Social & Environmental Benefits; Reliability & Safety; Transparency & Explainability; Accountability & Responsibility.
**Q2.** A system "only recommends" but 92% of recommendations are accepted unchanged. Governance implication? → De-facto autonomy: classify and control it as a deciding system; oversight must be designed and measured.
**Q3.** The single question that most efficiently tests risk tier? → What happens to a specific person when the system is wrong in each direction?
**Q4.** Four legal bases PDPL recognises besides consent? → Actual interest of the data subject; processing under another law / implementing an agreement; public-entity security/judicial purposes; legitimate interest (non-sensitive data, post-amendment).
**Q5.** Five data-subject rights under PDPL? → To be informed; access; obtain a copy; correction; destruction (plus withdrawal of consent where consent is the basis).
**Q6.** Breach notification to SDAIA — the timeline under the Implementing Regulations? → 72 hours.
**Q7.** The four national data-classification levels? → Top Secret, Secret, Restricted, Public.
**Q8.** Why does a training extract inherit its source's classification? → Until anonymisation is *demonstrated* by analysis, identifiability — and therefore impact of disclosure — is presumed unchanged.
**Q9.** Selection rates 0.48 vs 0.62 — compute DIR and interpret. → 0.774; below the 0.8 four-fifths tripwire → investigate (tripwire, not verdict).
**Q10.** Equal opportunity vs demographic parity in one sentence each. → EO: equal TPR among the truly eligible across groups; DP: equal selection rates across groups regardless of eligibility.
**Q11.** Why can identical global accuracy hide serious unfairness? → Accuracy aggregates over error types and groups; opposite error patterns (high FN for one group, high FP for another) can produce equal accuracy — Worked Example 1's exact construction.
**Q12.** Removing the protected attribute from features: sufficient, and what does it break? → Not sufficient (proxies remain); it also breaks disparity *measurement* — keep protected attributes for audit, exclude from features.
**Q13.** Pseudonymised data under PDPL — in scope or out, and why? → In scope: re-identification remains reasonably possible; only demonstrated anonymisation exits obligations.
**Q14.** Define k-anonymity and name the attack l-diversity answers. → Each record indistinguishable from ≥ k−1 others on the quasi-identifiers; the homogeneity attack (all k share the sensitive value).
**Q15.** Generalise first, suppress last — why? → Generalisation preserves records at lower resolution; suppression deletes information entirely and biases the sample against rare (often fairness-relevant) groups.
**Q16.** Which model-card section prevents scope creep, and what makes it work? → Out-of-scope uses; specificity plus binding status in registry/procurement.
**Q17.** The one-question validation of an audit-trail design? → Can this specific decision be reconstructed as it was made, with what was known then?
**Q18.** Why does a designed-but-unimplemented control not reduce residual risk? → Residual risk is a claim about operating effectiveness; a control that has never run has none — hence the designed/implemented/verified status column.
**Q19.** "The vendor is ISO 27001 certified" — what does this not establish? → Anything about your use case: data-use rights, fairness, Arabic performance, transfer paths; certification scopes the vendor's management system, not your risk.
**Q20.** An assurance the vendor gave in a meeting but not in the contract is…? → A conversation, not a control — convert it to a clause (C1–C10) or register the gap.
